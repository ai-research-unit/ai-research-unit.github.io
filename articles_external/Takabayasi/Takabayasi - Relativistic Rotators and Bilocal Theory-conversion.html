What the Paper Does

The four-author paper that turns the rotator from a model into a framework. Three results matter. An isolated rotator's Lagrangian implies, besides the expected conservation laws, the existence of a second point centre in a particular inertial frame — which is what makes the rotator equivalent in structure to Yukawa's bilocal theory. The hyperspherical rotator's Hamiltonian splits into two complex-conjugate parts, one built on $\omega^{+}$ and one on $\omega^{-}$, the two self-dual halves of the angular velocity bivector. And six relativistic Euler angles — three real and three imaginary — parametrise the tetrad, which makes the later quantisation tractable. It is the paper in this folder where the corpus's own two-sector structure appears as a kinematical fact rather than an algebraic choice.

Ideas Not Yet in the Corpus

1. The kinetic energy of a spinning body splits into self-dual and anti-self-dual halves — the corpus's two sectors are a fact about angular velocity

The idea. $\omega_{\mu\nu}$ is a bivector, hence $\omega=\omega^{+}+\omega^{-}$ with $\omega^{\pm}=\frac12(\omega\pm{}^{*}\omega)$ self-dual and anti-self-dual. The kinetic energy is quadratic in $\omega$, so it splits into a $(+)$ term and a $(-)$ term, and the Hamiltonian into two complex-conjugate parts. The split is forced by the kinematics of rotation, not chosen for convenience.

What it would add. The physical reason for the corpus's central algebraic structure. The Two Sectors: The Hermitian Split and the Four Bridges (articles_physics/the-two-sectors-the-hermitian-split-and-the-four-bridges.md) presents the Hermitian split as a choice among conventions, justified by what it makes easy. Bohm, Hillion, Takabayasi and Vigier show that for a spinning body the split is not a choice: the energy of rotation is diagonal in it, because energy is quadratic and the bivector space is the direct sum of the two self-dual halves. That turns the corpus's most-used convention into a derived fact, and explains why the sectors are complex conjugates: the conjugation is time reversal, and the two halves of a rotation are its two senses.

Where it lands. The two-sectors article's §Why This Split, as the kinematical derivation, and The Two Casimirs of the Lorentz Algebra (articles_physics/the-two-casimirs-of-the-lorentz-algebra.md), where the two half-spaces are the two Casimirs' homes.

What stands in the way. The rotator's $\omega$ is a body's angular velocity, while the corpus's split applies to the field's bivector part, which is not a rotation rate; the derivation transports as an analogy with a proof. It ranks first because it is the strongest argument available for the corpus's choice of basis.

2. An isolated rotator has a second centre — the bilocal structure is implied, not added

The idea. Momentum and angular momentum conservation for a rotator imply that in one particular inertial frame a second distinguished point exists, distinct from $X^{\mu}$, and the system is structurally a bilocal object. Extension is not put in by hand; a spinning body is bilocal.

What it would add. A derivation for a premise the corpus assumes. The corpus's structured-object articles take the extended data as given: Rigid-Body Dynamics and the Biquaternion Rotor (articles_physics/rigid-body-dynamics-and-the-biquaternion-rotor.md) has a body with an orientation and The Spectrum of a Lorentz Rotor (articles_physics/the-spectrum-of-a-lorentz-rotor-what-two-eigenvalues-leave-undetermined.md) has the rotor's two eigenvalues. Neither asks where a second point would come from. Here they come out of one point plus a triad. That is a real reduction: six rotational degrees of freedom plus one position are equivalent, for the conserved quantities, to two positions with a constraint. It also says something the corpus does not: that the second centre is frame-dependent ("a particular inertial frame"), so bilocality is not an invariant structure but a choice of frame in which the rotator looks bilocal.

Where it lands. The bilocal article's introduction, as the constructive origin, and Two Readings of One Transformation: A Comparison (articles_physics/two-readings-of-one-transformation-a-comparison.md), where the second point's frame dependence is the point at issue.

What stands in the way. The paper asserts the second centre and identifies its frame without giving the map from $(X,\text{triad})$ to $(x_1,x_2)$; the algebra would have to be reconstructed.
