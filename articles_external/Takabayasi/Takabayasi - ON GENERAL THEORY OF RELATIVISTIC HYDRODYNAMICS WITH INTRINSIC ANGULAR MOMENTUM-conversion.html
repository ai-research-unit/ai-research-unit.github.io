The Argument of the First Two Sections

§1, Introduction. Relativistic hydrodynamics has been treated in Møller's book but is confined there to simple and special cases. The concept of hydrodynamics ought not to be restricted to such cases, and the author argues that the general programme should be to construct the theory of continuous matter from a more general starting point than the usual fluid. Eckart is credited with an early consistent relativistic hydrodynamics including angular momentum and heat flow, but his definition of the heat flow is said to be insufficient, and in many of the cases encountered the accepted concept does not cover what is needed.

§2, Conservation Equations. Any matter field carries distributions of dynamical quantities — mass, momentum, energy, stress, angular momentum — and these are related to the energy-momentum tensor. The energy-momentum conservation law is written with an external force density on the right, and the intrinsic angular momentum (spin) density tensor is introduced alongside. A place is reserved, in the stress, for the contribution due to the convection and the work as distinct from the intrinsic part — which is the distinction the rest of the paper is built on.

What the Legible Material Adds

1. The programme is

"any matter field", not "a fluid"

The idea. The opening complaint is that hydrodynamics has been treated only in simple and special cases and that this is too narrow. The subject is the conservation structure that every matter field possesses, with the fluid as one instance.

What it would add. The same widening the corpus performs in the other direction. Spin Hydrodynamics in Biquaternionic Form (articles_physics/spin-hydrodynamics-in-biquaternionic-form.md) derives the spin-fluid equations for a specific field and then generalises; here the general conservation scheme is the starting point and particular fields are to be read off it. The two orders of presentation arrive at the same place, and the difference is worth recording because the general-first order makes the assumptions visible.

Where it lands. §The Conservation Structure, as the general-first ordering.

What stands in the way. Only the programme is legible; the execution is not, so the claim that the general scheme does cover the particular fields cannot be checked against this text.

2. Eckart's heat flow is judged insufficient

The idea. Eckart's 1940 paper is the standard relativistic hydrodynamics with angular momentum and heat conduction. The judgement here is that his definition of the heat flow does not suffice — a specific defect in a specific definition, not a rejection of the framework.

What it would add. A named disagreement about a definition the corpus uses without comment. Causal Dissipative Hydrodynamics in Biquaternionic Form (articles_physics/causal-dissipative-hydrodynamics-in-biquaternionic-form.md) takes the Eckart-type decomposition as its starting point; a contemporary's objection that the heat-flow definition is inadequate is directly relevant to whether that decomposition is well posed.

Where it lands. §The Decomposition, as the objection to Eckart's choice.

What stands in the way. The reason for the insufficiency is in the illegible part of §1. The verdict survives; the argument for it does not.

3. Convective and intrinsic parts are separated in the stress

The idea. The stress is split into a part due to convection and work and an intrinsic part, and the intrinsic angular momentum tensor is a separate object. The split is what allows spin to be carried independently of the mass flow.

What it would add. A statement of the decomposition as a convention with a reason rather than a definition. The corpus's spin-fluid articles write the split down; here it is motivated by the demand that the conservation law hold for arbitrary matter.

Where it lands. §The Decomposition of Spin Hydrodynamics in Biquaternionic Form, as the motivation.

What stands in the way. The decomposition is the standard one; the paper's novelty is claimed to lie further on, in the illegible sections.
