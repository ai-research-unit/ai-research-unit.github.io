What the Paper Does

The author argues that an event horizon cannot be observed, while an apparent or trapping horizon can. The reason is that the event horizon is defined by what happens in the infinite future, and a finite observer cannot wait for that. Apparent horizons are local, so detectors in a finite region can find them. The conclusion is that event horizons are the wrong tool for defining astrophysical black holes, especially ones that grow or evaporate. The only exception is a stationary black hole, where the various horizons coincide.

Summary in the Authors' Words

The claim

"Event horizons are (generically) not physically observable. In contrast, apparent horizons (and the closely related trapping horizons) are generically physically observable — in the sense that they can be detected by observers working in finite-size regions of spacetime."

The consequence

"Consequently event horizons are inappropriate tools for defining astrophysical black holes, or indeed for defining any notion of evolving black hole, (evolving either due to accretion or Hawking radiation)."

The exception

"The only situation in which an event horizon becomes physically observable is for the very highly idealized stationary or static black holes, when the event horizon is a Killing horizon which is degenerate with the apparent and trapping horizons"

The alternative picture

"there are a number of models in which the event horizon never forms in the first place"

The trap

"there is [an event horizon] waiting in the wings, ready to engulf the entire region of interest with an unexpected event horizon"
