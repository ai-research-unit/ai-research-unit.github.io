What the Paper Does

The total gravitational action for an asymptotically flat spacetime needs a third term that cancels the divergence of the Gibbons–Hawking surface term. Mann and Marolf defined that term implicitly, through the Ricci tensor of the boundary. This paper inverts the relation for a four-dimensional bulk, so the term is written explicitly using the Einstein tensor of the three-dimensional boundary. The inversion is done with the Jordan canonical form of a mixed tensor, because diagonalising a symmetric matrix fails in Lorentzian signature. The author notes that static spherically symmetric spacetimes hit the apparent division-by-zero case, and that several counterterms coincide in the embeddable situation.

Summary in the Authors' Words

The problem

"It has been known for many years that the Einstein–Hilbert term is only part of the story when it comes to considering the gravitational action. For a bulk region $\Omega$ bounded by a surface $\partial\Omega$ the full gravitational action is"

$$\text{(Einstein–Hilbert bulk term)}+\text{(Gibbons–Hawking surface term)}-\text{(Mann–Marolf surface term)}.$$

The gap in the literature

"As presented by Mann and Marolf, their surface term is specified implicitly in terms of the Ricci tensor of the boundary."

The new result

"Herein I demonstrate that for the physically interesting case of a (3+1) dimensional bulk spacetime, the Mann–Marolf surface term can be specified explicitly in terms of the Einstein tensor of the (2+1) dimensional boundary."

Why a canonical form is needed

"this procedure fails in Lorentzian signature — one has to classify all possible canonical forms for $3\times3$ symmetric matrices under local Lorentz transformations, and their stability under eigenvalue perturbations. In Lorentzian signature it is more direct to consider the non-symmetric mixed tensor $\hat K^i{}_j$ and appeal to the Jordan canonical form argument as above."

Why four dimensions

"Up to this stage we could have worked in any number of dimensions $d\ge4$, wheras $d=4$ ... is physically the most important case. Mathematically this is also the most difficult case to deal with, as $d=4$ does not exhibit the many technical simplifications that occur for $d>4$. However it is only in this physically most relevant situation of $d=4$ that we will succeed in inverting $R(\hat K)$ to obtain $\hat K(R)$."

A warning

"Note further that there is a risk of a 'divide by zero' if one of the denominators happens to vanish. (We shall soon see that this apparently technical point actually arises in surprisingly simple situations — such as static spherically symmetric spacetimes.)"

The coincidence

"Thus the KLS, Lau-Mann, and Mann–Marolf counterterms are in this situation all identical."
