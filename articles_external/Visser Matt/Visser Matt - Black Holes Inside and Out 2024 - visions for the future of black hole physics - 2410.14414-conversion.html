What the Paper Does

This is the proceedings of the BHIO 2024 conference in Copenhagen, and it is not a single paper. Each of the 32 invited speakers and round-table moderators was asked for a short "vision" document, and the volume collects them in six parts: classical black holes, black holes beyond general relativity, black holes as laboratories, tests of the black hole paradigm, quantum black holes, and a closing part. The contributions are opinion pieces of a few pages each, with their own references. Visser's contribution is "Black Holes, Cauchy Horizons, and Mass Inflation", which argues that event and Cauchy horizons are teleological and therefore not physically observable, while long-lived quasi-local horizons are observable and suffice for Hawking radiation and for mass inflation.

Summary in the Authors' Words

What the volume is

"All 32 invited speakers and round table moderators were asked for a 'vision' document. No style constraints were imposed on these documents, with the aim of offering the authors the possibility of freely expressing their thoughts about the state of the art and its possible future evolution, and maybe adding a personal touch."

The stated reason

"Black holes are the most amazing macroscopic objects in the universe. According to Einstein's General Relativity, their exterior is extremely simple, while their interior holds the failure of the underlying theory. There are thus strong reasons to test black hole spacetimes, an endeavor made possible by gravitational-wave astronomy, infrared interferometry and radio very large baseline interferometry."

Visser's thesis

"Event horizons and Cauchy horizons are highly idealized mathematical constructions that do not fully capture the key physics of either Hawking radiation or mass inflation. Indeed, because they are teleological, both event horizons and Cauchy horizons are (in a precise technical sense) not physically observable."

The alternative

"In contrast, by inspecting the quasi-local behaviour of null geodesics, long-lived apparent horizons (or more generally long-lived quasi-local horizons) are in principle physically observable, and are 'good enough' for then pragmatically redefining a black hole, and 'good enough' for generating Hawking radiation. Furthermore it is now also clear that long lived apparent horizons (quasi-local horizons) are also 'good enough' for generating mass inflation."

The warning

"These observations suggest that one should be somewhat careful when trying to extrapolate rigorous mathematical theorems, which often embody mathematical idealizations that do not necessarily correspond to what a finite resource astronomer can actually measure, into the astrophysical realm." The same author adds that "Event (absolute) horizons are good for developing abstract mathematical theorems — but not so good for physicists and astronomers."

The technical condition

Slow evolution is expressed as

$$|\dot\kappa|\ll\kappa^2, \qquad (paper\ 1)$$

and the adiabaticity condition $|\dot\kappa|/|\kappa|\ll|\kappa|$ (paper 2) "implies that the fractional change in Hawking temperature is small — on the oscillation timescale of a typical Hawking photon."

1. Observability as the criterion

The idea. A distinction is drawn between horizons that a finite-resource observer can detect and horizons defined by the infinite future.

What it would add. articles_physics/horizons-without-a-global-structure.md already separates the causal boundary from local conditions, and it treats the separation as a limitation of the algebra. The idea. A theorem that assumes an event horizon may not describe anything an astronomer can measure.
