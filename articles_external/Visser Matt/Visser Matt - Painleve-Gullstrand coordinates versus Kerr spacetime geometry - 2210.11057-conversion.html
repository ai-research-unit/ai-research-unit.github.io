What the Paper Does

Painlevé–Gullstrand coordinates make a static black hole look like a flowing fluid. The authors ask how far this construction reaches for a rotating black hole. They separate strong from weak versions of the coordinates and list what each can and cannot do. Weak and conformal variants cannot cover Kerr or Kerr–Newman. The best available route keeps unit lapse and deforms the spatial metric in a factorised, volume-preserving way. They close by asking how far this could support a laboratory analogue.

Summary in the Authors' Words

The question

"We discuss the tension between the possible existence of Painlevé–Gullstrand coordinate systems versus the explicit geometrical features of the Kerr spacetime"

The method

"We shall carefully distinguish strong and weak Painlevé–Gullstrand coordinate systems, and conformal variants thereof, cataloguing what we know can and cannot be done"

The limitation

"while the weak Painlevé–Gullstrand and conformal Painlevé–Gullstrand coordinate systems cover a lot of territory, they are incapable of describing the astrophysically important Kerr spacetime or the physically important Kerr–Newman spacetime."

The route that works

"to get to the Kerr or Kerr–Newman spacetimes one must take a different route — keep the lapse function unity, $N=1$, but allow a controlled deformation of the 3-geometry."

The deformation

"A factorized volume-preserving deformation of 3-space, wherein $g_3=(I-u\otimes v)(I-u\otimes v)^{T}$ with $\vec u\cdot\vec v=0$, proves to be sufficient for describing the Kerr or Kerr–Newman spacetimes."

The further aim

"We finish by exploring the (limited) extent to which this construction might possibly lead to implementing an 'analogue spacetime' model suitable for laboratory simulations of the Kerr spacetime."
