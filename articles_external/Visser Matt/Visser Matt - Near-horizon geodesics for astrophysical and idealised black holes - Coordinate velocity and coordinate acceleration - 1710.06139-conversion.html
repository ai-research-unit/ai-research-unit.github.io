What the Paper Does

A geodesic has zero four-acceleration. Its coordinate acceleration need not be zero, and near a horizon the coordinate velocity can exceed $c$ while the coordinate acceleration changes sign. The authors work through Schwarzschild in several coordinate systems: curvature, isotropic, and horizon-penetrating. They show that these quantities are coordinate artefacts and that statements about repulsive gravity near a horizon come from mixing Newtonian and relativistic notions. They recommend horizon-penetrating coordinates for near-horizon analysis.

Summary in the Authors' Words

The problem

"Geodesics (by definition) have an intrinsic 4-acceleration zero. However, when expressed in terms of coordinates, the coordinate acceleration ... can very easily be non-zero, and the coordinate velocity ... can behave unexpectedly."

The danger

"The situation becomes extremely delicate in the near-horizon limit—for both astrophysical and idealised black holes—where an inappropriate choice of coordinates can quite easily lead to significant confusion."

The recommendation

"arguing that in the near-horizon limit the coordinate acceleration ... is best interpreted in terms of horizon-penetrating coordinates."

The scope of the conclusion

"While the specific computations in this article have been carried out for the Schwarzschild geometry, this is known to be a good approximation for slowly rotating astrophysical black holes ... so the overall conclusions are generic to a wide class of physically and observationally interesting black holes."

The verdict on artefacts

"The most obvious conclusion we can draw is that the coordinate velocity, and coordinate acceleration, are (quite naturally) extremely coordinate dependent, and that no general physical conclusions can be drawn from the magnitude of the coordinate velocity, ($\dot r$ can easily exceed unity), or the sign of the coordinate acceleration $\ddot r$."

The criticism

"Claims that gravity in general relativity is 'repulsive' at high speeds and/or near the horizon are at best disingenuous — they are merely misinterpretations of coordinate artefacts."

The warning

"Indiscriminately mixing general relativistic and Newtonian concepts is dangerous and misleading."
