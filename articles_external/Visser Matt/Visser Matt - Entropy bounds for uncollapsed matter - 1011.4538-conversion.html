What the Paper Does

The paper bounds the entropy of a horizonless object in a static spacetime by half its surface area. The route is short: the quasilocal Tolman mass becomes a surface flux of a generalized surface gravity, the Gibbs–Duhem relation turns entropy into a bound involving that mass over temperature, and the Unruh effect relates the surface gravity to a temperature. The result resembles the holographic bound but with a coefficient of one half rather than one quarter, and the authors explain that the difference comes from treating uncollapsed matter with the ordinary Euler relation.

Summary in the Authors' Words

The mass

"Among the different notions of quasi-local mass, the Tolman mass has the advantage that when dealing with a horizonless object (like a star/planet/monster/gravastar/black star/quasi-black hole) in a static spacetime, it can ultimately be bounded in terms of quantities measurable in the surface of the object; particularly the average surface gravity and the total area."

The geometry

"In any static spacetime where the metric can be put into the form"

$$ds^2=-e^{-2\Psi}dt^2+g_{ij}dx^i dx^j,\qquad (paper\ 1)$$

"the Einstein equations then imply"

$$m_T(\Omega)=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_\Omega\sqrt{-g_4}\,R_{00}\,d^3x,\qquad (paper\ 3)$$

"which is a purely geometrical statement."

The thermodynamic input

"To take thermodynamics into account, and calculate a bound on the entropy of these types of object, we now need to consider the Euler (Gibbs–Duhem) relation for the entropy density of the matter forming the horizonless object,"

$$s=\frac{\rho+p-\mu n}{T}.\qquad (paper\ 16)$$

The first bound

"We see that the total entropy is bounded by the Tolman mass divided by the temperature as measured at infinity."

$$S(\Omega)\le\frac{m_T(\Omega)}{T_\infty}.\qquad (paper\ 21)$$

The Unruh step

"Finally we invoke the only semiclassical notion we use throughout the calculations, to relate the surface gravity to a temperature, via the Unruh effect."

$$T(x)\ge T_{\mathrm{Unruh}}(x)=\frac{\|A(x)\|}{2\pi},\qquad (paper\ 23)$$

The result

"Hence the entropy is bounded by one half of the total area $A(\partial\Omega)$,"

$$S(\Omega)\le\frac{A(\partial\Omega)}{2}.\qquad (paper\ 26)$$

The comparison

"Specifically, it resembles the holographic bound, which corresponds to $S(\Omega)\le\frac14A(\partial\Omega)$. The fundamental reason for the coefficient mismatch between these two bounds is because we calculated the entropy of a uncollapsed distribution of matter, where the temperature has its normal interpretation as an intensive variable, and the Euler relation keeps its usual uncollapsed form."

The claim

"Overall, the importance of this entropy bound, for an uncollapsed distribution of matter, resides in its simplicity and its robustness."
