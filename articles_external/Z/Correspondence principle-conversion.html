What the Paper Does

An encyclopedia article on the correspondence principle: Bohr's coinage in 1920, Planck's earlier statement in 1906 that quantum theory should reproduce classical mechanics as $h$ becomes infinitesimal, the modern reading as agreement at large quantum numbers, and the generalised form as a continuity requirement between an old and a new theory.

Summary in the Authors' Words

The definition and the attribution

"a correspondence principle is any one of several premises or assertions about the relationship between classical and quantum mechanics. The physicist Niels Bohr coined the term in 1920[1] during the early development of quantum theory; he used it to explain how quantized classical orbitals connect to quantum radiation.[2]"

The modern reading

"Modern sources often use the term for the idea that the behavior of systems described by quantum theory reproduces classical physics in the limit of large quantum numbers: for large orbits and for large energies, quantum calculations must agree with classical calculations."

The generalised form

"A 'generalized' correspondence principle refers to the requirement for a broad set of connections between any old and new theory." That version has the widest reach and the least content: it asks only that the successor contain its predecessor in some regime, without saying which.

Planck came first

"Max Planck was the first to introduce the idea of quanta of energy, while studying black-body radiation in 1900. In 1906, he was also the first to write that quantum theory should replicate classical mechanics at some limit, particularly if the Planck constant h were taken to be infinitesimal.[4][5] With this idea, he showed that Planck's law for thermal radiation leads to the Rayleigh–Jeans law, the classical prediction (valid for large wavelength).[4][5]"

Ideas Not Yet in the Corpus

1. The principle is a constraint on a new theory, not a result of it

The idea. It does not derive classical mechanics; it forbids theories that fail to reproduce it. Its force is negative, and it applies to candidate formalisms before they are believed.

What it would add. A named test. Each corpus article that builds a quantum object and reads off a classical sector satisfies the principle without stating it; naming it turns a scattered practice into a criterion that a new construction can fail.

Where it lands. articles_physics/relativistic-mechanics-from-the-dirac-wave.md, where the classical limit is extracted.

What stands in the way. Nothing structural; the corpus would only have to say which regime plays the role of large quantum numbers.

2. Large quantum numbers and small

$h$ are different limits that agree here

The idea. Bohr's statement is about large $n$; Planck's is about $h\to0$. They coincide because $nh$ is the action, which is a fact about the oscillator rather than a theorem.

What it would add. A caution about which limit a corpus derivation takes; the classical sector as a centre of the algebra corresponds to neither statement directly.

Where it lands. articles_physics/the-quantum-classical-divide-in-the-biquaternion-framework.md.

What stands in the way. The corpus's algebra has no $n$; the large-quantum-number statement would have to become a semiclassical one.
