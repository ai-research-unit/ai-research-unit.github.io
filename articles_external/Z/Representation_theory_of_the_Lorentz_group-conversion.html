What the Paper Does

A long encyclopedia article on the representations of the Lorentz group: the group as symmetries of Minkowski spacetime, its realisations as matrices, linear transformations or unitary operators, the finite-dimensional non-unitary representations labelled by two half-integers, and the infinite-dimensional unitary ones that a relativistic quantum theory requires.

Summary in the Authors' Words

The group and the question

"The Lorentz group is a Lie group of symmetries of the spacetime of special relativity. This group can be realized as a collection of matrices, linear transformations, or unitary operators on some Hilbert space; it has a variety of representations."

Why the representations matter

"special relativity together with quantum mechanics are the two physical theories that are most thoroughly established, and the conjunction of these two theories is the study of the infinite-dimensional unitary representations of the Lorentz group." The finite-dimensional representations are not unitary, so they cannot act on a Hilbert space of states directly; they label fields, not states.

The labelling

The complexified algebra splits into two commuting $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ factors, and the finite-dimensional representations are labelled by a pair $(m,n)$ of half-integers. The Lorentz scalars are $(0,0)$, the chiral spinors $(1/2,0)$ and $(0,1/2)$, the four-vector $(1/2,1/2)$, and the field strength $(1,0)\oplus(0,1)$.

Ideas Not Yet in the Corpus

1. The pair

$(m,n)$ is the honest label, not the spin

The idea. A Lorentz representation is labelled by two numbers, and "spin" is only the diagonal combination. A field's behaviour under boosts is fixed by the pair, so two fields of the same spin can transform differently.

What it would add. A labelling the corpus's Casimir article sets up and its applications sometimes abbreviate. The $(1,0)\oplus(0,1)$ identification of the field strength is the sharpest example.

Where it lands. articles_physics/the-two-casimirs-of-the-lorentz-algebra.md, at the label.

What stands in the way. The corpus works in a real algebra, where the two $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ factors are the self-dual and anti-self-dual parts; the pair has to be read as a pair of bivector sectors, not as a pair of complex numbers.

2. The finite-dimensional representations are not unitary, and that is not a defect

The idea. Non-unitarity of the finite-dimensional representations is why they describe fields and not states. A unitary representation of a non-compact group must be infinite-dimensional.

What it would add. A reason the corpus's field and state spaces are different objects, which its polar-form article uses but does not justify.

Where it lands. articles_physics/the-finite-dimensional-representations-and-their-unitarity-defect.md.

What stands in the way. The argument is a theorem about compactness; the corpus would have to state it for its own group, which is not presented as an abstract Lie group.
