What the Paper Does

An encyclopedia article on the Euler–Lagrange equations: the second-order system whose solutions are stationary points of an action, their discovery by Euler and Lagrange in the 1750s, the analogy with Fermat's theorem that a differentiable function has zero derivative at an extremum, and their use in Lagrangian mechanics.

Summary in the Authors' Words

The definition

"the Euler–Lagrange equations[1] are a system of second-order ordinary differential equations whose solutions are stationary points of the given action functional. The equations were discovered in the 1750s by Swiss mathematician Leonhard Euler and Italian mathematician Joseph-Louis Lagrange."

The analogy that explains the form

"Because a differentiable functional is stationary at its local extrema, the Euler–Lagrange equation is useful for solving optimization problems in which, given some functional, one seeks the function minimizing or maximizing it. This is analogous to Fermat's theorem in calculus, stating that at any point where a differentiable function attains a local extremum its derivative is zero."

That analogy is the article's clearest statement of what the equation is: the derivative test, transported from functions to functionals, so that the "derivative" becomes a variational derivative and the condition is that it vanish.

The usage

"In Lagrangian mechanics" the equations are the equations of motion, obtained from the Lagrangian by differentiation with respect to the coordinates and the velocities.

Ideas Not Yet in the Corpus

1. Stationarity is a condition on a functional, and it is local

The idea. The Euler–Lagrange equation is a differential equation because the functional is an integral of a local density. If the action were not local, stationarity would not reduce to a differential equation at all.

What it would add. A statement of why every corpus Lagrangian is written as an integral of a density. The corpus's Lagrangian articles assume locality without remarking that it is what makes the variation produce a differential equation.

Where it lands. articles_physics/the-lagrangian-and-the-dirac-equation.md, at the definition of the action.

What stands in the way. Nothing; the corpus is already local and would only need to say why.

2. Second order in the fields comes from first order in the derivatives

The idea. The Lagrangian depends on the field and its first derivative; the Euler–Lagrange equation is second order. A theory whose equations are first order, like the Dirac equation, therefore needs a Lagrangian with more than one field or a first-order formalism.

What it would add. An explanation of a structural puzzle the corpus meets constantly: the Dirac equation is first order, so its Lagrangian cannot be of the naive type. The corpus resolves this with a spinor and its adjoint as independent variables; the article supplies the general reason the resolution is needed.

Where it lands. articles_physics/the-lagrangian-and-the-dirac-equation-in-momentum-space.md.

What stands in the way. The article's general form is for one field; the Dirac case needs the two-variable variant, which it does not give.
