What the Paper Does

An encyclopedia article on the spectral theorem: the conditions under which a linear operator or matrix can be diagonalised, the finite-dimensional case for normal and self-adjoint matrices, the extension to compact and to unbounded self-adjoint operators on a Hilbert space, and the projection-valued-measure form.

Summary in the Authors' Words

What the theorem says

"a spectral theorem is a result about when a linear operator or matrix can be diagonalized (that is, represented as a diagonal matrix in some basis). This is extremely useful because computations involving a diagonalizable matrix can often be reduced to much simpler computations involving the corresponding diagonal matrix of eigenvalues."

Where the difficulty lies

"The concept of diagonalization is relatively straightforward for operators on finite-dimensional vector spaces but requires some modification for operators on in[finite-dimensional spaces]." In infinite dimensions the sum over eigenvalues becomes an integral against a projection-valued measure, and the operator is recovered as $\int\lambda\,dE(\lambda)$.

The hypotheses

A matrix over $\mathbb{C}$ is unitarily diagonalisable exactly when it is normal, $AA^*=A^*A$. Self-adjoint, skew-adjoint and unitary matrices are all normal, which is why the theorem applies to the operators physics uses.

"There is also an analogous spectral theorem for bounded normal operators on Hilbert spaces."

Ideas Not Yet in the Corpus

1. Normal, not Hermitian, is the right hypothesis

The idea. Unitary diagonalisability needs $AA^*=A^*A$ and nothing more. Hermiticity is sufficient and not necessary. This is a formula with practical consequences: the theorem applies to far more operators than the physical ones.

What it would add. A sharpened hypothesis for the corpus's spectral statements, which tend to assume self-adjointness and then use only normality.

Where it lands. articles_physics/the-symplectic-form-and-the-biquaternion-norm-cone.md, at the diagonalisation step.

What stands in the way. The corpus's conjugation is an algebra involution, not a Hilbert-space adjoint, so "normal" may not be the right word; the analogue would have to be defined.

2. The infinite-dimensional form is a measure, not a list

The idea. In infinite dimensions there is no list of eigenvalues; the theorem gives a projection-valued measure and the operator is an integral. A discrete spectrum is a special case.

What it would add. A precise sense in which the corpus's continuum of modes is still a spectral decomposition, which its expansion articles assume.

Where it lands. articles_physics/the-spectral-triple-of-the-biquaternion-algebra.md.

What stands in the way. The corpus has no Hilbert space and no measure on its spectrum, so the projection-valued form would have to be constructed rather than cited.
