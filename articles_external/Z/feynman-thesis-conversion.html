What the Paper Does

Reprints Feynman's 1942 Princeton thesis, "The Principle of Least Action in Quantum Mechanics", together with Laurie Brown's editorial preface. The thesis develops a quantum formulation for systems whose classical description is a principle of least action rather than Hamiltonian equations, and applies it to the Wheeler–Feynman action-at-a-distance electrodynamics.

Summary in the Authors' Words

The problem with Hamiltonians

"no method based upon the Hamiltonian could be used for the Wheeler–Feynman theory, either classically or quantum mechanically. The principal reason" half-retarded, half-advanced interaction. "The Hamiltonian method describes and keeps track of the state of the system of particles and ﬁelds at a given time. In the new theory, there are no ﬁeld variables, and every radiative process depends on contributions from the future as well as from the past! One is forced to view the entire process from start to ﬁnish."

The programme

"Feynman's thesis project was to develop and generalize this approach so that it could be used to formulate the Wheeler–Feynman theory (a theory possessing an action, but without a Hamiltonian). If successful, he should then try to find a method to quantize the new theory."

The scope claimed

"It is to be emphasized ... that the work described here is complete in itself without regard to its application to electrodynamics ... [The] present paper is concerned with the problem of finding a quantum mechanical description applicable to systems which in their classical analogue are expressible by a principle of least action, and not necessarily by Hamiltonian equations of motion."

The limitation

"All of the analysis will apply to non-relativistic systems. The generalization to the relativistic case is not at present known."

Ideas Not Yet in the Corpus

1. The action, not the Hamiltonian, is the primary object

The idea. A quantum formulation can be built from an action functional alone, with no Hamiltonian and no state at a time. The propagator is then the sum over all histories weighted by $e^{iS/\hbar}$, a functional of the whole trajectory rather than of an instantaneous state. This is a mechanism the corpus adopts in its path-integral articles.

What it would add. The historical and logical priority of the action, which explains why the corpus's propagator can be defined without a Hamiltonian at all.

Where it lands. articles_physics/the-fermionic-path-integral-in-biquaternionic-form.md, at the definition.

What stands in the way. Nothing structural; the corpus already takes this route and only lacks the reason.

2. Time-symmetric interactions force a global, not a local, description

The idea. If the interaction has an advanced half, the state at a time is not enough to predict the future; the whole interval must be considered. The formalism is forced by the physics, not chosen for elegance.

What it would add. A precedent for the corpus's own global constructions, which it presents as computational choices rather than as consequences of the physics.

Where it lands. articles_physics/exercise-the-retarded-potentials-and-the-greens-function.md, on the boundary data.

What stands in the way. The corpus's equations are local and hyperbolic, so the global description is available but not required.

3. The non-relativistic restriction is explicit

The idea. The thesis states plainly that the relativistic generalisation is unknown at the time. A reader who takes the thesis as a relativistic result would be wrong.

What it would add. A caution about attributing relativistic scope to the path integral's original derivation.

What stands in the way. Nothing structural.
