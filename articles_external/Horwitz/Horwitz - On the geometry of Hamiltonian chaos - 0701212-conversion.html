What the Paper Does

Turns the criterion for chaos in a Hamiltonian system into a geometric one. The known result is that a Hamiltonian quadratic in momenta with a metric depending only on coordinates has unstable orbits when the associated curvature is negative. That result applies only where such a metric exists, which is not the general potential model. The paper supplies the missing metric by making it conformal, with the conformal factor chosen so that the geodesics reproduce the Hamilton equations, and then shows that the second covariant derivative of the geodesic deviation generates a dynamical curvature that gives an energy-dependent instability criterion — different from Lyapunov's.

Summary in the Authors' Words

The starting point

"The characterization of chaotic Hamiltonian systems in terms of the curvature associated with a Riemannian metric tensor in the structure of the Hamiltonian can be extended to a wide class of potential models of standard form through definition of a conformal metric."

The classical statement being extended: a Hamiltonian of the form $H=\frac{1}{2M}g^{ij}p_ip_j$ "where $g_{ij}$ is a function of the coordinates alone, has unstable orbits if the curvature associated with the metric $g_{ij}$ is negative."

What the extension buys

"The geodesic equations reproduce the Hamilton equations of the original potential model when a transition is made to an associated manifold for which the geodesics coincide with the orbits of the Hamiltonian potential model. We therefore find a direct geometrical description of the time development of a Hamiltonian potential model."

The criterion

"The second covariant derivative of the geodesic deviation in this associated manifold generates a dynamical curvature, resulting in (energy dependent) criteria for unstable behavior different from the usual Lyapunov criteria."

The example

"We discuss some examples of unstable Hamiltonian systems in two dimensions giving, as a particular illustation, detailed results for a potential obtained from a fifth order expansion of a Toda lattice Hamiltonian."

Ideas Not Yet in the Corpus

1. The Lyapunov exponent is the wrong invariant

The idea. A disagreement with a standard: the Lyapunov exponent measures separation in time and depends on the trajectory; the curvature criterion measures separation in the manifold and is local. For a large class of potential models the two disagree, and the geometric one is the correct indicator of instability.

What it would add. The corpus's article on the Lyapunov exponent and information loss takes the exponent as the measure of chaos and connects it to entropy production. This paper undercuts the premise: the exponent can vanish for a genuinely unstable system and can be positive for an integrable one. Recording the disagreement is more valuable than recording the criterion, because it warns the corpus against a quantity it currently trusts.

Where it lands. articles_physics/the-lyapunov-exponent-and-information-loss-in-the-biquaternion-framework.md.

What stands in the way. A criterion that is energy-dependent and local is hard to compare with a single exponent; the corpus would have to present two notions of instability rather than one.

2. A conformal factor is enough to geometrise any potential model

The idea. A construction: the obstacle to the geometric method was that a general potential model does not have a coordinate-dependent metric in its Hamiltonian. Multiplying the metric by a conformal factor determined from the potential removes the obstacle, so every potential model becomes a geodesic flow.

What it would add. The corpus's rotor articles use conformal rescalings to relate transformations. This applies the same freedom to a different problem: not to simplify a transformation, but to manufacture a metric where none existed. It is a demonstration that the conformal factor is the right slack variable, and it is the technical step that the earlier papers in this batch assumed.

Where it lands. articles_physics/similitudes-between-biquaternion-rotors-and-hamiltonian-flow.md.

What stands in the way. The conformal factor is again a series, so the metric is local; the geometric description holds only where the expansion converges.

3. The second derivative of geodesic deviation is the observable

The idea. A definition: the quantity to compute is not the deviation itself but its second covariant derivative, which defines a dynamical curvature. That curvature is the local surrogate for the Lyapunov exponent.

What it would add. The corpus's stability articles compute eigenvalues of a linearised flow. The second covariant derivative along a geodesic is a different object — it involves the Riemann tensor contracted with the velocity — and it is computable from the potential without integrating anything. For a corpus that prefers local algebraic quantities to integrated ones, this is a better-fitting criterion, and it is the one the companion letters apply to Kepler motion.

Where it lands. articles_physics/the-conformal-anomaly-in-biquaternionic-form.md.

What stands in the way. The criterion is defined on the associated manifold, so its value depends on the conformal factor; different choices of factor would give different numbers unless the construction fixes the factor uniquely.

The rank: the Lyapunov disagreement first, because it corrects a corpus habit; the conformal-geometrisation second, because it is the enabling construction; the dynamical curvature last, because it is the quantity.

The remaining items in one line each: the Toda lattice example, which is where the criterion is tested; the explicit connection form built from the conformal factor, which is what makes the geodesics reproduce the orbits; and the two-dimensional unstable systems, which show the criterion discriminating between regimes that the exponent cannot separate.
