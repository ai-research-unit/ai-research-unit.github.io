What the Paper Does

Constructs the classical model $S_{D\,cl}$ of the Dirac particle by dynamic disquantization, and then asks what the model is. Two interpretations are offered — dynamical and geometrical — and the second is argued to be the only consistent one. Geometrical interpretation means an inversion of the usual order: the world line is the physical object and the particle is its attribute. Making that literal requires geometrising the mass, which is done by replacing the world line with a broken line of segments of equal length measured by the world function.

Summary in the Authors' Words

The construction

The abstract gives the procedure and its properties. "Classical model $S_{D\,cl}$ of the Dirac particle $S_D$ is constructed", and for that "one uses a new dynamic method: dynamic disquantization. This relativistic purely dynamic procedure does not use principles of quantum mechanics." The result: "described by a system of ordinary differential equations, containing the quantum constant h̄ as a parameter. Dynamic", with "amic equations for SDcl are determined by the Dirac equation uniquely.", and "$S_{D\,cl}$ has ten degrees of freedom and cannot be a pointlike particle, because it has an internal structure."

So the Dirac particle is composite in the precise sense that its classical limit has internal degrees of freedom, and the quantum constant survives in the classical description as a parameter rather than disappearing.

Two interpretations, one survivor

"There are two ways of interpretation of the dynamic system $S_{D\,cl}$: (1) dynamical interpretation and (2) geometrical interpretation." The choice is not presented as a matter of taste: "We shall see that the only solution of this problem is the geometrical interpretation instead of the dynamical one."

The inversion

The geometrical reading rests on a statement about what is primary:

"According to the principles of relativity the world line (WL) is a physical object, whereas the particle (pointlike object in 3-space) is an attribute of WL. We shall use abbreviation WL (instead of world line), when we want to stress that the world line is a physical object, but not an attribute of a particle (its history)."

The obstacle is the mass: "WL is not a completely geometrical object, because it contains a non-geometric parameter: mass $m$. To make WL to be a geometrical object we are to geometrize the mass." The mass is then a length: the world line is replaced by a broken line $T_{br}=\bigcup_iT[P_iP_{i+1}]$ of straight segments "of the same length µ  q q q", with the segment length given by the world function, $[P_iP_{i+1}]=R\sqrt{2\sigma(P_i,P_{i+1})}$. A particle is then a chain of segments of one length, and its mass is that length.

Ideas Not Yet in the Corpus

1. The world line is primary, the particle is derived

The idea. A reading that reverses the usual ontology: a particle is not an object that traces a world line, it is a feature of a world line. Positions and momenta are then properties of a curve, not of a thing.

What it would add. The corpus starts from a field and recovers particles as configurations, so it has a third option — neither object-first nor curve-first. Placing the corpus's own account against Rylov's inversion would clarify what the corpus's particles are, which is a question its derivations leave implicit. The corpus's curved-spacetime article is where world lines are treated as primary objects already, so the comparison has a home.

Where it lands. articles_physics/curved-spacetime-and-the-biquaternion-framework.md.

What stands in the way. The inversion is asserted from "the principles of relativity", which is a large appeal for a small step; and the two views are empirically indistinguishable, since a particle and its world line carry the same data. Without an observable that separates them, the inversion is a preference.

2. Mass as a segment length

The idea. A formula: mass is the length $\mu$ of a link, with $[P_iP_{i+1}]=R\sqrt{2\sigma(P_i,P_{i+1})}$, and the world line is a chain of equal links. So the mass shell is a statement that every link has the same length.

What it would add. The corpus has two kinds of mass and derives both from the algebra. This gives a third reading — mass as a lattice spacing along a curve — and it is the only one of the three that is a length with a geometric construction rather than a norm in an algebra. Comparing the three would say whether the corpus's two masses are two norms or one spacing under two conventions.

Where it lands. articles_physics/antilinear-structure-and-the-two-kinds-of-mass-in-biquaternionic-form.md.

What stands in the way. The construction needs a broken line, so it presupposes discreteness that the corpus does not have, and the factor $R$ is undetermined. A length fixed up to a constant is not yet a mass.

3. Ten degrees of freedom as the definition of composite

The idea. A number: composite means ten degrees of freedom, not "made of parts". The count is what makes the question in the title answerable.

What it would add. The corpus's articles on the Dirac field as a fluid count components, and a comparison of the two counts would decide whether the corpus's field carries the same internal structure or less. That is a check with a number at the end, and the corpus has no such count of its own for the Dirac particle's classical limit.

Where it lands. articles_physics/the-spin-half-fluid-and-the-takabayasi-correspondence.md.

What stands in the way. The ten are the degrees of freedom of his constructed system, and the count depends on what one counts — positions, velocities, internal angles. Without a stated rule for counting, agreement or disagreement proves nothing.

The rank: mass as a length first, because it touches the corpus's two-mass article directly; the chain of equal links second, because it is the construction that makes the first one work; the degree count last, because counting rules differ.
