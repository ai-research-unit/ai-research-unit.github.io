What the Paper Does

Argues that the concept of a particle state was never made relativistic. The dynamics was relativised in the twentieth century, but the state remained a point in phase space, and phase space presupposes a continuous geometry, because it is built on the limit that defines momentum. In a multivariant or discrete geometry that limit does not exist, so phase space cannot be built and the conventional description is incomplete. The skeleton is offered as the relativistic concept of state: a finite set of space-time points, carrying mass, charge and momentum as its geometry.

Summary in the Authors' Words

The diagnosis

"Conventional particle dynamics is formulated in an unaccomplished form, which is adequate only in the continuous space-time geometry. The conventional differential equations of the particle motion cannot be written in the discrete space-time geometry."

The sharper form of the charge is historical: "in the twentieth century a transition from the nonrelativistic physics to the relativistic one has been produced only in dynamic equations, but not in the concept of the particle state. In the nonrelativistic physics the particle state is described as a point in the phase space."

Why phase space fails

The mechanism is a missing limit. Momentum in the conventional description is $p_k(\tau)=m\,dx_k/d\tau$ together with $u_l(\tau)=\lim$ of the corresponding difference quotient, the limit being what defines a velocity at a point. In a multivariant geometry "e limit (1.3) does not exist, and one cannot introduce the phase space, founded on a use of the limit (1.3)."

So "Existence of primordially indeterministic particles (elementary particles) is possible only if the space-time ge- ometry is multivariant. One cannot construct phase space, because the limit (1.3), determining the particle momentum, does not exist in a multivariant space-time geometry. We are forced to describe the particle state without limits of the type (1.3)."

What replaces it

The skeleton: "The relativistic concept of a particle state is realized by means of a skeleton of a particle. The skeleton consists of several space-time points." Its size is the particle's complexity — "In the simplest case of a pointlike particle its skeleton contains two points" — and everything is carried there: "the skeleton describes all characteristics of the particle, including its mass, charge, momentum and other characteristics, if they take place, (spin, flower, etc. )", with the last word printed as flower in the PDF. The result is called monistic: "all fundamental physical phenomena (including electromagnetic and gravitational interactions) are described in terms of points of the event space and world functions between them."

And the equations are algebraic rather than differential: "Dynamic equations are algebraic equations, formulated in a coordinateless form. These equations are simpler and more universal, than equations, used" conventional description.

Ideas Not Yet in the Corpus

1. The state was never relativised

The idea. A claim about the history and structure of the theory: relativistic dynamics plus a nonrelativistic concept of state is an inconsistent pair, and the inconsistency is not repaired by making the equation covariant.

What it would add. The corpus's fundamental-elements article sorts what is fundamental from what is derived, and its state is an element of $\mathbb{B}$ at a point with a frame attached. Rylov's charge is that "at a point" is the nonrelativistic residue. Whether the corpus's pointwise state is relativistic in his sense is a question the corpus can answer, and the answer would be informative either way — it would either identify a genuine gap or show that a $\mathbb{B}$-element already carries the extended information.

Where it lands. articles_physics/fundamental-and-derived-elements-in-the-biquaternion-framework.md.

What stands in the way. The argument assumes that a state must be described by a finite configuration rather than by a field, which is a choice of ontology. A field-theoretic state — a value of the field everywhere — is relativistic without a skeleton, and the paper does not address that option.

2. Momentum as a limit that may not exist

The idea. A mathematical observation: momentum is defined by a derivative, a derivative is a limit, and in a geometry without infinitesimal separations the limit is not defined. So the quantity is undefined rather than approximate.

What it would add. The corpus's momentum is an algebraic object — a component of a four-vector — and never a limit, so it is immune to this objection. That immunity is worth stating, because it is a real advantage of the algebraic route and the corpus has not claimed it. It also raises the converse question: if momentum is algebraic, must the geometry be continuous, or does the algebra supply its own discreteness?

Where it lands. articles_physics/antilinear-structure-and-the-two-kinds-of-mass-in-biquaternionic-form.md.

What stands in the way. The claim is about a specific definition of momentum, and a discrete geometry can define a difference quotient without a limit. The paper does not show that no alternative definition is available, only that this one fails.

3. Skeleton size as particle structure

The idea. The number of points in the skeleton is the particle's complexity, and the simplest particle has two points — so "elementary" becomes a size in a discrete structure rather than a claim about compositeness.

What it would add. The corpus's generations article looks for a reason for three families in the algebra. A skeleton count is a completely different kind of reason, and it is the kind that could in principle give a number. If a three-point skeleton were the first that fails to admit a chain, the corpus's three would have a competitor explanation.

Where it lands. articles_physics/the-number-of-generations-and-the-biquaternion-algebra.md.

What stands in the way. No skeleton is counted for any real particle and no chain is solved, so the identification of "two points" with "pointlike" is a definition. Nothing in the paper produces a number that could be compared with three.

The rank: the unrelativised state first, because it is a structural claim about the corpus's own starting point; momentum as a limit second, because it identifies a real advantage of the algebraic route; skeleton size last, because it produces no number.
