What the Paper Does

Picks one space-time model out of a class by a single requirement — that the world tubes of all free particles, classical and quantal, be characteristic structures of the geometry — and exhibits the world function that does it. The answer is the Minkowski world function plus a constant: $\sigma=\sigma_{E}+\sigma_{0}$ with $\sigma_{0}$ proportional to $\hbar$. From the requirement and one inequality he extracts numerical bounds on the new constant and on the radius of the tube. This is the paper in the Rylov set that prints numbers.

Summary in the Authors' Words

The requirement that selects the model

The argument starts from what a space-time model is for: "First, some of characteristic geometric structures of the pseudo-Euclidean space (straight lines) occur to be real physical objects. For instance, world lines of free macroscopic particles are timelike straights, i.e., characteristic geometrical structures of the pseudo-Euclidean space." Then the failure: "But world lines of free microparticles (electrons, positrons, etc. ) are stochastic. They are not characteristic structures of the pseudo-Euclidean space."

Hence the question he sets himself: "Is it possible such an isotropic homogeneous model of the space-time, that its characteristic geometric structures (analog of timelike straights) would describe the motion of both macro- and microparticles?"

Ideas Not Yet in the Corpus

1. Quantum effects as a constant added to the world function

The idea. A mechanism with the smallest possible form: no new field, no extra dimension, one constant added to the distance between two points. The metric is unchanged, so all classical predictions survive, and the only difference is that the distance is never exactly the Minkowski one.

What it would add. The corpus has no account of why quantum effects exist at all, only a formalism that contains them. A single constant in the geometry is the most economical such account on offer, and it is testable in the corpus's own terms because it leaves the metric — and thus everything the corpus computes from the metric — untouched. Reading the corpus's Born weight as the shadow of an additive constant in the distance would give the weight an origin; the corpus's weight article does not have one.

Where it lands. articles_physics/states-observables-and-the-born-weight.md.

What stands in the way. An additive constant in $\sigma$ changes the distance for every pair of points by the same amount, which cannot reproduce interference, since interference depends on relative phases accumulated along paths. So the constant can at most set a scale, not produce the phenomenon it is supposed to explain, and the paper's own appeal to "random world tubes" does the real work.

2. The bound on the elementary length

The idea. A number derived from a self-consistency inequality: $\sqrt{\sigma_{0}}<10^{-10.5}\ \mathrm{cm}$, about $3\times10^{-13}$ m, the same maximal tube radius for all massive particles.

What it would add. The corpus's data-boundary article governs which articles may print a measured strength and is silent on bounds that follow from an internal consistency condition rather than from measurement. This is exactly such a bound, and it is the kind of object the boundary needs a rule for: a number that is not a fit, not a prediction, but an exclusion. Making room for that third category would sharpen the rule.

Where it lands. articles_physics/the-data-boundary-which-articles-may-print-a-measured-strength.md.

What stands in the way. The bound is $3\times10^{-13}$ m, at the electron Compton wavelength, which is where the non-relativistic approximation used to derive it stops being valid. So the number is at the edge of its own derivation, and the paper does not discuss whether the inequality survives relativistically.

3. Selecting a geometry by what it makes primitive

The idea. A criterion: among homogeneous isotropic models, choose the one whose characteristic structures are the world tubes of both macro- and microparticles. The model is then not fitted to data but fitted to a requirement about what counts as a straight line.

What it would add. The corpus's parameter-count article judges a framework by what it leaves open. This judges by what it makes primitive, which is a different and arguably prior question: a framework that leaves four parameters open is better than one that leaves three if its primitives are the right ones. Stating the criterion would let the corpus defend its own choice of primitives rather than only counting what remains.

Where it lands. articles_physics/the-parameter-count-as-the-measure-of-the-frameworks-boundary.md.

What stands in the way. The requirement is satisfied only by construction — he exhibits one model that meets it and does not show it is unique. A criterion met by an example is a demonstration, not a selection principle.

The rank: the additive constant first, because it is the minimal hypothesis on offer; the bound second, because it is a usable number; the selection criterion last, because it is met by example only.

The remaining items in one line each: the tubular model as a generalisation of the straight line, which is the geometric object the whole construction is built on; the note that $\sigma$ and the classical $\sigma_{E}$ differ only inside the tube, so all classical tests are passed by construction; and the admission that mean values involving momentum cannot be computed in the pure ensemble, which is where the model stops short of full quantum mechanics.
