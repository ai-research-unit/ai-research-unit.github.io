What the Paper Does

Derives the Wigner angle of two arbitrary boosts with nothing but linear algebra, on the observation that a boost is a symmetric matrix. Symmetry is not a technicality here; it is the reason the product of two boosts is not a boost and the reason the rotation can be read off. The result is the closed formula for $\tan\theta_{W}$ in terms of the two rapidities, and the paper ends with an application to aberration, where the Wigner angle appears as the difference between two aberration angles that a naive composition would confuse.

Summary in the Authors' Words

Why the subject has a reputation

"Because of its apparent complexity, the discussion of Wigner rotation is usually reduced to the study of Thomas precession, which is too specific a case to allow a deep understanding of boost composition."

And the diagnosis of the literature: "Sometimes the papers are aimed at the understanding of certain properties of the Lorentz group, instead of looking for a straightforward way to get the Wigner rotation, leaving in the reader the impression that this topic is complicated". The remedy: "The mathematical tool that we will use is simple linear algebra. After all, boosts are linear transformations. However, the key point is that boosts are symmetric linear transformations."

The eigenvector argument

"One could give an answer to the question by starting from the fact that boosts are represented by symmetric matrices." Why they must be: "boosts leave four independent directions in spacetime invariant: namely, i) they do not modify the light-cones; on the light-cone there are two independent directions, belonging to light-rays travelling back and forth along the boost direction, that remain invariant (see Appendix A); ii) in addition, the spacelike directions that are perpendicular to the boost direction are also left unchanged (a further two independent directions). Then, boosts have four independent real eigen(four)-vectors, and their representative matrices must be symmetric".

The conclusion: "Since the product of matrices representing boosts is non-symmetric (unless both boosts are parallel), then one can answer that the composition of two boosts is not, in general, equivalent to a single boost."

The angle

The product is written $B^{(y)}B^{(x)}=RB_{f}$, and "0 0 . 0 1 (6) The angle θW can be obtained by demanding the symmetry of the matrix Bf : − γ2 sin θW = γ1 sin θW + γ2 γ1 β2 β1 cos θW , (7) i.", which gives

$$\tan\theta_{W}=-\frac{\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}\beta_{1}\beta_{2}}{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}},\qquad \cos\theta_{W}=\frac{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}{\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}+1}.$$

The factors are the two boosts' parameters and nothing else — no angle between the directions appears, because for perpendicular boosts the directions are fixed by construction.

Ideas Not Yet in the Corpus

1. Symmetry as the whole proof

The idea. A method: the failure of boosts to close under composition is proved by counting eigenvectors, since every boost has four real invariant directions and a product of two non-parallel boosts does not. The rotation then follows from demanding that the residual factor be symmetric.

What it would add. The corpus's boost-composition article proves the same thing with the commutator table, which is a group-theoretic route: the bracket of two boost generators is a rotation generator. This route is matrix-theoretic and needs no Lie algebra. Having both would let the corpus say which structure is doing the work — the bracket, which is algebraic, or the symmetry, which is a property of one particular matrix representation — and the article's claim that the rotation "is a single term of a product" would be visibly representation-dependent.

Where it lands. articles_physics/composition-of-two-boosts-the-commutator-table-and-non-additivity.md.

What stands in the way. The eigenvector count uses the fact that a boost's four invariant directions are real, which is true in the Lorentz signature and not a general statement about symmetric matrices. So the proof is less general than it looks, and importing it as an alternative route would need that caveat.

2. The Wigner angle as a difference of two aberration angles

The idea. An operational definition: the Wigner angle is what is left when the aberration produced by a sequence of boosts is compared with the aberration produced by the single boost of the composed velocity. Both are measurable, and their difference is the rotation.

What it would add. The corpus's aberration article treats the boost as a Möbius flow on the sky, which gives aberration as a conformal map. This gives a second observable — the residual rotation — and identifies it with a difference of two maps the corpus already computes. It would let the corpus's aberration machinery produce the Thomas–Wigner angle as a by-product rather than as a separate calculation.

Where it lands. articles_physics/aberration-and-the-sky-the-boost-as-a-mobius-flow.md.

What stands in the way. The two aberration angles are defined with respect to different frames' axes, so their difference is only a rotation if the axes are compared in a fixed way. The paper fixes the convention implicitly by working with perpendicular boosts, and the general case would need the convention stated.

3. Four invariant directions as a characterisation

The idea. A characterisation: a Lorentz transformation is a boost exactly when it has four independent real eigendirections — two null, two spacelike — and this is what distinguishes boosts from rotations and from mixed transformations.

What it would add. The corpus's Cartan decomposition splits a general transformation into a boost and a rotation and identifies the factors by their action. An eigenvalue criterion would identify the boost factor without decomposing first, which is a cleaner statement of what "the boost part" means and gives the corpus's decomposition a uniqueness test it does not currently state.

Where it lands. articles_physics/the-cartan-decomposition-every-lorentz-transformation-as-a-boost-and-a-rotation.md.

What stands in the way. The criterion holds for boosts and fails for products, so it characterises the set rather than providing an algorithm; the paper uses it to prove a negative, not to compute a decomposition.

The rank: symmetry-as-proof first, because it is a rival method; the aberration difference second, because it is an observable; the eigenvector criterion last, because it is a test rather than a construction.

The remaining items in one line each: the remark that the three inertial systems involved must be distinguished before any physical interpretation is attempted, which is a warning the corpus's frame articles also make; the observation that the composed boost is not the boost of the composed velocity plus a rotation in the naive order, since the rotation sits on the left; and the choice of perpendicular boosts for the worked example, which is what makes the formula collapse to two parameters.
