What the Paper Does

Proves Macfarlane's closed formula for the Thomas–Wigner angle with Clifford-algebra methods, in a presentation aimed at students who know special relativity and no Clifford algebra. The rotation is defined as a closed loop of three boosts — $u$ to $v$ to $w$ and back to $u$ — and the paper shows that this loop is a rotation in the spacelike plane orthogonal to $u$. The proof goes through reflections, since each boost is a product of two reflections and the parity of the reflection count decides whether a product is a rotation or a boost.

Summary in the Authors' Words

The aim

"We derive Macfarlane's formula for the Thomas-Wigner angle of rotation using Clifford-algebra methods. The presentation is elementary and pedagogical, suitable for students with some basic knowledge of special relativity; no prior knowledge of Clifford algebras is required."

More precisely: "The aim of these notes is to provide a self-sufficient and relatively concise derivation of the angle, as well as the direction of the axis, of the Thomas-Wigner rotation." The lineage is declared: the presentation "essentially coincides with Section III of [5]", with the calculations expanded for a few lectures.

The object

The paper composes a round trip rather than a single pair:

$$R:=B(u,w)B(w,v)B(v,u).$$

"Silberstein [3] (see also Thomas [4] and Wigner [6]) pointed out that $R$ is a nontrivial rotation in a spacelike hyperplane orthogonal to $u$."

Why it must be: "By definition $R$ is a Lorentz transformation that maps $u$ to $u$, and is proper, and is thus a rotation in the hyperplane orthogonal to $u$." And the localisation: "It follows that the interesting action takes place in the three dimensional space Span{u, v, w}", because $R$ is the identity on the codimension-three orthogonal complement.

The formula

With three unit timelike vectors and $\gamma_{uv}=-\eta(u,v)$, $\gamma_{uw}=-\eta(u,w)$, $\gamma_{vw}=-\eta(v,w)$, the angle satisfies

$$\cos\psi=\frac{(1+\gamma_{uv}+\gamma_{uw}+\gamma_{vw})^{2}}{(1+\gamma_{uv})(1+\gamma_{uw})(1+\gamma_{vw})}-1 .$$

The formula is symmetric in the three vectors, which is the point: the loop's rotation depends on the three pairwise $\gamma$'s and on nothing else.

The method

The reflection: "Given a vector u such that η(u, u) 6= 0 (thus, u 6=" "consider the linear map Su from V to V defined as Su (v) = v − 2 η(u, v) u."

$$S_u(v)=v-2\frac{\eta(u,v)}{\eta(u,u)}u .$$

The paper notes that $S_u$ "is a Lorentz transformation which is an orthogonal reflection with respect to the plane orthogonal to $u$", and builds boosts from pairs of reflections.

Ideas Not Yet in the Corpus

1. A closed loop of boosts is a rotation

The idea. A definition: the Thomas–Wigner rotation is best defined not as the leftover of two boosts but as the holonomy of a closed triangle of three boosts, $R=B(u,w)B(w,v)B(v,u)$. The two-boost statement is the special case obtained by cutting the triangle.

What it would add. The corpus defines the effect as the rotation factor in a product of two boosts and derives its angle from that product. The triangle definition makes two things obvious that the product definition hides: the result is a rotation (because it fixes $u$), and it depends on three velocities symmetrically. It also matches the corpus's Cartan decomposition, where a transformation fixing a timelike vector is exactly a rotation, so the definition would slot into the existing structure.

Where it lands. articles_physics/composition-of-two-boosts-the-commutator-table-and-non-additivity.md.

What stands in the way. The three-boost loop needs three frames, and the corpus's examples use two; the generalisation costs a notational layer that the two-boost derivation does not need.

2. Macfarlane's formula is manifestly cyclic

The idea. A formula: $\cos\psi$ is a symmetric function of the three pairwise $\gamma$ factors. No angle between the boost directions appears, and the cyclic symmetry is exact rather than accidental.

What it would add. The corpus's Thomas–Wigner angle is written with the angle between the two boost directions and the two rapidities — the standard parametrisation. Macfarlane's form shows that this is a choice: the invariant content is the three $\gamma$'s, and the angle between directions is one way of packaging them. For a corpus that repeatedly rewrites one result in several algebras, this is a rewriting worth having, and it makes the two-boost formula look like a projection of a symmetric object.

Where it lands. articles_physics/exercise-the-thomas-precession.md.

What stands in the way. The formula needs a third velocity even when only two boosts are composed, so applying it to the corpus's two-boost case requires inventing a closing boost; and the expression is not simpler than the standard one for computation.

3. Reflections generate the classification

The idea. A method: every Lorentz transformation is a product of reflections, and the number of reflections modulo two decides whether the result is a rotation or a boost. Boosts are two reflections; rotations are also two reflections in spacelike planes; the difference is the type of plane.

What it would add. The corpus's Cartan decomposition states that a general transformation is a boost times a rotation and proves it by construction. The reflection count gives a proof by parity that is independent of the construction and immediately explains why the product of two boosts can be a boost times a rotation but never a pure boost in general. It is the kind of structural argument the corpus's representation articles use elsewhere.

Where it lands. articles_physics/the-cartan-decomposition-every-lorentz-transformation-as-a-boost-and-a-rotation.md.

What stands in the way. The reflection treatment lives naturally in a Clifford algebra, and the corpus's biquaternion formalism would have to encode reflections as anti-involutions, which is a nontrivial translation.

The rank: the loop definition first, because it changes what the object is; Macfarlane's formula second, because it is an alternative closed form; the reflection parity last, because it is a proof technique the corpus may not need.

The remaining items in one line each: the exact statement that the angle's sign depends on the orientation convention for the basis, which is a warning that the "direction of the axis" is convention-bound; the reduction to three dimensions, which shows the whole problem is effectively 2+1-dimensional; and the attribution of the formula to Macfarlane's 1962 paper on the restricted Lorentz group, with Urbantke's 1990 American Journal of Physics article as the intermediate source.
