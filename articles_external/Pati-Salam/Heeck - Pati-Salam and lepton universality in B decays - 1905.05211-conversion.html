What the Paper Does

It takes the $R(K^{(*)})$ and $R(D^{(*)})$ anomalies — the 2019 hints of lepton-flavour non-universality in $B$ decays — and explains them with leptoquarks that Pati–Salam produces without being asked.

"Recent hints for lepton-flavor non-universality in $B$-meson decays can be interpreted as hints for the existence of leptoquarks. We show that scalar leptoquarks unavoidably arise in grand unified theories, using the well-known Pati–Salam model as an example. These GUT-motivated leptoquarks can have a number of appealing features including automatic absence of proton decay, purely chiral couplings, and relations between the various leptoquark couplings."

"Unavoidably arise" is the claim and it is the right one. The paper's argument is not that you can add leptoquarks to Pati–Salam; it is that the scalar multiplets Pati–Salam needs for symmetry breaking contain the leptoquarks as components. You do not choose to have them. You get them.

Historical note worth recording: these anomalies have since weakened considerably. The paper is a 2019 Moriond proceeding, and the reader should treat the numerical pulls as of that date.

Summary in the Authors' Words

Where the Leptoquarks Come From

The fermions: $$ \Psi_L\sim(\mathbf4,\mathbf2,\mathbf1)\to(\mathbf3,\mathbf2)_{1/6}\oplus(\mathbf1,\mathbf2)_{-1/2}\equiv Q_L\oplus L_L, $$ $$ \Psi_R\sim(\mathbf4,\mathbf1,\mathbf2)\to(\mathbf3,\mathbf1)_{2/3}\oplus(\mathbf3,\mathbf1)_{-1/3}\oplus(\mathbf1,\mathbf1)_{-1}\oplus(\mathbf1,\mathbf1)_0\equiv u_R\oplus d_R\oplus e_R\oplus N_R . $$

The $\mathbf4$ decomposes into a colour triplet and a colour singlet. That is the whole content of "lepton number as the fourth colour": the fourth component of the $\mathbf4$ index is the lepton.

The scalars, and this is where the leptoquarks appear: $$ \Delta_L\sim(\mathbf{10},\mathbf3,\mathbf1)\to(\mathbf6,\mathbf3)_{-1/3}\oplus(\mathbf3,\mathbf3)_{1/3}\oplus(\mathbf1,\mathbf3)_{1}\equiv\Sigma_3\oplus S_3\oplus\delta_3, $$ $$ \Delta_R\sim(\mathbf{10},\mathbf1,\mathbf3)\to(\mathbf6,\mathbf1)_{-1/3}\oplus(\mathbf6,\mathbf1)_{2/3}\oplus(\mathbf6,\mathbf1)_{4/3}\oplus(\mathbf3,\mathbf1)_{1/3}\oplus(\mathbf3,\mathbf1)_{2/3}\oplus(\mathbf3,\mathbf1)_{4/3}\oplus(\mathbf1,\mathbf1)_0\oplus(\mathbf1,\mathbf1)_{1}\oplus(\mathbf1,\mathbf1)_{2} $$ $$ \equiv\Sigma_1\oplus\tilde\Sigma_1\oplus\Sigma_1'\oplus S_1\oplus\tilde S_1\oplus S_1'\oplus\delta_1\oplus\tilde\delta_1\oplus\delta_1' . $$

The $\mathbf{10}$ of $SU(4)$ is the antisymmetric two-index tensor, six-dimensional, and its decomposition under $SU(3)$ gives a $\mathbf6$ (diquark-like, colour antisymmetric) and a $\mathbf3$ (leptoquark). "which contain amongst other things all $S_j$ LQs."

That is the key statement: the $(\mathbf{10},\mathbf1,\mathbf3)$ that Pati–Salam needs to break $SU(2)_R$ and give $\nu_R$ a mass already contains leptoquarks of every type. The corpus's Pati–Salam article presents $\Delta_R$ as the $B-L$ breaking field. It should also present it as a leptoquark multiplet, because that is what makes the model testable in $B$ decays.

The Coupling Relation That Does the Work

"The SM-singlet $\delta_1$ will obtain a large vacuum expectation value that breaks $PS\to SM$, whereas the triplet $\delta_3$ will obtain a small SM-breaking VEV that generates type-II seesaw neutrino masses"

$$ M_\nu\simeq-\sqrt2\,\langle\delta_3\rangle V_L^*\,y_L\,V_L^\dagger . $$

"Here $V_L$ is the standard Cabibbo–Kobayashi–Maskawa matrix and $\langle\delta_3\rangle$ the sub-GeV triplet VEV."

"However, $y_L$ is not only the (symmetric) Yukawa coupling of $\delta_3$ to the neutrinos, but thanks to PS also the $S_3$ LQ coupling":

$$ \mathcal L=Q_L\,y_L\,L_L\,S_3+\tfrac12L_L^c\,y_L\,L_L\,\delta_3+{\rm h.c.} $$

That is the paper's central identity and it is exact in the Pati–Salam limit. The same Yukawa matrix multiplies the quark–lepton–leptoquark vertex and the lepton–lepton–triplet vertex, because both come from one $SU(4)$-invariant coupling $\Psi_L^T\,C\,\Delta_L\,\Psi_L$ with the indices contracted differently.

So a measurement of neutrino masses fixes the leptoquark couplings. That is a prediction with no free parameters beyond the overall scale — and it is the strongest kind of statement in this batch: one observable sector determines another.

$$ \Delta C_9=-\Delta C_{10}=\frac{\pi v^2}{\alpha^*\,V_{L,tb}V_{L,ts}^*}\frac{1}{M_{S_3}^2}\left(y_L^{L*}y_L^{L*}\right)_{23\,22}, $$

with the Moriond-updated fit requiring ${\rm Re}(\Delta C_9)\simeq-0.53\pm0.08$.

Why Normal Ordering Is Required

"We want to stress that it is highly non-trivial that the $M_\nu$–$R(K^{(*)})$ connection is successful. It only works because $M_\nu$ with normal mass ordering has a peculiar hierarchical structure: a dominant 23 block with potentially tiny 11 and 12 entries."

"This makes it possible for $S_3$ to explain $R(K^{(*)})$ (which resides in the 23 sector) without running into problems with muonic lepton flavor violation (which resides in the 12 sector)."

"If we had instead assumed inverted mass ordering we would have found an $S_3$ coupling matrix that is impossible to reconcile with $R(K^{(*)})$ and $\mu\to e$ processes. The same is true if we assume type-I seesaw dominance."

This is the most interesting structural point in the paper and it is worth stating carefully.

The $R(K^{(*)})$ anomaly needs a coupling in the $\mu$–$b$–$s$ sector, i.e. the (2,3) entry of $y_L$. The $\mu\to e$ conversion bound needs the (1,2) entry to be small. The neutrino mass matrix with normal ordering happens to have exactly that shape: the atmospheric mixing is $23$-dominated and the reactor angle $\theta_{13}$ is small, so the $1$–$2$ block can be tiny.

So the same matrix that fits neutrino oscillations also fits the $B$ anomaly and evades $\mu\to e$. That is a coincidence of the measured neutrino parameters, not a model choice — and it is why the paper calls the connection "a rather surprising and non-trivial feature that would be a strong hint at grand unification if confirmed."

The corpus should record that this is a conditional statement about normal ordering. If the neutrino mass ordering turns out inverted, this explanation of $R(K^{(*)})$ dies. So the two measurements are coupled: JUNO's ordering determination would decide whether this model survives.

"we note that the above suppression of muonic lepton flavor violation is not expected to hold far below the existing limits, so upcoming experiments such as Mu2e, COMET, and Mu3e should see signs of new physics if our explanation is correct."

A testable prediction at the edge of reach — the same position as the $R(D^{(*)})$ and axion predictions elsewhere in this batch.

Texture Zeros

"The existing limit requires rather small $y_{11}^L$ and $y_{12}^L$ couplings compared to $y_{23}^L$ and $y_{22}^L$, which in turn leads to a prediction of the so-far unknown CP phases in the neutrino sector. This is in direct analogy to the often-studied texture zeros in the neutrino mass matrix, which here translate into coupling zeros of the LQ $S_3$."

That is a precise and useful translation. A texture zero in $M_\nu$ (a vanishing entry in the mass matrix) becomes a vanishing leptoquark coupling, because the two are the same matrix. So the abstract "texture zero" literature acquires a physical meaning: a zero in the neutrino mass matrix means a leptoquark does not couple to that pair of generations, which is an experimentally checkable statement.

Fitting gives "a rather small Dirac CP phase."

The $R(D^{(*)})$ Problem

"$R(D^{(*)})$ requires a rather low new-physics scale around TeV and is thus subject to many more constraints. Particularly constraining is the so-far unobserved decay $B\to K\nu\nu$, which is connected to $B\to D\tau\nu$ via $SU(2)_L$ and typically requires a cancellation."

"one way out is to explain $R(D^{(*)})$ via an additional decay mode $B\to D\tau N_R$, which automatically evades the $B\to K\nu\nu$ constraint"

The $B\to K\nu\nu$ problem is a general one for any $R(D^{(*)})$ explanation and worth recording. A vector or scalar coupling that produces $b\to c\tau\nu$ also produces $b\to s\nu\nu$ through $SU(2)_L$ gauge invariance, and the latter is measured (or bounded) more tightly than the former can tolerate. So an $R(D^{(*)})$ explanation must either cancel this or produce the tau through a new channel that has no neutrino counterpart.

The paper's route: produce a right-handed neutrino alongside the tau, $B\to D\tau N_R$, which is not subject to the constraint because the final state contains the $N_R$ not a $\nu$.

"In our PS framework the decay $B\to D\tau N_R$ can in principle be induced by the $S_1$ LQ. However, the $S_1$ couplings are related to the $N_R$ mass matrix, in complete analogy to the $S_3$ couplings and $M_\nu$ above. This implies that a light $N_R$ is automatically weakly coupled to $S_1$, making it impossible to explain $R(D^{(*)})$."

That is a beautiful failure and it is the best thing in the paper. The same Pati–Salam relation that makes $S_3$ predictive also makes $S_1$ useless here: if $N_R$ is light, its Yukawa is small (seesaw), and if the Yukawa is small the decay rate is too small.

So the framework that explains $R(K^{(*)})$ cannot explain $R(D^{(*)})$, and the reason is the same structural relation. That is a real negative result and it is more informative than a success.

"It is thus necessary to extend the PS model and decouple the $S_1$ couplings from the $N_R$ mass generation."

"here we will simply assume that one of the $N_R$ is indeed light (below $100$ MeV) and has some unrelated couplings to $S_1$. Since we are staying agnostic about the neutrino mass mechanism we no longer have the $M_\nu$–$R(K^{(*)})$ connection from above, so the LQ couplings are now free parameters."

So the combined explanation costs the predictivity. You can have both anomalies, but then $M_\nu$ no longer fixes the leptoquark couplings, and the model becomes a generic leptoquark model with a Pati–Salam veneer.

That trade-off should be stated plainly in the corpus: in this framework, predictivity and the ability to explain both anomalies are mutually exclusive. You get one or the other.

Parity Equates $S_1$ and $S_3$

"Still, parity equates the $S_1$ and $S_3$ LQ couplings, $y_L=y_R$" — with the ansatz $$ y_L=y_R\simeq\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\0&y_{22}&y_{23}\\0&y_{23}&y_{33}\end{pmatrix} $$ "in order to evade constraints from muonic lepton flavor violation."

The zero in the (1,1), (1,2) and (1,3) entries is imposed, not derived — it is the texture that evades $\mu\to e$ and $\tau\to e$. So even in the less predictive version, the left–right symmetry still relates two sectors.

The left–right symmetry is doing real work here: without it, $S_1$ and $S_3$ would have independent couplings and there would be no relation between the two anomalies at all. With it, one matrix describes both. That is the sense in which Pati–Salam is predictive: not through unification, but through parity.

The Fit

$$ R_{D^{(*)}}\equiv\frac{\Gamma(B\to D\tau\nu,D\tau\hat N)/\Gamma(B\to D\tau\nu_\tau)_{\rm SM}}{\Gamma(B\to D\ell\nu,D\ell\hat N)/\Gamma(B\to D\ell\nu_\ell)_{\rm SM}}\simeq1+\left(\frac{v^2V_{R,cs}}{4M_{S_1}^2V_{L,cb}}y_{23}\right)^2, $$

with $R_{D^{(*)}}\simeq1.14\pm0.04$ preferred.

The best fit: $M_{S_3}=6.1$ TeV, $y_{22}=0.030$, $y_{23}=1.02$, $y_{33}=0.006$, $\chi^2=2.2$ for six observables — "Both LQs are in the TeV mass range and $y_{23}$ is of order one, much larger than all the other couplings."

$y_{23}\approx1$ and $y_{22}\approx0.03$ is a ratio of thirty. That is the hierarchical structure that normal ordering gives, and it is what allows the $23$ block to drive the anomaly while the $12$ block stays quiet.

"$B\to K\nu\nu$ and $B\to K\mu\tau$ are predicted large enough that Belle II can exclude the relevant parameter space, while LHC can independently test this model in the channels $pp\to\tau\nu$ and $pp\to S_1S_1\to t\bar\mu t\bar\mu$."

"Note that we further predict the $S_1$ branching ratios ${\rm BR}(S_1\to t\mu)\simeq{\rm BR}(S_1\to c\tau)\simeq{\rm BR}(S_1\to b\,{\rm invisible})$."

Three branching ratios equal, which is a sharp signature. If you find an $S_1$ you know which final states to look in, and the equality is a consequence of the coupling matrix, not a fit.

Ideas Not Yet in the Corpus

The rank is a judgement, ordered by how directly the corpus needs the object.

1. Pati–Salam's symmetry-breaking scalars are leptoquarks

The idea. The $(\mathbf{10},\mathbf1,\mathbf3)$ needed to break $SU(2)_R$ contains $\mathbf6$ and $\mathbf3$ pieces under $SU(3)$, and the $\mathbf3$s are leptoquarks. "contain amongst other things all $S_j$ LQs."

What it would add. A completely different merit for the group. The corpus presents $\Delta_R$ as the field that gives $\nu_R$ a mass and breaks $B-L$. It is also a leptoquark multiplet, so a Pati–Salam model automatically contains the states that current $B$-anomaly explanations need. There is no extra field to add.

Where it lands. articles_physics/the-pati-salam-model-in-the-biquaternion-framework.md, in the scalar-sector section, next to the $\Delta_R$ discussion.

What stands in the way. Nothing. The decomposition $(\mathbf{10},\mathbf1,\mathbf3)\to\mathbf6\oplus\mathbf3\oplus\mathbf1$ under $SU(3)$ is standard.

2. One Yukawa matrix gives both neutrino masses and leptoquark couplings

The idea. $y_L$ multiplies $Q_L L_L S_3$ and $\frac12L_L^cL_L\delta_3$, because both come from the same $SU(4)$ invariant.

What it would add. The sharpest prediction in the batch, and it is of a kind the corpus does not have: two experimental sectors — neutrino oscillations and $B$ decays — determined by one matrix. The consequence is that measuring neutrino parameters fixes leptoquark couplings, and the CP phases become predictable from $B$ physics.

Where it lands. The Pati–Salam article's Yukawa section, and the seesaw article.

What stands in the way. Nothing in the Pati–Salam limit; the relation is exact there and broken when the model is extended (see idea 4).

3. Normal ordering is required, so JUNO decides whether this model lives

The idea. The $23$-dominant, $12$-suppressed structure of $M_\nu$ in normal ordering is what lets $S_3$ explain $R(K^{(*)})$ and evade $\mu\to e$. Inverted ordering fails.

What it would add. A hard link between a neutrino-oscillation measurement and a $B$-physics explanation. This is a falsification channel the corpus should record: if the mass ordering is inverted, this model is dead, and that is a decisive test with a definite timeline.

Where it lands. The seesaw article, and the Pati–Salam article's experimental section.

What stands in the way. The claim is specific to type-II seesaw dominance; with type-I dominance the relation changes, as the paper notes and as 1808.07492 shows.

4. The framework cannot explain both anomalies without losing predictivity

The idea. $S_1$'s coupling is tied to $M_{N_R}$ by the same Pati–Salam relation. A light $N_R$ (needed for $B\to D\tau N_R$) is weakly coupled, so the rate is too small. Decoupling the two costs the $M_\nu$–$R(K^{(*)})$ link.

What it would add. A worked example of a model that is predictive and incomplete, with the incompleteness traceable to the same structure that gives the predictivity. The corpus should not present this as a success story. It is a partial success with a precise failure mode, and the failure is more informative.

Where it lands. The Pati–Salam article's $B$-anomaly section.

What stands in the way. Nothing. The paper states it in one paragraph.

5. Parity relates the two leptoquark sectors

The idea. $y_L=y_R$ because of the left–right exchange symmetry, so one matrix describes both $S_3$ and $S_1$.

What it would add. The concrete mechanism by which Pati–Salam is predictive here — not unification, parity. The corpus should reorganise its account of why the group is useful: unification relates couplings at a scale, but the discrete symmetry relates couplings at all scales, which is stronger.

Where it lands. The Pati–Salam article's motivation section.

What stands in the way. The equality holds only in the exact parity limit; the $Z_{LR}$ must be imposed and must survive to the leptoquark scale, which the paper assumes.

6. Texture zeros become leptoquark coupling zeros

The idea. A zero in $M_\nu$ is a zero in $y_L$, which is a leptoquark that does not couple to a given generation pair.

What it would add. A physical interpretation for a formal device. The corpus's neutrino article, if it discusses texture zeros, presents them as a parametrisation. Here they are statements about new particles — "this leptoquark does not decay to $e$ and $\mu$" — and therefore testable.

Where it lands. The seesaw article.

What stands in the way. The correspondence holds in the type-II-dominant case only.

One observation about the corpus's map

This paper and the Feldmann–Luhn–Moch LFV paper (1608.04124) are the two places in the batch where Pati–Salam makes a sharp prediction in flavour, and they get their sharpness from different structures.

Feldmann–Luhn–Moch get it from the $SU(4)$ adjoint direction: the $\mathrm{Diag}(1,1,1,-3)$ that produces the Georgi–Jarlskog factor. That is group theory, and it holds for any Pati–Salam model with that scalar content.

Heeck–Teresi get it from the Yukawa identification: one matrix for two sectors, which requires the specific $\Delta_L$ coupling and the type-II seesaw to dominate.

So one prediction is robust and one is conditional. The Georgi–Jarlskog factor is a consequence of the group; the $M_\nu$–$R(K^{(*)})$ link is a consequence of a choice about which seesaw dominates. The corpus should mark the difference, because a reader who sees both presented as "Pati–Salam predicts" will overestimate the second.

The second thing to record is the failure. The paper's honest statement that $S_1$ cannot explain $R(D^{(*)})$ within the predictive framework is more valuable than a fit would have been. It tells you that the two anomalies, if both real, require physics beyond minimal Pati–Salam — and that is a constraint on the group, not a triumph for it.
