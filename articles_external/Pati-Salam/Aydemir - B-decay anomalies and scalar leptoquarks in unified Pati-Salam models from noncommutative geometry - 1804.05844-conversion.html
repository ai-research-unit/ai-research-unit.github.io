What the Paper Does

The paper asks whether the scalar leptoquark that could explain the $B$-decay anomalies fits inside the Pati–Salam models that come out of noncommutative geometry. Three such models were built by Chamseddine, Connes and van Suijlekom, differing only in their scalar content, and the paper checks each one's fermion–scalar couplings. Exactly one, Model C, contains a complex $(6,1,1)$ whose $SU(3)\times U(1)$ descendant is $S_1^*(3,1,-\frac13)$, the leptoquark the anomaly fits want. That state cannot decay the proton, because the order-one condition of the noncommutative geometry removes its diquark couplings. Coupling unification in this non-supersymmetric Pati–Salam model needs one intermediate breaking scale. The paper is a phenomenological audit of a geometric construction: the interesting content is that the scalar sector, and with it the leptoquark content, is derived and not chosen.

Summary in the Authors' Words

What the Models Are

The gauge structure is $G_{422}=SU(4)\times SU(2)_L\times SU(2)_R$ with the gauge couplings unified at a single scale. The fermion content is the Standard Model plus three right-handed neutrinos. What is unusual is the scalar sector: its content and its couplings are fixed by the noncommutative geometry of the model.

The construction is explained in the paper's own terms. Noncommutative geometry "generalizes the concepts of spinor bundle and principal bundle, and manages to combine the two concepts into one". The Dirac operator then has a continuous part, which is the ordinary one, and a discrete part, which "accounts for the representation space of the gauge algebra". Gauge transformations run in both directions, and the fields in the discrete direction are the Higgs fields. So

$$ A'_\mu=uA_\mu u^{-1}-\frac{i}{g}u\partial_\mu u^{-1}, \qquad \Phi'=\Phi+u\Phi u^{-1}-\frac{i}{g}u[D,u^{-1}]. $$

The paper notes the consequence for the geometry: "if one tries to interpret the space as an extra-dimension model, the metric dimension of the extra dimension is zero, because the extra direction is discrete. Therefore, in contrast to the usual extra-dimension models, no extra effort is required to stabilize the two 'sheets'."

The term $u[\Phi,u^{-1}]$ is what decides everything. If it can be written in the form $\sum a[D,b]$, the gauge field stays a gauge field; if it cannot, the transformation is not closed and higher-order terms are needed. Requiring it to vanish is the order-one condition. The paper records the known result: the order-one condition together with a Majorana mass for right-handed neutrinos "selects the SM gauge group from $G_{422}$". Lifting it partially, on the Standard Model subgroup, gives Model A; lifting it and restricting to a left–right asymmetric slice of the moduli space of the Dirac operator gives Model B; lifting it on the full moduli space gives Model C.

The three scalar sectors are:

model symmetry scalars
A $G_{422}$ $\varphi(1,2,2)$, $\Sigma(15,1,1)$, $\Delta_R(4,1,2)$
B $G_{422}$ $\varphi(1,2,2)$, $\tilde\Sigma(15,2,2)$, $\Delta_R(10,1,3)$, $H_R(6,1,1)$
C $G_{422D}$ $\varphi(1,2,2)$, $\tilde\Sigma(15,2,2)$, $\Delta_R(10,1,3)$, $H_R(6,1,1)$, $\Delta_L(10,3,1)$, $H_L(6,1,1)$

Only C is left–right symmetric, and only C contains $H_L$.

What the Anomalies Ask For

The paper reviews the two anomaly families and the operators each needs. For $R_{K^{(*)}}$, the target is $b_L\to s_L\mu^+\mu^-$ through

$$ \mathcal O_9\propto\bar s_L\gamma^\mu b_L\,\bar\mu\gamma_\mu\mu, \qquad \mathcal O_{10}\propto\bar s_L\gamma^\mu b_L\,\bar\mu\gamma_\mu\gamma_5\mu, $$

and at tree level these come from $S_3^{(4/3)}$ (giving $\mathcal O_9-\mathcal O_{10}$) or $R_2^{(2/3)}$ (giving $\mathcal O_9+\mathcal O_{10}$); the wrong-chirality versions come from $\tilde R_2^{(2/3)}$ or $\tilde S_1^{(4/3)}$. Global fits prefer $C_9$ suppressed by about $25\%$ from its Standard Model value. For $R_{D^{(*)}}$ the target is

$$ \bar c_L\gamma^\mu b_L\,\bar\tau_L\gamma_\mu\nu_{\tau L}, $$

which needs $S_1^{(1/3)}$ or $S_3^{(1/3)}$. The paper's reason for preferring $S_1^{(1/3)}$ is stated: it explains $R_{D^*}$ at tree level and $R_{K^{(*)}}$ at one loop, the same order as the Standard Model contribution. The objection that this conflicts with $R_{D^{(*)}}^{\mu/e}$ is answered by allowing the mass to be "a TeV or larger, the preferred mass being a few TeV".

The Leptoquark

Model C's $H_L(6,1,1)_{422}$ is complex and splits into $S_1^*(3,1,-\frac13)_{321}$ and a partner that "couples only to diquarks". Only the first has the right coupling to left-handed fermions. So the answer to the paper's question is:

$H_3^L(3,1,-\frac13)_{321}$, from $(6,1,1)_{422}$, at a mass of a few TeV, in Model C.

Why the Proton Survives

This is the section the corpus should read. A light colour triplet with these quantum numbers would normally be fatal, and the paper explains why it is not.

The ordinary Pati–Salam scalar sector — $\Delta_R(10,1,3)$, $\Delta_L(10,3,1)$ and the bidoublet $\varphi(1,2,2)$ — is proton-safe, but for a reason that is easy to misstate. The protection is a global symmetry that exists because those multiplets are totally symmetric under $SU(4)$. The paper says it plainly: the mechanism "is due to the totally-symmetric nature of $\Delta_{L,R}$ under $SU(4)$, and existence of any field(s) transforming anti-symmetrically would potentially spoil this symmetry". Model C has exactly such a field: $H_{R/L}(6,1,1)$ "reside in the totally-anti-symmetric representations of $SU(4)$".

So the sextet is the danger, and the geometry is what saves it: "the restrictive nature of the non-commutative-geometry framework happens to operate in our favor and does not allow the proton-decay-inducing diquark couplings". The paper is careful that this is a statement about the model and not about the low-energy theory: from a bottom-up view "one observes only the absence of the corresponding diquark couplings. From this viewpoint, there is no indication regarding the nature of the underlying mechanism".

The price is stated too: to keep the $S_1^*$ light one must suppress its mixing with the diquark-coupled partner, and assume that partner is heavy. The paper concedes this "might raise the question of naturalness, which is not different from the corresponding situation found in generic GUT models".

Unification

The paper runs the one-loop renormalisation group equations and asks whether the leptoquark helps. The answer is that Pati–Salam unification needs one intermediate breaking step: $G_{422}$ emerges at a scale $M_U$ and stays intact down to $M_C$, where it breaks to the Standard Model. The paper notes that a single light $(3,1,-\frac13)$ in a non-supersymmetric framework "does not imply unification of couplings as the $SU(5)$ GUT theory would require", and that $SO(10)$ could accommodate the same state with intermediate scales.

The Closing Argument for the Framework

The paper's discussion is a defence of the construction's economy. The Standard Model action is rewritten "in a Fujikawa-like form familiar from the study of chiral anomalies", which introduces a scale "not a Wilsonian cut-off, fixed by the existence of one overall coupling, thus implying the unification of the three SM couplings at a GUT scale without GUT degrees of freedom". The scheme "hides a left-right symmetric structure which can be naturally broken to the canonical SM", and this completion is "quite constrained compared to the already existing effective field theory literature". The paper also sketches a possible ultraviolet origin: fermions propagating in a background of induced gravitational and gauge degrees of freedom, whose zero-mode integration reproduces the Fujikawa form.

Ideas Not Yet in the Corpus

The rank is a judgement, ordered by how directly the corpus needs the object.

1. What a real noncommutative Pati–Salam model looks like, and why the corpus is not one

The idea. Take a spectral triple whose finite part is chosen so that the order-one condition holds, and the gauge group comes out as $G_{422}$ rather than being declared; lift the condition partially and the scalar content comes out as one of three lists. The scalars are not chosen — they are the fields the discrete direction requires.

What it would add. The comparison standard the corpus's spectral-triple article says it lacks. articles_physics/the-spectral-triple-of-the-biquaternion-algebra.md states its own position bluntly: "The framework is not a non-commutative geometry of the Standard-Model kind", because Connes' program needs an almost-commutative triple with a finite factor and the axioms — first-order condition, orientability, Poincaré duality, KO-dimension — that select it, and because the biquaternion algebra with its involution $\flat=-{}^{*}$ is not a $C^*$-algebra at all, so there are no unitaries to quotient. The article examines the first-order condition and does not reproduce orientability or Poincaré duality.

This paper shows what the alternative costs and buys. Buys: the group, the scalar content, and the proton-decay structure come out of the geometry. Costs: a finite geometry engineered for the Standard Model, a Dirac operator with a discrete part, and a scale that is not a cutoff.

Where it lands. The spectral-triple article, in The Almost-Commutative Triple and the Finite Geometry and in What Is Established and What Is Interpretation. The claim to add is comparative and honest: the corpus's triple fails the axioms by construction; a triple that passes them produces the Pati–Salam group and its scalar list, at the price of a different algebra.

What stands in the way. The corpus's algebra is $\mathbb B$ and the NCG models use a finite algebra over $\mathbb C$ with a real structure of specified KO-dimension. Matching them is a research problem, not a paragraph. But the corpus's article already frames the question correctly, and this paper is the reference that answers "what would it look like if it worked".

2. A discrete internal direction whose metric dimension is zero

The idea. The internal space is not a small rolled-up manifold. It is a finite set of points, the "distance" across it is zero in the metric sense, and consequently "no extra effort is required to stabilize the two sheets" — the modulus that would destabilise a Kaluza–Klein circle does not exist.

What it would add. A third option in a dichotomy the corpus has not opened. The corpus builds its extra structure algebraically and its spacetime continuously; it never considers that an internal space can be finite, with the Higgs as the gauge field of the discrete direction. That identification — the Higgs is the component of the connection along a direction with no extent — is one sentence and it is not in the corpus.

Where it lands. The spectral-triple article's Inner Fluctuations, Gauge Fields, and the Spectral Action, which already says "the scalar field is the off-diagonal part of $D_F$". This paper supplies the physical reading of the same statement, with the stabilisation argument attached.

What stands in the way. The corpus's $D_F$ is the mass term, and the article already notes that the identification is exact at the level of the operator. What is missing is the geometric story — that the internal direction is finite, so there is no modulus — and that story needs no new mathematics to state.

3. Antisymmetric $SU(4)$ representations spoil proton stability

The idea. The standard Pati–Salam scalar sector, built from the symmetric $10$s, is proton-safe because a global symmetry survives. Any field in an antisymmetric representation of $SU(4)$ — the $6$ — can carry diquark couplings and destroy the protection. A light sextet is therefore dangerous by default, and its safety must be explained, not assumed.

What it would add. A sharpening of something the corpus says loosely. articles_physics/the-pati-salam-model-in-the-biquaternion-framework.md carries the sextet with its $K_L\to\mu e$ bound — a flavour bound — and mentions proton decay once. It does not say that the sextet is the representation that endangers the proton, or why the decuplet does not. The distinction is structural: symmetric versus antisymmetric under $SU(4)$, hence whether a global symmetry can be defined at all.

Where it lands. The Pati–Salam article's boson table and proton-decay paragraph.

What stands in the way. The corpus's frame does not contain a $10$, so the contrast cannot be drawn inside it. But the statement to import is a caution about the $6$: it is the multiplet that can write a diquark coupling, and any model keeping it light owes an explanation. That is one row in the table and it is worth having.

4. Unification with one intermediate scale — an answer to the corpus's $\sin^2\theta_W$ failure

The idea. Pati–Salam unification of the three couplings is achievable in a non-supersymmetric model with a single intermediate breaking step: $G_{422}$ at $M_U$, unbroken down to $M_C$, then the Standard Model.

What it would add. A candidate repair for the corpus's most explicit failure. The Pati–Salam article records $\sin^2\theta_W=\frac38=0.375$ against $0.231$ measured and calls it a failure of the assumption that the three couplings meet at one scale. This paper says the meeting can be arranged — not by threshold corrections, which the Aulakh conversion shows are worth about $10\%$, but by an intermediate scale.

Where it lands. articles_physics/the-pati-salam-model-in-the-biquaternion-framework.md, the paragraph on the unification condition, and the ledger row recording the $\sin^2\theta_W$ failure. The row can then say: the failure is of the single-step assumption and not of the group; one intermediate breaking restores it, at the cost of a second scale the frame does not supply.

What stands in the way. The intermediate scale is the price: $G_{422}$ would have to survive a long desert, which needs a hierarchy the corpus has no mechanism for. So the import converts a failure into a conditional: the group is not excluded, the single-step version is.

5. The scalar sector as output rather than input

The idea. Models A, B, C differ only in their scalars, and which one you get is decided by how much of the order-one condition you keep. The scalar content is a consequence of a condition on the Dirac operator.

What it would add. A direct attack on the row of the corpus's table that reads "which charge each field carries is chosen". The corpus is candid that its field content and its assignments are declared. Here the content is derived from a condition, and even the choice among three models is a choice of how strictly to impose it.

Where it lands. The Pati–Salam article's table Where Each Factor Comes From, and How Firmly — the rows marked "declared".

What stands in the way. As in idea 1, the corpus's algebra cannot host the condition. The importable content is the standard of derivation: a field should be present because a condition requires it, and a corpus that cannot meet that standard should mark the shortfall explicitly, which the article already half does.

One tension recorded

The $R_{K^{(*)}}$ anomaly that motivates the paper was not confirmed. The 2022 LHCb measurement of $R_K$ moved to values consistent with the Standard Model, and the earlier deficit is no longer a live target. The paper's leptoquark remains a legitimate particle to search for and the proton-stability argument stands unchanged, but the reason for wanting it at a few TeV has weakened. A reader arriving at this conversion after 2022 should read the model-building sections as lasting and the motivation section as historical.
