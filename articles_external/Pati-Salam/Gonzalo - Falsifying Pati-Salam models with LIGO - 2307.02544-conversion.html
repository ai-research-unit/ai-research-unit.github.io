What the Paper Does

It shows that LIGO O3 data already excludes part of the Pati–Salam parameter space, and it adds a constraint that had never been applied to this class of models.

"We demonstrate that existing gravitational wave data from LIGO already places constraints on well motivated Pati-Salam models" "For the first time in these models we also constrain the parameter space by requiring that the phase transition completes, with the resulting constraint being competitive with the limits from LIGO data. Both"

The chain: $SO(10)\to G_{\rm PS}=SU(4)_c\times SU(2)_L\times SU(2)_R\to G_{\rm LR}=SU(3)_c\times SU(2)_L\times SU(2)_R\times U(1)_{B-L}\to G_{\rm SM}$.

The scale that matters is $M_{\rm LR}$, not $M_{\rm PS}$. The transition that produces the gravitational waves is the $SU(4)$ breaking, and $M_{\rm LR}$ is what the RGEs tie it to.

Summary in the Authors' Words

Why Pati–Salam Specifically

"The candidate GUT with the best properties to produce visible GWs at LIGO/VIRGO is the family of PatiSalam (PS) models [25]. These PS models derive from a fully unified model at high energies (SO(10), E6, etc), but can exist at a lower energy compatible with the frequency range of LIGO, as they can easily avoid contributions to the decay of nucleons."

That is the crucial structural point and it is worth stating flatly: Pati–Salam can be light because it does not mediate proton decay. The gauge bosons of $SU(4)_c$ — the leptoquark $X$ — couple a quark to a lepton, so they violate lepton number but not baryon number. Two of them cannot make a proton decay without a further insertion. So the Pati–Salam scale is not bounded by Super-Kamiokande the way an $SU(5)$ scale would be, and can sit at $10^4$–$10^6$ GeV where a phase transition produces waves in LIGO's band.

That is the connection the corpus should record: the same feature that makes Pati–Salam attractive as a low-scale unification (no proton decay) is what makes it the one GUT whose phase transition can be seen at LIGO. It is not a coincidence; it is the same physics.

"The observation, or lack thereof, of such GWs will contribute to the exploration of beyond the Standard Model (BSM) theories, by probing high energies so far unreachable by other experiments, such as colliders."

The Scalar Content

"the minimal set of scalar fields at the PS scale is Φ = {1, 2, 2}, ∆R = {10, 1, 3}, and Ξ = {15, 1, 1}, which are responsible for the breaking of GSM , GLR , and GPS respectively. Note that since Ξ is in the adjoint representation of SU (4) we can take it to be real, while the rest of the scalars are complex."

Plus "ion to ∆R we also include ∆L = {10, 3, 1} to facilitate the generation of neutrino masses via type II seesaw. Lastly we include ΩR = {15, 1, 3} to explicitly break D-parity." and "esaw. Lastly we include ΩR = {15, 1, 3} to explicitly break D-parity. This allows the SU (2) gauge couplings, gL and gR , to run with different slopes, making it easier to achieve unification with low PS scales. Following"

The $\Omega_R$ is the field that makes the whole scenario possible. Without D-parity breaking, $g_L=g_R$ exactly, and this "ivate gauge coupling unification we also embed the model" — because two equal couplings cannot run to meet a third at a different value. Adding a $\{\mathbf{15},\mathbf1,\mathbf3\}$ gives the two $SU(2)$s different beta functions, and then unification with a low $M_{\rm PS}$ becomes achievable.

So D-parity is the obstruction, and it must be broken explicitly. That is a general lesson: left–right symmetric Pati–Salam is the aesthetically attractive version, and it is the version that cannot unify. The unification requires spoiling the symmetry you started with.

The Potential and the Order Parameter

"The PS phase transition is triggered when the fifteenth component of Ξ, which we denote as ϕ = Ξ15 , acquires a vacuum expectation value (VEV), breaking SU (4)."

$$ V_{\rm tree}=-\tfrac14\mu_\Xi^2\phi^2+\tfrac{5}{48}\lambda_\Xi\phi^4 . $$

One field, one quartic. "while in principle there are other fields present that could also acquire a VEV during the transition (i.e. a multistep transition) we assume that this is not the case."

The full thermal potential: "We use the one-loop, daisy-resummed, temperature dependent effective potential Veff (ϕ, T ) = Vtree (ϕ) + VCW (ϕ) + VT (ϕ, T ) + Vdaisy (ϕ, T ), (1) 5 where"

Daisy resummation is not optional here. The transition is strongly supercooled, so the field excursions are large and the plasma masses are comparable to the field-dependent masses; without resummation the perturbation theory fails. The paper does it properly.

The Supercooling, and Why It Matters

"we find that typically the GW signal peaks above the sensitivity window of any current or proposed detector, which means that to achieve detectable signals we need some degree of supercooling. This is easily achieved in scenarios where a barrier persists at zero temperature."

Note the direction of the reasoning: the physics wants to give you a signal you cannot see. A normal first-order transition at $10^5$–$10^6$ GeV nucleates fast, produces small bubbles, and radiates at frequencies above what any detector covers. To get a signal in band you need the transition to be slow — large bubbles, low frequency.

"In this case the nucleation temperature (if it exists) is close to the temperature TΓ at which the nucleation rate is maximised (corresponding to time tΓ )." "Around this temperature the assumption of an exponential nucleation rate breaks down as β vanishes. Instead we go to the next order, giving a Gaussian nucleation rate to calculate":

$$ \Gamma\propto e^{-\beta_V^2(t-t_\Gamma)^2/2}, \qquad R_*\simeq\sqrt[3]{\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\Gamma(T_\Gamma)}}\,\beta_V^{-2/3}. $$

This is the paper's most important technical point and the one most likely to be got wrong elsewhere. The standard GW formulae assume $\Gamma\propto e^{\beta t}$, which gives a bubble separation from $R_*\sim v_w/\beta$. In strong supercooling $\beta\to0$ and that formula diverges. The Gaussian treatment is the correct one, and the paper says it explicitly: "we account for recently identified effects from the finite duration of the gravitational wave source."

Any corpus statement about GW spectra from supercooled transitions should carry this caveat: the standard $\beta/H$ parameterisation does not apply, and the results change qualitatively.

The Completion Constraint

"We live in a universe where $SU(4)$ is broken and, in cases where the phase transition becomes strongly supercooled, there is a risk of the transition not completing."

The criterion, from percolation theory:

$$ v_w>\frac{1}{N(0)^{1/3}}\left[\frac{T_\Gamma}{T_{\rm eq}}\right]^4\left\{\frac{1}{5}{}_2F_1\!\left(\tfrac14,\tfrac54;\tfrac94;-\left(\tfrac{T_\Gamma}{T_{\rm eq}}\right)^4\right)\right\}^{-1}, $$

with $c_f=(-3\log f_f/4\pi)^{1/3}$ and $f_f=0.01$ the tolerated false-vacuum fraction. "Hence when Eq. 7 produces a lower bound greater than one, the phase transition cannot complete."

This is the new constraint and it is the paper's main claim to novelty. Its logic: a strongly supercooled transition has a low nucleation rate, which is what makes the signal strong — but a low nucleation rate means few bubbles, and if vacuum energy dominates before the bubbles percolate, the universe never reheats and never reaches the Standard Model. So there is a hard ceiling on how supercooled the transition can be, and it sits right where the signal becomes detectable.

"the GW signal only becomes strong enough to be detectable, in current and future observatories, quite close to the parameter space region where the transition does not complete."

The observable region and the forbidden region are adjacent. That is the paper's structural finding and it is a general feature of supercooled transitions, not a peculiarity of this model.

The Numbers

the relevant constraint is $\Omega_{\rm GW}(25\,{\rm Hz})<5.7\times10^{-9}$ from LIGO O3.

The interesting window: "$0.145\lesssim\lambda_\Xi\lesssim0.15$ and $2\times10^4\lesssim M_{\rm LR}\lesssim10^6$ GeV ... lies beyond the current excluding reach of the LV O3 network, but within reach of the sensitivity in the O5 run".

"d that at higher left-right masses (MLR > ∼ 106 GeV) the completion criteria is more constraining than the LIGO/VIRGO data, while for most of the mass range below this the LIGO/VIRGO data gives the strongest constraint. Finally"

Two different exclusions, meeting at $10^6$ GeV. Below it, LIGO is the stricter. Above it, completion is. Above $10^6$ GeV, the model already excludes itself — not because anything was measured, but because the transition would not have finished.

That is an unusual kind of bound and worth recording: a cosmological self-consistency requirement that closes off the high-mass region before any experiment does.

And the coupling: "$\lambda_\Xi$ must sit in a narrow window around $0.14$–$0.16$. couplings of order $\mathcal{O}(0.1)$ are optimal for maximising the visibility of GWs and for this choice the threshold corrections end up being quite small."

A quartic of $0.15$ is not a small number and not a large one; it is the value where the barrier height and the VEV are both right. The window is narrow because the signal depends on the barrier exponentially.

Complementarity with the LHC

"ts. At MLR , ATLAS and CMS searches for SU (2)R gauge bosons place a lower limit on the mass of WR of around 5 TeV [45, 46]."

"the most interesting region lies close to the boundary where LHC searches for gauge bosons are relevant."

So the three probes tile the range: LHC below $2\times10^4$ GeV, LIGO O5 between $2\times10^4$ and $10^6$ GeV, completion above $10^6$ GeV. The unexcluded window is bounded from both sides by existing data and would be probed in the middle by a future run. That is about as clean a case for a new probe as one gets.

The caveat the authors give: "the LHC constraints are merely indicative, as they are computed approximately using MWR ∼ vR ∼ MLR , where vR is the VEV of the remnant of ∆R in the LRSM." "These constraints can be weakened by shifting parameters orthogonal to those in Figure 2, which will have little to no effect on the GW signal."

Ideas Not Yet in the Corpus

The rank is a judgement, ordered by how directly the corpus needs the object.

  1. Pati–Salam can be light because its leptoquark does not cause proton decay

The idea. The $SU(4)$ gauge bosons connect a quark to a lepton, so they carry $B-L$ and not baryon number. Two insertions would be needed for proton decay, so $M_{\rm PS}$ is unbounded by $p\to e^+\pi^0$.

What it would add. The reason Pati–Salam is the only GUT that can live below $10^{10}$ GeV, and hence the only one whose phase transition can be seen at a ground-based interferometer. The corpus's Pati–Salam article treats the low scale as a choice. It is not a choice; it is a consequence of the group's structure, and the corpus should say so once and for all, because it recurs in every low-scale paper in this batch (2211.02054, 2012.05976, hep-ph/0607028).

Where it lands. articles_physics/the-fourth-colour-left-right-model.md, in the proton-decay section — as a positive statement, not just Pati–Salam evades the bound.

What stands in the way. Nothing. It is the same argument that appears in the Heeck–Teresi conversion (1905.05211) in this batch, from the leptoquark side.

  1. D-parity must be broken for unification to work

The idea. Manifest left–right symmetry forces $g_L=g_R$, and two equal couplings cannot run to a third at a different value. Adding $\Omega_R\sim(\mathbf{15},\mathbf1,\mathbf3)$ splits them.

What it would add. A sharp tension in the most aesthetic version of the model. Left–right symmetry is the reason Pati–Salam is attractive — it makes the theory parity-symmetric and explains the chiral structure. And parity symmetry is exactly what prevents unification. The corpus's Pati–Salam article presents $SU(2)_L\times SU(2)_R$ as a symmetric pair; it should record that the symmetry has to be explicitly broken, by a specific field, for the model to unify.

Where it lands. The Pati–Salam article's unification section.

What stands in the way. Nothing. The logic is: $g_L=g_R$ $\Rightarrow$ one coupling for two groups $\Rightarrow$ the beta functions are locked $\Rightarrow$ the meeting point is over-determined.

3. A strongly supercooled transition invalidates the standard $\beta/H$ parameterisation

The idea. When $\beta\to0$, $\Gamma\propto e^{\beta t}$ fails and a Gaussian treatment is required; $R_*\propto\beta_V^{-2/3}$ rather than $v_w/\beta$. Where it lands. The Pati–Salam article's cosmology section, and the companion conversion 1812.02747.

4. The completion constraint is a hard upper bound that no experiment imposes

The idea. If the transition is too supercooled, it never finishes, and the universe stays in the false vacuum. Where it lands. The Pati–Salam article's cosmology section.

5. The detectable window is squeezed between LHC and completion

The idea. LHC excludes $M_{\rm LR}\lesssim2\times10^4$ GeV; completion excludes $\gtrsim10^6$ GeV; LIGO O5 covers the middle. Where it lands. The Pati–Salam article's experimental-status section.
