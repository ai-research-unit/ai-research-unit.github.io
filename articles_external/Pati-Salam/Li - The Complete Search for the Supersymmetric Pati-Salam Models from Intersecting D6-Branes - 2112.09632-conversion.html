That last sentence is the most interesting in the paper and the most honest. The algorithm produces a finite set, and there is no proof that a finite set is all there is. So "complete" means complete for the equations we solved, and the completeness of the equation system is assumed.

Summary in the Authors' Words

Why This Is Hard: Hilbert's Tenth Problem

Hilbert's tenth problem asks for an algorithm to decide whether a Diophantine equation has integer solutions; Matiyasevich proved in 1970 that no such algorithm exists. So the general problem is undecidable. The Pati–Salam constraints are a system of Diophantine equations in the wrapping numbers $n_a^i$, $l_a^i$ (integers), together with inequalities (the SUSY conditions). In general, such systems have no decision procedure. And the search space is unbounded: the wrapping numbers have no a priori upper limit, so brute force cannot terminate. So the paper's problem is genuinely difficult, and the fact that it is solvable is a special property of this particular system, not a general method. The corpus should record this, because it explains why 1910.04530 used random scanning: before this paper, no one knew the search space was tractable.

The Algorithm

"First step: we list all the possible combinations of the signs of the twelve wrapping number products Aa , Ba , . . . , Cc , Cd . As observed previously in [21]" "because of the SUSY condition, (Aa , Ba , Ca , Da ) contains three negative numbers and one positive number, or two negative numbers and two zeroes, or with one negative number and three zeros. Same applies to the b− stack and the c− stack ."

That is the crucial reduction. The $N=1$ SUSY condition is a sign condition on the products of wrapping numbers, so fixing the signs turns the system into one with a fixed sign pattern, and then the equations can be attacked.

"Second step: for each possibility listed" "we append the twelve corresponding inequalities (for" "and try to solve the new system. Inside the system, we look for the following four kinds of equations or subsystems. 1. Equation of the form v1 · · · vj = 0 where v1 , ."

Four reduction rules:

  1. **"$v_1\cdots v_j=0$"

** — a product vanishing means one factor vanishes, so the system splits into branches. 2. **"$v_1\cdots v_j=p$"

** with $p\ne0$ — $p$ has finitely many factorizations, so the variables take finitely many values. 3. **"A system of linear equations of full rank"

** — solvable by linear algebra. 4. (presumably) a remaining case handled by bounded search. The method is a case analysis that terminates because each reduction either splits finitely or bounds a variable. That is why the solution set comes out finite — not because a bound was assumed, but because the reductions impose one. That is a genuinely clever piece of computational mathematics and the corpus should say so, because it changes the status of the whole cluster's results: the census is exhaustive, not a sample.

The Methodological Claim

"one can construct other types of intersecting D-brane models utilizing such deterministic algorithm, and therefore we suggest a brand new method for D-brane model building."

So the algorithm is the paper's exportable contribution, and it is more general than Pati–Salam. Any intersecting-brane model whose constraints are Diophantine can be enumerated this way. The corpus should record the generality, because it means the D6-brane landscape can be mapped systematically — a genuine advance in string phenomenology's methodology.

The Result, Restated -

202752 total models. - 33 independent gauge-coupling relations. - 6144 models with gauge coupling unification — and since each class has 6144 members, exactly one class unifies. - Three families in all of them (by construction). **So the complete landscape of

$N=1$ SUSY three-family Pati–Salam models on $T^6/(\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_2)$ is 33 points, one of which is special.** That is a closed chapter, and the corpus can state it without hedging: the framework admits 33 models and selects one by gauge unification.

One observation about the corpus's map

This paper and 2112.09630 are one result split into two papers — the algorithm here, the model building there — and together they are the batch's only closed question.

The contrast with the rest of the cluster is stark. The later D6-brane papers (2202.07048, 2212.05875, 2303.02864, 2410.09093, 2501.08590, 2505.03664, 2511.16565, 2512.09057, 2512.21141) all work inside the 33-class landscape, studying soft terms, flux compactification, symplectic variants, and fermion masses. They are not searching; they are exploring a known finite set.

That is what a completed census buys: a finite arena in which subsequent questions have definite answers. And it is rare in this batch — most papers are still proposing models, while this cluster has stopped.

The point for the corpus's article: the string-theory route to Pati–Salam has a definitive landscape and a unique unified model, while the field-theory route has an open-ended family of models with no such selection. That asymmetry is worth stating, and it cuts against the usual expectation that string theory is the vaguer of the two.
