What the Paper Does

It uses $SU(3)\times SU(3)$ as the family group, and argues that more non-abelian factors mean more predictivity.

"tract: We present a framework of underlying SU (3) × SU (3) family symmetries consistent with Pati-Salam unification and discuss advantages that can justify introducing multiple non-Abelian factors. Advantages"

"uum alignment and increased predictivity. We explore in this framework deviations from tri-bi-maximal neutrinos, such as relatively large θ13 . Keywords"

So the paper is about why $SU(3)\times SU(3)$, and about $\theta_{13}$.

Summary in the Authors' Words

Why $SU(3)\times SU(3)$?

The standard choice is one non-abelian family group ($A_4$, $S_4$, $A_5$) times abelian factors. The paper uses two.

The argument: one $SU(3)$ can fix the lepton sector, another the quark sector, so the two can be tuned independently, which increases predictivity — because the scheme is more constrained, not less.

That is counter-intuitive but correct: more symmetry means fewer allowed operators, hence fewer parameters. The corpus should record the argument, because it is the standard justification for large discrete groups, stated well.

And it explains the batch's trend toward bigger groups (1406.7005's $S_4$, 1605.02072's $A_4$): larger group, fewer operators, more predictivity.

With a caveat the corpus should state: beyond a point, the symmetry forbids the required operators too, and you need more flavons to break it — which is 1003.4502's complications in the Higgs sector.

$\theta_{13}$ and the End of Tri-Bimaximal

"deviations from tri-bi-maximal neutrinos, such as relatively large θ13 . Keywords"

In 2011 $\theta_{13}$ was being measured (Daya Bay, RENO, Double Chooz reported in 2012), and the TB prediction $\theta_{13}=0$ was dying.

So the paper is a response: a framework that can accommodate a large $\theta_{13}$ naturally.

And the corpus should record the date and the shift: 1003.4502 (2010) still built on TB-compatible mixing; 1111.3952 (2011) is designed for $\theta_{13}\ne0$.

That is a one-year transition, and it is visible in the batch. The corpus should present it as a case study in how flavour model building responds to data.

The Relation Between the Two $SU(3)$s

The paper's framework: $SU(3)$ acting on the lepton doublets and another on the quark doublets, with the PS unification relating them.

And the PS structure means the two sectors share the $\bar{\mathbf4}$: the leptons and quarks are in the same multiplet, so the two $SU(3)$s must be consistent with that.

That is the "interplay" of 1003.4502, in a different group.

The Flavour-Changing Problem

Any family symmetry with new gauge bosons — **and

$SU(3)\times SU(3)$ could be gauged or global** — has FCNC constraints. The paper's framework is presumably global (a family symmetry broken by flavons), which avoids the gauge-boson FCNC. The corpus should note the distinction: gauged family symmetry (the three-site model of 2012.10492) versus global (here, and most of the batch). And the trade: gauged gives a UV completion and testable gauge bosons; global gives fewer constraints but no UV completion.

One observation about the corpus's map

This paper is the batch's best statement of why family groups are large, and the corpus should use it to explain a pattern that otherwise looks arbitrary.

The pattern: the groups grow over time — $S_3$ (early), then $A_4$/$S_4$ (2010s), then $SU(3)\times SU(3)$, then modular groups.

The reason: each step constrains more operators, and each step needs more flavons or moduli to break.

And the endpoint is the modular-symmetry idea (2404.06520), where the group is infinite but the breaking is by the modulus, not by flavons — so the predictivity grows without a flavon sector.

That arc is the flavour chapter's spine, and the corpus should present it: from imposed discrete groups to geometric and modular origins, driven by the same desire for predictivity without proliferating scalars.

1111.3952 is a middle step, and it is the one that states the motive plainly.
