What the Paper Does

It explains both $B$-anomalies with the scalar leptoquarks that minimal Pati–Salam already contains, and states the three structural advantages in one sentence.

"Here we have shown that the Pati–Salam model naturally contains the relevant scalar LQs to address the B-meson anomalies and has additional welcome features: i) proton decay via LQs is avoided, ii) the LQ couplings are automatically chiral, iii) the couplings are restricted and related, making the model highly predictive."

Those three advantages are the paper's thesis and each deserves its own line.

i) No proton decay. The leptoquark $S_3$ couples $Q_L$ to $L_L$ — quark to lepton — which carries $B-L$ and not baryon number. A proton cannot decay through it because the final state must have the same baryon number. This is the same fact that lets Pati–Salam be light (2307.02544, 1812.02747), used here for a different purpose.

ii) Automatic chirality. The leptoquark couples to left-handed doublets only, because the $SU(4)$ structure pairs $Q_L$ with $L_L$ inside $\Psi_L$. So the scalar leptoquark has a chiral coupling without any symmetry being imposed to arrange it. Scalar leptoquarks in the SM effective theory couple to both chiralities, and the parity-violating structure is what makes them dangerous; here it is fixed.

iii) Restricted couplings. The Pati–Salam embedding ties the leptoquark couplings to the Yukawa couplings and to each other, which is what makes the model falsifiable.

Summary in the Authors' Words

The Two Anomalies and Why $R(D^{(*)})$ Is Harder

"Combined explanations for $R_{K^{(*)}}$ and $R_{D^{(*)}}$ can be found most easily in leptoquark models, although these are usually ad-hoc extensions without any relations to other processes or structures."

"the most constraining observable here is $B\to K\nu\nu$ because the involved quark-level transition $b\to s\nu\nu$ is often related to" $b\to c\tau\nu$ — "with more than one" leptoquark "the active ones in $R_{D^{(*)}}$ typically do not run into problems with $B\to K\nu\nu$".

"$R_{K^{(*)}}$ by itself is instead much easier since the EFT scale involved is $\Lambda\simeq31$ TeV."

So there is a scale separation and the paper states it: $R(K^{(*)})$ needs $31$ TeV, $R(D^{(*)})$ needs about $1$ TeV. That is why $R(D^{(*)})$ is the difficult one — it requires new physics at a scale that many other observables already probe.

The $S_3$ for $R(K^{(*)})$, and What It Predicts

"The anomaly in $R_{K^{(*)}}$ can be easily addressed by assuming an $S_3$ LQ around $10$ TeV. Within the Pati–Salam model the coupling structure is restricted to be symmetric in flavour space and can furthermore be related to the type-II seesaw neutrino mass structure, leading to interesting predictions for the neutrino mass parameters. This leads to testable rates for muonic LFV."

The symmetric coupling is a Pati–Salam consequence. The $\Delta_L=(\mathbf{10},\mathbf3,\mathbf1)$ is a symmetric two-index tensor of $SU(4)$, so its Yukawa $Y_\Delta$ is symmetric, so the leptoquark coupling is symmetric. That halves the number of parameters relative to a generic $S_3$, and it is why the neutrino mass matrix can be the same matrix.

And from that follows the normal-ordering requirement discussed at length in the proceedings conversion: the symmetric matrix that fits the neutrino data has a dominant $23$ block and small $12$ entries, which is exactly what evades $\mu\to e$ while explaining $b\to s\mu\mu$.

The $S_1$ for $R(D^{(*)})$, and the Light Neutrino

"Explaining also the anomaly in $R_{D^{(*)}}$ requires one of the right-handed neutrinos to be sub-GeV, as well as an $S_1$ LQ around TeV."

Sub-GeV $\nu_R$ is a strange requirement and it is forced. The $R(D^{(*)})$ explanation needs $B\to D\tau N_R$ with $N_R$ light enough to be produced, and the same Pati–Salam relation that ties $S_1$'s coupling to the $N_R$ mass matrix means a heavy $N_R$ gives too small a coupling.

So the model demands a light sterile neutrino, which is testable in its own right — by beta decay, by cosmology ($\Delta N_{\rm eff}$), and by beam-dump experiments.

"Pati–Salam once again heavily restricts the coupling structure, making it non-trivial to explain both $R_{K^{(*)}}$ and $R_{D^{(*)}}$ simultaneously. We have shown that one can obtain a near-perfect explanation for the anomalies despite the restricted couplings, leading again to highly testable predictions for processes such as $B\to K\nu\nu$ and $B\to K\mu\tau$, as well as direct collider searches."

The Cost

"The connection to neutrino masses has to be sacrificed; it would be interesting to address it in an extension of the basic Pati–Salam model analyzed here."

So the choice is explicit and it is the same trade as in the proceedings: you can have the neutrino-mass link or both anomalies, not both. The corpus should state this as a single fact about the framework rather than as two separate results.

The Gauge-Boson Problem

"at new physics in the TeV range, such a light PS gauge boson would conflict with experiments" — the coloured $SU(4)$ gauge bosons must be above "$\sim1000$ TeV to satisfy constraints on $K_L\to e^\pm\mu^\mp$ induced by the heavy coloured PS gauge".

So the model has TeV-scale leptoquarks and $10^3$ TeV-scale gauge bosons. That is a splitting by three orders of magnitude within one group, and it requires the gauge bosons and the scalars to get their masses from different sources. The corpus should record this because it is the structural weakness of all TeV-scale Pati–Salam leptoquark models, and it is what motivates the 4321 constructions in this batch (2106.03876 and following): $G_{4321}$ lets the vector leptoquark be light without the coloured gauge bosons being light.

"A number of modifications to the PS model have been suggested to circumvent" this — which is the opening for the rest of the literature in this batch.

Ideas Not Yet in the Corpus

1. Three structural advantages of Pati–Salam leptoquarks, stated once

The idea. No proton decay; automatic chirality; restricted and related couplings.

What it would add. The cleanest available statement of why a unified origin for leptoquarks is better than an ad-hoc one. The corpus's leptoquark discussion, if it has one, treats them as effective-field-theory objects with free couplings. The three advantages are exactly the three things an EFT cannot give you, and each follows from the $SU(4)$ structure.

Where it lands. The Pati–Salam article's leptoquark section, as the organising claim.

What stands in the way. Nothing; the three follow from the representation content.

2. The coupling matrix is symmetric because $\Delta_L$ is a symmetric tensor

The idea. $(\mathbf{10},\mathbf3,\mathbf1)$ is the symmetric two-index tensor of $SU(4)$, so $Y_\Delta=Y_\Delta^T$, so the $S_3$ coupling is symmetric.

What it would add. A group-theoretic origin for a flavour assumption. The neutrino mass matrix is symmetric for a trivial reason (it is a Majorana mass); the leptoquark coupling being symmetric is not trivial and has the same origin. That is what makes the two the same matrix.

Where it lands. The Pati–Salam article's Yukawa section.

What stands in the way. Nothing. The $\mathbf{10}$ of $SU(4)$ is symmetric; the $\mathbf{15}$ is the adjoint.

3. $R(K^{(*)})$ needs $31$ TeV; $R(D^{(*)})$ needs $1$ TeV

The idea. A scale separation of a factor of thirty between the two anomalies' EFT scales.

What it would add. A quantitative reason why one is easy and the other is hard, and why the easy one is the one that gets tied to neutrino masses. The corpus should record the numbers, because the qualitative statement "one is harder" appears everywhere without them.

Where it lands. The Pati–Salam article's $B$-anomaly section.

What stands in the way. Nothing; the EFT scales are quoted.

4. TeV leptoquarks plus $10^3$ TeV gauge bosons is the structural weakness

The idea. The scalar leptoquarks can be light (they get mass from the $\Delta$ VEVs) while the $SU(4)$ gauge bosons cannot (they are bounded by $K_L\to\mu e$).

What it would add. The problem that the whole 4321 subliterature exists to solve. The corpus's Pati–Salam article treats the leptoquarks and the gauge bosons as one sector; here they are split by three orders of magnitude, which is unnatural and is what motivates replacing $SU(4)$ with $SU(4)\times SU(3)'$.

Where it lands. The Pati–Salam article's gauge-sector section, as the transition to the 4321 papers.

What stands in the way. Nothing; it is the paper's own statement.

One observation about the corpus's map

This paper is the origin of the scalar-leptoquark programme in the batch, and reading it next to the twin-PS series shows what changed and why.

Heeck–Teresi's advantage is economy: the leptoquarks are already in the minimal model, with no new gauge group. Their weakness is the gauge bosons: keeping $SU(4)$ intact means the coloured vectors are at $10^3$ TeV while the scalars are at TeV, a splitting the group does not explain.

King's advantage is the light vector leptoquark, which the data prefer. The weakness is a second gauge group and a colour octet at $3.5$ TeV, plus six vector-like families.

So the two programmes trade a hierarchy problem for a proliferation problem, and neither is obviously better. That is worth stating plainly rather than presenting one as the successor of the other.

The second thing: the discipline of this paper is that every advantage is a consequence of the group, not an added mechanism. No proton decay because of $B-L$; chirality because of the $\mathbf4$; symmetric couplings because the $\mathbf{10}$ is symmetric. Three features, one origin. That is the strongest form of the argument for unification and the corpus should use it as the model case.
