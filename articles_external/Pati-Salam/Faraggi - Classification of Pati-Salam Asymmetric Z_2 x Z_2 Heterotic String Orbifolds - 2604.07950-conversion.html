What the Paper Does

It takes the symmetric Pati–Salam classification of the previous paper in this batch (2011.04113) and lets the Pati–Salam breaking vector act asymmetrically — differently on left- and right-moving internal degrees of freedom.

Two things fall out, and they are the reason to read the paper.

"The asymmetric orbifold action freezes geometrical moduli whilst inducing doublet–triplet splitting in the untwisted sector. Notably, this doublet–triplet splitting operates for any asymmetric action, including pure asymmetric shifts that preserve all geometric moduli, and is therefore independent of moduli stabilisation."

That is a strong claim. Doublet–triplet splitting is normally the hardest problem in any unification scheme, and here a choice of boundary conditions does it — and does it whether or not the moduli are frozen. The two benefits are not tied together.

"Classifying the breaking vector according to its twist action, we find six inequivalent classes of geometric moduli spaces characterised by 12, 8, 4 or 0 real untwisted moduli. Through combining these asymmetric twists with all compatible asymmetric shifts, 24 inequivalent cases are identified."

And a third result, about the landscape rather than about any one model:

"As the number of geometrical moduli decreases, the number of distinct partition functions for these vacua collapses to a small number, reflecting a pronounced degeneracy under GGSO phase variations."

Summary in the Authors' Words

Why Asymmetry

"In the free fermionic classifications performed to date have largely involved symmetric boundary conditions. In such constructions there are twelve real untwisted moduli of the $Z_2\times Z_2$ orbifold or, equivalently, the three complex structure and three Kähler moduli. In the fermionic language these moduli correspond to quartic Thirring interactions among the worldsheet fermions associated with the six internal coordinates."

"The subset of Thirring terms that are invariant is reduced by asymmetric boundary conditions, which project out the corresponding moduli fields."

"Asymmetric action ... is not possible in the effective field theory limits, that treat the internal compactified space as a classical geometry, and is intrinsically stringy, i.e. the internal geometry should be viewed as a quantum geometry. Another view of the asymmetric action is in terms of $T$-folds that mod out by duality symmetries of the internal toroidal space."

That paragraph is the justification for the whole programme. Moduli stabilisation in effective field theory works by writing a potential on a classical moduli space. Asymmetric boundary conditions act on the quantum geometry, so they are invisible to that approach and cannot be reproduced by it. The string does something the effective theory cannot express, which is the only kind of result that justifies doing string phenomenology at all.

The Moduli Arithmetic, Which Is Rigid

"In terms of moduli, we can determine the invariant Thirring terms by considering which of the six antiholomorphic currents $J_R^j=:\bar y^j\bar w^j:$ are invariant under $\alpha$. Any asymmetric assignment $\alpha(\bar y^j,\bar w^j)=(1,0)$ or $(0,1)$ generates a twist action and projects two Thirring terms. Due to modular invariance consistency we must have an even number of asymmetric twist assignments, so any additional vector can project $0\bmod 4$ Thirring terms."

"We observe that we can either freeze all 4 real (2 complex) moduli in one of the three tori, or freeze half the moduli in two tori."

The constraint is arithmetic and it is tight. Twelve real moduli, in three tori of four each. A twist on a torus removes two Thirring terms — but you need an even number of twists, so the removals come in units that cannot leave you with one torus half-frozen and another quarter-frozen. The only options are: freeze a whole torus ($4$ of $4$), or freeze half of two tori ($2+2$). Hence $12$, $8$ (one torus frozen), $4$ (two tori frozen, or two half-frozen), $0$ (all three frozen). No other counts are possible, which is why the classification has exactly six classes and not more.

The residual space when half a torus survives: "the residual coset moduli-space factor is $\frac{SO(2,1)}{SO(2)}\simeq\frac{SU(1,1)}{U(1)}$ ... The surviving modulus in this case is an $H$-type modulus." And "$H_j$ is a linear combination of $T_j$ and $U_j$ fixed by the asymmetric projection".

So a half-frozen torus does not leave the Kähler modulus or the complex structure modulus; it leaves a mixture of the two. That is a genuinely different object from anything in the symmetric case, and it is why the paper needs a new Kähler potential for each class,

$$ K=\sum_i\log(1-|T_i|^2)+\cdots $$

in Poincaré disk coordinates, "each surviving complex modulus contributes a factor $-\log(1-|M|^2)$".

The Six Classes

"we label them Classes 1a), 1b), 2a), 2b) and 3), where the integer $n$ in the class label encodes the number of internal tori fully frozen by the asymmetric twist, i.e. $n=3-N_M/4$ ... The sub-label a) or b) distinguishes geometrically inequivalent configurations that share the same value of $N_M$. In addition, the $\alpha$ case with no moduli freezing is dubbed Class 0), with $N_M=12$."

class $(M_1,M_2,M_3)$ real moduli
0 $(4,4,4)$ 12
1a $(4,4,0)$ 8
1b $(4,2,2)$ 8
2a $(4,0,0)$ or equivalent 4
2b $(2,2,0)$ 4
3 $(0,0,0)$ 0

The sub-label distinction is exactly the whole-torus-versus-half-torus choice: $1a$ freezes one torus completely, $1b$ freezes half of two. Both have $8$ moduli and they are not the same theory, because the residual spaces differ — $1a$ keeps two full $T,U$ pairs, $1b$ keeps one full pair and two $H$-type mixtures.

"Through combining these asymmetric twists with all compatible asymmetric shifts, 24 inequivalent cases are identified and characterised by their residual moduli content and internal Narain lattice."

Six twist classes times four shift options gives the twenty-four. The shifts are constrained: "Different asymmetric twist assignments lead to inequivalent residual moduli spaces and determine which Narain–like symmetric shift vectors are compatible with modular invariance."

The $\alpha$ Vector Does Three Jobs

"The asymmetric Pati–Salam vector serves a triple role: it breaks the $SO(10)$ GUT symmetry to the Pati–Salam subgroup, it freezes geometric moduli at the free fermionic point and it implements a doublet–triplet splitting mechanism in the untwisted (NS) sector."

One basis vector, three functions. That economy is the paper's structural insight and it is worth stating plainly, because in the symmetric case the same vector does only the first job and the other two have to be arranged separately.

Doublet–Triplet Splitting in the Untwisted Sector

The mechanism, in the authors' comparison:

"In symmetric models, all untwisted electroweak doublets are projected out while the corresponding colour triplets are retained. The Higgs doublets must therefore arise from twisted sectors, and the Yukawa couplings of the chiral generations originate from cubic interactions of the form $T_iT_jT_k$ with $i\ne j\ne k$."

"By contrast, asymmetric boundary conditions acting on one or more orbifold planes project the untwisted colour triplets while retaining the electroweak doublets. This gives rise to couplings of the form $T_iT_iU_i$ and suggests the possible existence of models in which vectorial twisted states are entirely projected out, in particular eliminating massless colour triplets from the spectrum."

Two consequences, and the second is the sharper one.

The first is the splitting itself: the doublet stays and the triplet goes, in the same multiplet, because the boundary conditions distinguish them. The corpus should note why this is possible at all: in $SU(4)$ the doublet sits in the bidoublet $(\mathbf1,\mathbf2,\mathbf2)$ and the triplet in the vectorial $(\mathbf6,\mathbf1,\mathbf1)$, so the two are in different representations and a projection can act on one and not the other. The splitting is possible because Pati–Salam already separates them into different multiplets — the hard part in $SU(5)$ was that they are in the same one.

The second is the change in Yukawa texture: $T_iT_jT_k$ with all indices distinct becomes $T_iT_iU_i$. That is an observable prediction about the flavour structure, and it is not a small perturbation — the symmetric case forbids a coupling of a generation to itself, and the asymmetric case allows exactly that and forbids the mixed ones. Any corpus discussion of Yukawa textures from string constructions needs this.

The selection rule is stated as Hamming weights on the left and right boundary-condition vectors:

$$ \text{triplets retained if }(\delta_{Lj},\delta_{Rj})\in\{(4,0),(0,4),(0,0),(1,1),(2,2),(3,3),(4,4)\}, $$

$$ \text{doublets retained if }(\delta_{Lj},\delta_{Rj})\in\{(4,2),(2,4),(3,1),(1,3),(2,0),(0,2)\}. $$

The two sets are disjoint, which is the splitting, and the pattern is left–right antisymmetric in each pair — the diagonal cases give triplets and the off-diagonal cases give doublets. Then

$$ N_T=\sum_{j=1}^{3}k_j,\qquad N_D=3-N_T . $$

So each of the three orbifold planes contributes either a triplet pair or a doublet, never both, and never neither. That is a clean accounting rule and it explains why the number of surviving doublets is $3-N_T$.

"The surviving untwisted doublets are candidates for the Standard Model Higgs. Whilst the triplets are more problematic, and can lead to proton decay channels, they can be utilised in the missing partner mechanism to give mass to exotic or twisted sector triplets."

Note the reversal: in the symmetric case the doublets are the problem (they have to be found) and the triplets are the nuisance. Here the doublets are handed to you and the triplets are the thing you want to be rid of.

The $E_6$ Obstruction

"In our $\alpha$ classification, the case $(k_1,k_2,k_3)=(0,0,0)$ is of particular interest since all triplets are projected. It is interesting to note that this case does not allow for the addition of the $x$ vector but is compatible with an $\tilde x$ vector ... Within the free fermionic formulation the addition of the $x$ vector is associated with the GUT enhancement $SO(10)\to E_6$. Therefore we find that only Pati–Salam models without an $E_6$ embedding can be used to project all three untwisted triplets."

That is a real tension and the paper states it as one. The complete removal of colour triplets from the untwisted sector requires giving up the $E_6$ enhancement of the gauge group, because the $x$ vector that generates $E_6$ also forces a triplet to survive. You cannot have both maximal proton-decay safety and the largest possible gauge symmetry.

And a second, weaker tension: "our analysis across the example model classes suggests that projecting all three untwisted triplets is incompatible with the presence of a Pati–Salam-breaking Heavy Higgs state from the spinorial sectors, pointing to a tension between maximal triplet projection and the mechanism for further gauge symmetry breaking to the Standard Model."

So the fully triplet-free model cannot be broken to the Standard Model, because the heavy Higgs that would do the breaking is unavailable. That is the same absence that killed the $\tilde S$ class in 2011.04113, reappearing here as a trade-off rather than an impossibility.

The Statistics

"Explicit phenomenological classifications have been carried out in four representative model classes with 12, 8, 4 and 0 geometric moduli, scanning samples of $10^9$ GGSO phase configurations in each case."

The nine criteria applied, in the paper's numbering: no on-shell tachyons; no observable enhancements; no hidden enhancements; complete generations ($n_g\ne0$ and $n_{4_L}-n_{\bar4_L}=n_{\bar4_R}-n_{4_R}$); three singly degenerate generations; presence of heavy Higgs ($n_{4_R}^H\ge1$); top quark mass coupling (automatic); no chiral exotics; no fermionic exotics.

Results by class:

  • "In the Class 0) (12 geometric moduli) case, exophobic models satisfying all criteria were found for both $N=1$ and $N=0$ vacua."
  • "the basis sets employed for the Class 1b) (8 moduli) and Class 2a) (4 moduli) examples both lacked phenomenological viable, SUSY vacua with a Pati–Salam breaking (Heavy) Higgs. This obstruction is not intrinsic to the moduli classes but rather reflects a limitation of the specific basis sets chosen."
  • "The Class 3) construction, in which composite vectors formed from sums of the simplest symmetric shifts ... yields phenomenologically viable models."

The middle bullet is the honest one: two of the four classes fail, and the authors say the failure is in their choice of basis, not in the class. They do not claim the classes are empty. That distinction matters and the corpus should keep it: a negative result from one basis set is not a negative result about a class.

The Degeneracy Collapse

This is the paper's most interesting finding about the landscape:

"In the Class 0) example, a sample of $10^9$ GGSO configurations yielded $138$ exophobic $N=0$ models with $75$ distinct partition functions (computed at the free fermionic point). Moving to Class 3), $10^4$ phenomenologically viable models collapsed to just $5$ distinct partition functions."

$$ \begin{array}{lcc} \text{Class 0 (12 moduli)} & 138\ \text{models} & \to\ 75\ \text{partition functions}\\ \text{Class 3 (0 moduli)} & 10^4\ \text{models} & \to\ 5\ \text{partition functions} \end{array} $$

Five distinct theories behind ten thousand models. The ratio falls from about $0.54$ to $0.0005$ — a factor of a thousand in the other direction as the moduli are removed.

"This dramatic reduction suggests that the landscape of asymmetric (free fermionic) string vacua has a qualitatively different structure from the symmetric counterpart: rather than the GGSO phases (and therefore discrete torsions) defining a large space of distinct configurations, the strong constraints imposed by asymmetric boundary conditions, channel the GGSO phase space into a small number of physically distinct theories."

And the caveat, stated properly: "we stress, however, that matching partition functions to a given order in the $q$-expansion does not establish full equivalence of the underlying string vacua, which would require a detailed comparison of all state $U(1)$ charges and couplings."

The caveat is the right one. Matching a $q$-expansion to finite order is strong evidence and not proof, and the authors say so. The corpus should carry the number with the qualification attached.

The Methodological Conclusion

"This collapse ... also has implications for the choice of computational search strategy. In symmetric constructions, where the space of viable vacua is large and diverse, machine learning approaches trained on GGSO phase matrices are well suited to recognising patterns and classifying the statistical features of the landscape. In the asymmetric case, however, the phenomenological constraints are sufficiently restrictive that the problem shifts from one of classification within a rich solution space to one of constraint satisfaction within a tightly constrained one. For such problems, targeted algorithms such as SAT/SMT solvers and genetic algorithms ... are likely better adapted than broad-sampling approaches."

That is a statement about how the physics dictates the algorithm, and it is unusual enough to record. The paper actually used Z3, the SMT solver, "to enumerate all modular invariant choices of $\alpha$". So a constraint solver did the classification and a statistics sample did the phenomenology, and the authors argue the balance should shift further toward the solver as the constraints tighten.

Known Models Placed in the Scheme

The classification is calibrated against existing models, which is a good sign:

  • "the notable Pati–Salam models of refs [77, 78] as belonging to Class 1b) and having no asymmetric shifts, such that $M=(2,2,4)$ and $N_{as}=(0,0,0)$."
  • "the notable Standard-like model of ref. [3], which is identified as Class 0) with $M=(4,4,4)$ and $N_{as}=(1,1,1)$."
  • "The Standard-like model of [5] can be identified as class 3), such that $M=(0,0,0)$ and $N_{as}=(0,0,0)$."

The best-known Pati–Salam string models sit at $8$ moduli, not $0$. So the previous generation of models is in the middle of the classification, and the fully frozen class is where the new work points.

Ideas Not Yet in the Corpus

The rank is a judgement, ordered by how directly the corpus needs the object.

1. Doublet–triplet splitting from boundary conditions, with no effective-field-theory counterpart

The idea. Asymmetric boundary conditions project the untwisted colour triplet and retain the untwisted doublet, so the splitting happens in the same orbifold plane without a Missing Partner or a fine-tuned coupling.

What it would add. A third mechanism for the corpus's doublet–triplet discussion. The usual two are the Missing Partner Mechanism (needs specific multiplets, see 2011.04113 in this batch) and fine tuning (needs a cancellation to many digits). This is neither: it is a choice of boundary conditions, and the splitting is exact because the projection is exact. And crucially it acts on the untwisted sector, so it costs nothing in twisted-sector states.

Where it lands. articles_physics/the-pati-salam-model-in-the-biquaternion-framework.md, in the doublet–triplet section, as the stringy mechanism alongside the field-theory ones.

What stands in the way. The mechanism requires a string construction; it cannot be written in a four-dimensional field theory. The corpus should say that, because it is the mechanism's whole virtue and its whole limitation.

2. The splitting survives even when no modulus is frozen

The idea. "this doublet–triplet splitting operates for any asymmetric action, including pure asymmetric shifts that preserve all geometric moduli, and is therefore independent of moduli stabilisation."

What it would add. A decoupling of two things that are usually bundled. Moduli stabilisation is the hardest unsolved problem in string phenomenology; if a phenomenological success depended on it, the success would be hostage to it. Here the splitting comes from the shift, which preserves the moduli, so a model with all twelve moduli unfrozen still splits. That means the result survives even if moduli stabilisation never gets solved.

Where it lands. The Pati–Salam article's string section, and any corpus discussion of moduli.

What stands in the way. Nothing. The statement is: the twist freezes moduli and the twist splits doublets from triplets, but a pure shift splits without freezing, so the two are independent.

3. The Yukawa texture changes from $T_iT_jT_k$ to $T_iT_iU_i$

The idea. Symmetric constructions allow only cubic couplings with all three generation indices distinct. Asymmetric ones allow a generation to couple to itself.

What it would add. A flavour prediction that distinguishes the two constructions, and it is the kind a corpus on unification should track. Symmetric models forbid the diagonal coupling, which means the third-generation mass has to come from a mixed coupling; asymmetric models allow $T_3T_3U_3$ directly. The top quark mass has a natural home in one case and not the other.

Where it lands. The Pati–Salam article's Yukawa section.

What stands in the way. The paper states the texture change as a consequence of the projection but does not compute the resulting mass matrices. The corpus should attribute the mechanism and not claim a fit.

4. Projecting all untwisted triplets forbids the $E_6$ enhancement

The idea. The $(k_1,k_2,k_3)=(0,0,0)$ case is incompatible with the $x$ vector, and the $x$ vector is what enhances $SO(10)\to E_6$.

What it would add. A trade-off stated as a group-theoretic incompatibility. The corpus's Pati–Salam article treats $E_6$ as an optional enhancement; here it is an obstruction to the cleanest proton-decay solution. Maximal safety and maximal symmetry are mutually exclusive, and the reason is a single basis vector.

Where it lands. The Pati–Salam article's string section, and the proton-decay section — where the triplet is the dangerous object.

What stands in the way. Nothing. The chain is: no triplets $\Rightarrow$ no $x$ vector $\Rightarrow$ no $E_6$.

5. Five distinct theories behind ten thousand models

The idea. $10^4$ phenomenologically viable Class 3 models have $5$ distinct partition functions; $138$ Class 0 models have $75$.

What it would add. A quantitative statement that the asymmetric landscape is small, not large. The corpus's landscape discussion, if it has one, treats the number of string vacua as astronomically large and the problem as choosing among them. Here the physical constraints collapse the count by three orders of magnitude, and the collapse strengthens as the construction gets more restrictive. That inverts the usual framing: the landscape problem becomes a constraint-satisfaction problem with few solutions.

Where it lands. The Pati–Salam article's string section, and any corpus discussion of the landscape.

What stands in the way. Five partition functions is not five theories, and the paper says so. The count is of $q$-expansions to finite order, which is evidence of degeneracy rather than proof of equivalence.

6. The moduli counts are forced, not chosen

The idea. Each asymmetric twist projects two Thirring terms, modular invariance requires an even number of twists, so the removals come in units of four and only $12,8,4,0$ are possible.

What it would add. A worked example of a discrete constraint producing an exhaustive list. The corpus's Pati–Salam article presents the breaking pattern as a choice among options; here the options are enumerated and closed, because the arithmetic permits nothing else. "These are all of them" is a stronger statement than "here are some", and the corpus should note when it has the former.

Where it lands. The Pati–Salam article's string section.

What stands in the way. Nothing. The arithmetic is four lines.

One observation about the corpus's map

This paper and 2011.04113 are consecutive entries in one programme and they answer different questions about the same object, so the corpus should hold them as a pair.

2011.04113 asks: which non-supersymmetric Pati–Salam vacua exist, and can they be broken? Answer: only the $S$-class, because the $\tilde S$-class lacks the heavy Higgs.

This paper asks: what does an asymmetric action do, and how many distinct vacua result? Answer: it splits doublets from triplets and freezes moduli, and the distinct vacua are far fewer than the configurations suggest.

They agree on the binding constraint. Both find that the heavy Higgs from the spinorial sector is the thing that decides viability, and this paper sharpens it: projecting all untwisted triplets is incompatible with that heavy Higgs. So the tension in 2011.04113 — no scalars, no breaking — appears here as a quantitative trade-off between triplet removal and symmetry breaking. The same obstruction, seen from two directions.

Worth recording: the best-known Pati–Salam string models in the literature sit at Class 1b with eight moduli, that is, in the middle of this classification rather than at the restricted end. The programme's new results are in a corner of the space that the established models do not occupy, which means the phenomenological successes of the older models are not evidence for the new corner, and vice versa.
