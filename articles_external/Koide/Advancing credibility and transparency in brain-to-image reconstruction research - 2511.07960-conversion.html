What the Paper Does

This paper is not about the Koide mass relation. It was retrieved by the arXiv search because the authors of the study it reanalyses are named Koide-Majima, Nishimoto and Majima. It is a methodological critique in computational neuroscience: it argues that a 2024 Neural Networks paper claiming to reconstruct visual images from brain activity suffers from selective reporting of the best examples, circular evaluation metrics, baseline comparisons that remove the claimed advantage, an inert "Bayesian" sampling step, and unsubstantiated claims of Bayesian novelty. Its subject matter is brain decoding, not particle masses.

What Still Might Be Worth Keeping

The idea. A method: judge a claimed result by (1) whether examples were selected after the fact, (2) whether the metric can be satisfied without the claimed ability, (3) whether a fair baseline removes the advantage, and (4) whether a component actually does anything.

What it would add. This is exactly the checklist the Koide literature needs, and the folder would be stronger if applied to it uniformly. Several conversions state the same worry in different words — Hübner's look-elsewhere rank, Rivero's "it is really not common", Liang–Sun's two free parameters per sector — and none of them has a fixed procedure. The four questions above are such a procedure, and they transfer without modification: a selective mass tuple, a metric that any triple satisfies, a baseline that is another tuple with an extra parameter, and a component (a phase, a potential term) that does nothing.

Where it lands. Nowhere in the physics articles; at most as a note in articles_external/Koide/ if the folder ever gains a review document. Recorded here so that the paper is not searched for again.

What stands in the way. It is not a physics paper, and importing its methods would be a borrowing of analogy rather than of results. The corpus should not cite it.
