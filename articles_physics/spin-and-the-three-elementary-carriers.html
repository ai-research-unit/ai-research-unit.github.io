Spin and the Three Elementary Carriers

Introduction

The biquaternion algebra $\mathbb{B}$ has three elementary carrier types, represented by four spaces, and their spins are $0$, $\tfrac12$ and $1$. The centre $\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{B}}=\mathbb{C}e_t$ carries $0$ under the adjoint action; each Peirce plane $S_\pm=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_\pm$ carries $\tfrac12$ under left multiplication; the traceless part $V=\ker\operatorname{tr}$ carries $1$ under the adjoint action. There is no fourth elementary value. Every finite-dimensional associative module of $\mathbb{B}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$ is a direct sum of copies of the two-dimensional simple module, so the left action sees only $\tfrac12$; the adjoint action adds the trivial centre and the three-dimensional traceless part, so it sees $0$ and $1$.

These are also the spins of every observed elementary particle. The Higgs boson is $0$; the six quarks and the six leptons are $\tfrac12$; the photon, the $W^\pm$, the $Z$ and the eight gluons are $1$. No observed elementary particle carries another spin. The graviton of spin $2$ is not an observed particle, and the hadrons of spin $\tfrac32$, $2$ and higher are bound states. The match is a match of labels: the algebra supplies the set, and it does not select which particle carries which spin.

The spin-$\tfrac12$ carrier is also the place where the algebra offers two copies. The plain product splits it into two minimal left ideals,

$$ \mathbb{B}=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+\oplus\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-, \qquad \tilde\Pi_+=\tfrac12(e_t+i\vec e_z), \qquad \tilde\Pi_-=e_t-\tilde\Pi_+=\tfrac12(e_t-i\vec e_z), $$

with

$$ S=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_y\}, \qquad \mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_-,\vec\Upsilon_x\}. $$

The two planes are the two state spaces of the spin-$\tfrac12$ carrier. They are distinct subspaces, module-isomorphic, metrically identical and Hilbert-orthogonal, and the copy-flip coupling is the pair of right multiplications by the two nilpotents that carries one onto the other and back. That coupling is not the chiral mass, which pairs $S$ with the conjugate module $S^{\mathrm{c}}$. The doubling is a copy of one carrier, not a second particle.

The article has two theses. First, the three carrier types are the elementary list of $\mathbb{B}$, and the observed elementary spins are exactly that list. Second, within the spin-$\tfrac12$ carrier the two copies are not two views of one object: the module structure belongs to the plain bilinear product, the Hilbert structure to the plain sesquilinear product, and the complement to right multiplication. The sharpest evidence for the separation is that the algebra's own bilinear form is degenerate on the carrier, with Gram matrix $\operatorname{diag}(\tfrac12,0)$. The module has no norm of its own, and the metric is added by the second slot.

Why There Is No Fourth

Here elementary means an isomorphism class of nonzero irreducible invariant subspaces of $\mathbb{B}$ under one of the two actions, left multiplication or the commutator. Two subspaces define the same carrier when the actions are isomorphic. A general Lie-algebra module is not of this form. The left action has one irreducible type: every minimal left ideal is isomorphic to $S$, and $S_+$ and $S_-$ are two representatives. The adjoint action has two: the centre $\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{B}}$ and the traceless part $V$. The algebra itself is reducible under both.

The algebra is $\mathbb{B}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$, and $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ is simple. It has one isomorphism class of simple modules, of complex dimension two, and every finite-dimensional left module is a direct sum of copies of it. On that module the spin element $\tilde S_{\vec u}=\tilde\Xi/2$ is a traceless $2\times2$ matrix with $(2\tilde S_{\vec u})^2=1$; its weights are $\pm\tfrac12$ and $j=\tfrac12$. The associative left action therefore sees only $j=\tfrac12$. A unital one-dimensional module would be a unital algebra map $M_2(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{C}$; its kernel is a two-sided ideal, so by simplicity it is $0$ or the whole algebra, and neither is possible. Hence no one-dimensional module exists, and the left action has no $j=0$.

The adjoint action is a Lie action. As a Lie algebra, $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}e_t\oplus V$ with $V\cong\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$. The centre is trivial, so $j=0$; the traceless part is the three-dimensional irreducible module, with weights $+1,0,-1$ under $\mathrm{ad}_{\tilde S_{\vec u}}$, so $j=1$. There is no other irreducible invariant subspace.

The two constructions together give the elementary list $$ \{0,\tfrac12,1\}, $$ and no other value occurs on $\mathbb{B}$ alone. Higher spin is not forbidden. It requires a tensor product or a larger algebra, and it is the subject of Higher Spin from Tensor Products; the spin-$2$ graviton, for instance, is the traceless symmetric part of the square of $V$.

The Observed Elementary Spins

The three elementary carrier spins are the spins of the established elementary particles.

$1$ photon $\gamma$; $W^+,W^-,Z$; eight gluons $g$ traceless part $V$

The quarks and the gluons are established elementary particles, although they are observed only as confined constituents of hadrons and jets, never as free particles; the table counts them by flavour and by colour.

No observed elementary particle carries another spin. Four boundaries keep the table from overclaiming.

  • The graviton is not observed. Spin $2$ is the quantum of the metric deviation, constructed from the symmetric traceless square of $V$. It belongs to the tensor construction, not to $\mathbb{B}$ alone.
  • Hadrons are composites. The $\Delta(1232)$ and the $\Omega^-$ have spin $\tfrac32$, the tensor mesons have spin $2$, and the $N(1680)$ has spin $\tfrac52$; all are bound states of quarks and gluons, not elementary particles.
  • The match is not a prediction. The Standard Model assigns the spins by hand. The algebra reproduces the set $\{0,\tfrac12,1\}$ and says nothing about which particle carries which spin, nor about masses, generations, charges or colours.
  • The spin is the rotation spin. The number $j$ is the highest weight of the spin element $\tilde S_{\vec u}=\tilde\Xi/2$ on the carrier, the rotation generator for $\mathrm{Spin}(3)\subset\mathbb B^{\times}$; the full Lorentz label is $(j_L,j_R)$, and the massless case uses helicity. The table uses the rotation spin, which is the standard particle-data label.

Two further physical qualifications belong to the carrier, not to the table.

  • The massless spin-$1$ count is two. $V$ has three states. For a massless field the longitudinal state is removed by gauge invariance, leaving the two helicities of the photon and the gluon. The algebra supplies the triplet; the massless restriction is a separate input.
  • The weak chirality is not the Peirce index. $S_+$ and $S_-$ are isomorphic as left modules, so the left action does not distinguish them. The chirality of the weak interaction is the Lorentz chirality, not the copy index.

The Carrier and Its Forced Basis

The two idempotents $\tilde\Pi_+$ and $\tilde\Pi_-$ are orthogonal and complete in the plain product, and the Peirce decomposition cuts the algebra into four one-dimensional complex corners:

$$ \tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_+,\quad \tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-=\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_x,\quad \tilde\Pi_-\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+=\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_y,\quad \tilde\Pi_-\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_-. $$

The spinor space is the Peirce direct sum of a diagonal corner and an off-diagonal one:

$$ S=\tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+\oplus\tilde\Pi_-\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+ =\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_+\oplus\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_y . $$

The basis is therefore not a choice. The carrier is one column of the matrix model, and in that model it is the minimal left ideal; a column of a rank-two matrix algebra is one diagonal corner plus one off-diagonal corner. The diagonal corner contributes the idempotent $\tilde\Pi_+$; the off-diagonal corner contributes a nilpotent, $\vec\Upsilon_y$. Every element of an off-diagonal corner is square-zero, because $(fae)(fae)=fa(ef)ae=0$ for orthogonal idempotents $e,f$. In rank $n$ the same count gives one idempotent and $n-1$ nilpotents; the spinor space is the case $n=2$.

The basis of the spinor space is one idempotent and one nilpotent, and the nilpotency is structural. It is not a property of the particular column, and it is not a choice of coordinates.

The physical reading of the basis is the first statement of the article. The idempotent is the state direction of the column, and the nilpotent is the shift direction, the off-diagonal element that raises or lowers the column index. The two directions are what a two-level carrier needs: one projection and one shift. The algebra supplies both, and it supplies them in that proportion.

The Module Structure: Composition

The plain product makes $S$ a left $\mathbb{B}$-module. On the basis $(\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_y)$ the left multiplications by the four basis elements are

$$ L_{\tilde\Pi_+}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad L_{\tilde\Pi_-}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad L_{\vec\Upsilon_x}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad L_{\vec\Upsilon_y}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, $$

the matrices of $\mathsf M_2(\mathbb{C})$ in the model $\mathsf M_2(\vec e_k)=-i\sigma_k$. The module is literally the column space $\mathbb{C}^2$ on which the algebra acts by matrix multiplication.

Three module-theoretic facts carry the reading.

  • The module is simple and unique. $\mathbb{B}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$ is simple and finite-dimensional, so its modules are completely reducible and there is, up to isomorphism, exactly one simple module. By Schur's lemma $\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{B}}(S)=\mathbb{C}$.
  • The algebra is the endomorphism algebra. $\mathbb{B}\cong\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(S)$, and the left regular module splits as $\mathbb{B}\cong S\oplus S$. The algebra is the algebra of all linear maps of the carrier, and the regular module is two copies of it.
  • The Lorentz action is the left action. A unit-norm biquaternion $\tilde U$, $\tilde U\tilde U^{\natural}=e_t$, acts by $\psi\mapsto\tilde U\psi$, and the action is the defining representation of $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$.

The third fact is the physical content of the module structure. The action is faithful, and it does not descend to the Lorentz group: $-e_t$ acts as $-\mathrm{id}$ on $S$ while it acts trivially by conjugation on the four-vectors of $\mathbb{M}_-$. The double cover therefore appears on the module side and not on the four-vector side, and spin-$\tfrac12$ is a module datum. It is a property of how the algebra acts on the carrier, not of a form on the carrier.

One caution belongs here, because it is easy to state wrongly. A single minimal left ideal is one Peirce copy, not two. The ideals $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+$ and $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$ are both isomorphic to $S$ and carry the same defining representation; they differ by the Peirce idempotent, not by the Lorentz chirality. The two Lorentz chiralities are the defining representation and its conjugate, $S$ and $S^{\mathrm{c}}$, which are non-isomorphic as complex representations. The doubling below treats the second ideal; this section treats one carrier and its two structures.

The Probability Structure: the Second Slot

The second structure uses the plain sesquilinear product. Its scalar part is the Hermitian form

$$ \langle\tilde Q_1,\tilde Q_2\rangle_{*}=\operatorname{Sc}(\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2^{*}), $$

which is positive definite on the algebra. Restricted to the carrier, its Gram matrix on the basis $(\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_y)$ is

$$ \boxed{\;G=\begin{pmatrix}\langle\tilde\Pi_+,\tilde\Pi_+\rangle_{*} & \langle\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_y\rangle_{*}\\[2pt]\langle\vec\Upsilon_y,\tilde\Pi_+\rangle_{*} & \langle\vec\Upsilon_y,\vec\Upsilon_y\rangle_{*}\end{pmatrix} =\tfrac12 I_2\;}. $$

The carrier is therefore a two-dimensional Hilbert space with the standard metric, and the two basis spinors are orthogonal and of equal norm. The form is invariant under the $*$-unitary slice $\mathrm{U}(2)=\{\tilde U:\tilde U\tilde U^{*}=e_t\}$; inside $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$ the invariance is the compact subgroup $SU(2)$, and a generic phase $e^{i\theta}e_t$ is an isometry of the Hilbert structure that lies outside $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$. The Hilbert form is therefore invariant under a group that is not the Lorentz group, and the extra factor is the central phase. In module coordinates the same form is the spinor inner product $h(\psi,\phi)=\tfrac12\psi^{\dagger}\phi$, with the basis spinors of norm $1/\sqrt2$. The Born pairing is the trace formula

$$ \operatorname{Tr}(\tilde\Pi\tilde H^{*})=2\operatorname{Sc}(\tilde\Pi\tilde H), $$

which pairs a state idempotent with a Hermitian observable, the star being inert on $\mathbb{M}_+$. The normalisation is a condition on the Hilbert structure alone: $\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{*}=1$ for a state vector, and $\langle\tilde\Pi,e_t\rangle_{*}=\tfrac12$ for the corresponding idempotent.

The physical reading of the second structure is the one the informational sector has built. A state is a Hilbert vector, an observable is a Hermitian element, and the pairing is the probability. The ray, not the vector, is the state: $\psi$ and $e^{i\alpha}\psi$ define the same ray and the same probabilities, and the Hilbert form is blind to the phase.

The sharpest way to see that this is a second structure and not a second view is the behaviour of the algebra's own form on the same carrier. The symmetric bilinear form

$$ \langle\tilde Q_1,\tilde Q_2\rangle=\operatorname{Sc}(\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2)=Q_{1t}Q_{2t}-(\vec Q_1,\vec Q_2) $$

is non-degenerate on $\mathbb{B}$, of signature $(4,4)$ on the real space. On the carrier its Gram matrix is

$$ \boxed{\;B_S=\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac12&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\;}, $$

of rank one, with radical $\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_y$. The nilpotent is orthogonal to both basis spinors and to every element of $S$: $\vec\Upsilon_y\tilde\Pi_+=\vec\Upsilon_y$ has zero scalar part, and $\vec\Upsilon_y^2=0$. The algebra's own form cannot separate the two basis spinors and cannot produce a norm on the carrier. Positivity is not a property of the plain product at all; it needs the second slot.

The Second Column

The complement is a left ideal for the same reason $S$ is. The Peirce decomposition with respect to the pair $(\tilde\Pi_+,\tilde\Pi_-)$ cuts the algebra into four one-dimensional complex corners, and the second column is the sum of its diagonal and off-diagonal corners:

$$ \boxed{\;\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-= \tilde\Pi_-\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-\oplus\tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_- =\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_-\oplus\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_x\;}. $$

In the matrix model $\mathsf M_2(\vec e_k)=-i\sigma_k$ the four elements are the matrix units

$$ \tilde\Pi_+\leftrightarrow E_{11},\quad \vec\Upsilon_y\leftrightarrow E_{21},\quad \vec\Upsilon_x\leftrightarrow E_{12},\quad \tilde\Pi_-\leftrightarrow E_{22}, $$

so $S$ is the first column and $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$ is the second. The basis of the complement is again one idempotent and one nilpotent, and the nilpotent is again structural: it is the off-diagonal corner.

The left action on the complement's own basis is the mirror of the action on $S$:

$$ L_{\tilde\Pi_+}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad L_{\tilde\Pi_-}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad L_{\vec\Upsilon_x}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad L_{\vec\Upsilon_y}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}. $$

The pattern is the interchange $\tilde\Pi_+\leftrightarrow\tilde\Pi_-$ and $\vec\Upsilon_x\leftrightarrow\vec\Upsilon_y$, but that interchange is a statement about the two left actions and not a map between the modules. The next section supplies the actual map.

The two columns have a second reading, in which they are the two levels of one mode. Writing the matrix units as occupation idempotents,

$$ \tilde\Pi_+=|0\rangle\langle0|,\qquad \tilde\Pi_-=|1\rangle\langle1|,\qquad \vec\Upsilon_x=|0\rangle\langle1|,\qquad \vec\Upsilon_y=|1\rangle\langle0|, $$

the spinor space is the ideal of the level $|0\rangle$ and the complement is the ideal of the level $|1\rangle$. The nilpotents that exchange the two are the raising and lowering operators of the mode, and their nilpotency is the algebraic form of the statement that two raises erase the state. The occupation reading is that the doubling is the ground and the excited ideal, or the empty and the occupied ideal, of a single fermionic mode.

The reading is one of several and not a decision. The same map that reads as a copy flip reads as a level shift, and the two vocabularies are different readings of one algebraic fact: the copy-flip section uses the column, the occupation reading uses the level, and neither is derived from the other.

The Isomorphism Is a Right Multiplication

The module isomorphism $S\to\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$ is right multiplication by the nilpotent:

$$ \tilde\Pi_+\vec\Upsilon_x=\vec\Upsilon_x,\qquad \vec\Upsilon_y\vec\Upsilon_x=\tilde\Pi_-, \qquad S\vec\Upsilon_x=\mathbb{C}\{\vec\Upsilon_x,\tilde\Pi_-\}=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_- . $$

It is a module map because it acts on the right and the algebra acts on the left: $\tilde Q(x\vec\Upsilon_x)=(\tilde Qx)\vec\Upsilon_x$. It is nonzero between simple modules of the same dimension, so it is an isomorphism. Symmetrically, right multiplication by $\vec\Upsilon_y$ returns:

$$ \boxed{\;\tilde\Pi_-\vec\Upsilon_y=\vec\Upsilon_y,\qquad \vec\Upsilon_x\vec\Upsilon_y=\tilde\Pi_+,\qquad (\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-)\vec\Upsilon_y=S\;}. $$

The naive basis swap is not the map. The relabelling $\tilde\Pi_+\leftrightarrow\tilde\Pi_-$, $\vec\Upsilon_y\leftrightarrow\vec\Upsilon_x$ does not commute with the left action: $\tilde\Pi_-\tilde\Pi_+=0$ while $\tilde\Pi_-\tilde\Pi_-=\tilde\Pi_-$. The isomorphism must mix the two idempotents, and right multiplication by the nilpotent is exactly such a mixing.

The complement is reached by multiplying on the right by a nilpotent, not by relabelling the basis.

Two properties of the map are worth naming. It is $\mathbb{C}$-linear and $\mathbb{B}$-linear on the left, so it preserves the module structure and the Lorentz action. It is not invertible as an element of the algebra, because $\vec\Upsilon_x^2=0$ and a nilpotent is not a unit; its inverse between the modules is the right multiplication by $\vec\Upsilon_y$, and the two maps compose to the identity on each side. So the element is not invertible while the map is an isomorphism: the inverse is another right multiplication, not an inverse element.

That asymmetry is the algebraic form of a physical statement. The complement is a copy, and the operation that reaches it is a nilpotent: a step whose generator squares to zero. The next section separates that step from the chiral mass.

The Mass Coupling

The coupling supplied by the two ideals is a copy flip, not the chiral mass. The corpus once called it the chirality-off-diagonal mass; that name conflated the Peirce index with the Lorentz chirality. The pair of right multiplications by $\vec\Upsilon_x$ and $\vec\Upsilon_y$ moves a state from $S$ to its complement and back, and the reading rests on three algebraic facts.

  • The copy flip is off-diagonal. A coupling that is $\mathbb{B}$-linear on the left cannot be a left multiplication by an element of $\mathbb{B}$: the left action preserves each ideal, since the ideals are left ideals. Such a coupling is a right multiplication, and right multiplication is what the module theory supplies.
  • The generator is null, the map is an isometry. The natural generator is the matrix unit $\vec\Upsilon_x$: it has vanishing norm and $\vec\Upsilon_x^2=0$, so it is not a unit of the algebra. The map it induces is nevertheless an isometry of the two Hilbert structures, because $\vec\Upsilon_x\vec\Upsilon_x^{*}=\tilde\Pi_+$ and $\xi\tilde\Pi_+=\xi$ for every $\xi\in S$: the idempotent is a right identity on the carrier. The nilpotence is a property of the chosen generator and not of the map: the representatives that induce the same map differ by an element of $\operatorname{Ann}(S)=\mathbb{C}\{\vec\Upsilon_y,\tilde\Pi_-\}$, and some of them are units. The inverse between the modules is the right multiplication by $\vec\Upsilon_y$.
  • The copy flip changes the column. It sends $\tilde\Pi_+\mapsto\vec\Upsilon_x$ and $\vec\Upsilon_y\mapsto\tilde\Pi_-$, so the column index changes. That index is the Peirce index, not a chirality: the two ideals are isomorphic, and a chirality must separate inequivalent halves. In the spectral-triple reading the finite Dirac operator is off-diagonal between $S$ and $S^{\mathrm{c}}$, not between the two Peirce copies.

The copy flip is the right multiplication that takes a state to the other Peirce copy. It is not the mass.

The reading has a boundary that must be stated in the same breath. The article gives the algebraic mechanism of the copy flip and no mass value and no mass spectrum. The number belongs to the dynamical articles, where the mass is a parameter of the equation and not a property of the doubling. What the algebra supplies is the shape of the copy flip: off-diagonal, column-changing, generated by a nilpotent matrix unit. What it does not supply is its size.

The chiral mass is a different coupling. It is the invariant sesquilinear pairing $\beta:S\times S^{\mathrm{c}}\to\mathbb{C}$ of Chirality: The Conjugate Pair, off-diagonal in the grading $\chi=ij$ and odd under it. It is not $\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}}$-linear, so it is not a right multiplication and not an element of the algebra; it exists because $S$ and $S^{\mathrm{c}}$ are real-isomorphic and complex-inequivalent. A biquaternion is a section of $S\oplus S$ and carries no chirality; the Dirac field is the pair in $\Delta=S\oplus S^{\mathrm{c}}$. The two couplings must not be identified: the copy flip is $\mathbb{B}$-linear and moves between isomorphic copies, while the chiral mass is not $\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}}$-linear and moves between inequivalent halves.

A convention warning belongs here, because the corpus uses the word chirality in two ways. The two ideals are sometimes called the two chiralities — more precisely, the two Peirce copies — and the two Lorentz chiralities are the defining representation and its conjugate, $S$ and $S^{\mathrm{c}}$. These are different objects. The ideals are isomorphic as modules; $S$ and $S^{\mathrm{c}}$ are not isomorphic as complex representations. The copy flip of this section moves between the two ideals, and it is the column flip of the first convention. It is not the passage to the conjugate representation, and it is not the chiral mass.

Three Complements

The word complement names three subspaces in this algebra, and only two of them coincide.

complement definition subspace dimension over $\mathbb{C}$
module complement the other Peirce summand $\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_-,\vec\Upsilon_x\}$ $2$
Hilbert complement $S^{\perp}$ for the positive definite form $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{*}$ $\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_-,\vec\Upsilon_x\}$ $2$
$\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$-orthogonal complement $S^{\perp_B}$ for the algebra's own form $\mathbb{C}\{\vec\Upsilon_y,\tilde\Pi_-\}$ $2$

The module complement is the second ideal. The Hilbert complement is the orthogonal complement for the sesquilinear form of the second slot, $S^{\perp}=\{v:\langle u,v\rangle_{*}=0\ \forall u\in S\}$, and it is computed on the matrix units: the four units are mutually orthogonal of norm $\tfrac12$, so the orthogonal complement of the first column is the second column. The coincidence is exact, and it is the algebraic core of the doubling:

$$ \mathbb{B}=S\oplus\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-=S\perp\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-, $$

a direct sum of left modules and an orthogonal direct sum of Hilbert spaces at once.

The coincidence is not automatic. It holds because $\tilde\Pi_+$ is Hermitian. The Peirce complement of a Hermitian idempotent is Hermitian, so $\tilde\Pi_-$ is Hermitian, and the two columns are orthogonal for the positive definite form. For a general idempotent $\tilde\Pi=\tfrac12(e_t+\tilde\Xi)$, whose root $\tilde\Xi$ need not be Hermitian, the two summands are still complementary left modules, but the Hilbert complement need not coincide with the module complement: the cross pairing $\langle\tilde\Pi,e_t-\tilde\Pi\rangle_{*}$ can be nonzero. The identification of the two complements is therefore a property of the vacuum pair, not of the algebra's decomposition alone.

The algebra's-own-form complement is a third subspace. Because the symmetric form $\langle\tilde Q_1,\tilde Q_2\rangle=\operatorname{Sc}(\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2)$ is degenerate on the carrier, with $B_S=\operatorname{diag}(\tfrac12,0)$ and radical $\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_y$, the orthogonal complement of $S$ is

$$ \boxed{\;S^{\perp_B}=\mathbb{C}\{\vec\Upsilon_y,\tilde\Pi_-\}\;}, $$

which shares the idempotent $\tilde\Pi_-$ with the module complement and differs in its nilpotent. Equivalently,

$$ \langle\vec\Upsilon_y,\vec\Upsilon_x\rangle=\tfrac12, \qquad S\cap S^{\perp_B}=\operatorname{rad}B_S=\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_y, \qquad S+S^{\perp_B}=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_y,\tilde\Pi_-\}, $$

a sum of dimension three. The nilpotent direction is exactly what the algebra's own form cannot see, and the module decomposition is not orthogonal for it.

The module and Hilbert complements coincide; the algebra's-own-form complement does not.

The three-way table is the sharpest statement of the article about the word complement. The first two are the doubling the physics uses: two identical copies, module-isomorphic and metrically orthogonal. The third is a different subspace, and it is a warning that the algebra's bilinear form is not the metric.

What the Complement Is Not

Three negative statements keep the reading exact.

  • It is not the dual. The dual of $S$ is the right ideal $S^{*}=\tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_x\}$, the first row of the matrix model, reached by the Hermitian conjugation ${}^{*}$. The complement is a left ideal reached by right multiplication. A bra is not an antiparticle.
  • It is not a second representation. The complement is isomorphic to $S$, so it carries the same defining representation of the double cover. The two Lorentz chiralities are the defining representation and its conjugate, $S$ and $S^{\mathrm{c}}$, which are non-isomorphic as complex representations. The doubling is a copy, not a second representation.
  • It is not a second sector. Each ideal meets each sector in exactly one real dimension: $S\cap\mathbb{M}_+=\mathbb{R}\{\tilde\Pi_+\}$, $S\cap\mathbb{M}_-=\mathbb{R}\{i\tilde\Pi_+\}$, and similarly for the complement. Neither ideal is material or informational, and the sector split cuts across both. There is, in particular, no nonzero idempotent in $\mathbb{M}_-$, so the complement cannot be the material sector.

The independence is sharper than the dimension count. Multiplication by the central imaginary exchanges the sectors, $i\mathbb{M}_+=\mathbb{M}_-$, so a complex subspace contained in one sector is zero; the two ideals are complex two-dimensional and can never be split along $\mathbb{M}_\pm$. The sector content of an ideal sits entirely on its diagonal corner: $\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_+\cap\mathbb{M}_+=\mathbb{R}\{\tilde\Pi_+\}$ and $\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_+\cap\mathbb{M}_-=\mathbb{R}\{i\tilde\Pi_+\}$, while the nilpotent line $\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_y$ meets both sectors in $\{0\}$. The sector label therefore attaches to the idempotent and not to the ladder: the doubling is an informational doubling, two idempotents of $\mathbb{M}_+$, and not a material–informational pair.

The map that does carry $S$ onto the complement at the level of the ring is the coefficient conjugation $\bar{\cdot}$, which fixes the four basis elements and negates $i$:

$$ \overline{\tilde\Pi_+}=\tilde\Pi_-,\qquad \overline{\vec\Upsilon_y}=-\vec\Upsilon_x,\qquad \bar S=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_- . $$

It is anti-linear and involutive; it is not the algebra's real structure $\flat$, whose fixed space is $\mathbb{M}_-$, and it is not multiplication by an element of the algebra. The complement is therefore reached by two different maps: the $\mathbb{B}$-linear right multiplication by the nilpotent, and the anti-linear coefficient conjugation. The first is the module isomorphism; the second is the ring-level conjugation. They agree on the subspace and differ in what they preserve.

What the Structures Do Not Carry

Three separations keep the article honest.

  • No positivity. The algebra's form is indefinite on the real space and degenerate on $S$. The module has no canonical normalised state.
  • No Born rule. The probability pairing is the scalar part of the sesquilinear product, not of the plain product. It belongs to the second structure.
  • No pairing of the carrier by the product. The sesquilinear products do not preserve $S$: they couple a state to a conjugate slot and land outside the ideal. A sesquilinear product pairs the space; it does not act on it.

A fourth separation is about the sectors. The carrier meets each of $\mathbb{M}_\pm$ in one real dimension, so the spinor space is not a sector, and its two structures are not the material–informational split. The sector split is a third structure, real and independent, and it cuts across the ideal.

The doubling adds its own separations:

  • No mass value and no spectrum. The copy-flip coupling is a shape; the number is dynamical.
  • No choice between the readings of the doubling. The second Peirce copy, the occupation ladder and the conjugate state space are readings of one algebraic fact.
  • The coincidence is conditional. The module and Hilbert complements coincide for the Hermitian vacuum pair; for a general idempotent they separate.
  • One pair of ideals only. The general idempotent $\tilde\Pi(\vec u)=\tfrac12(e_t+i\vec u)$ generates a minimal left ideal for every unit vector, and its complement is a different subspace. The article treats the coordinate pair.
  • No dynamics. Nothing here says how a state moves between the copies in time, or why the chiral mass has the value it has.

The Two Structures Side by Side

job structure the algebra's part the physical carrier
composition module plain left multiplication the Lorentz action and the double cover
probability Hilbert space the plain sesquilinear pairing the norm, the normalisation and the Born rule

The module structure makes the carrier an arena: the algebra is the algebra of operators and the carrier is what they act on. The Hilbert structure makes the arena a probability space: the form is what can be evaluated on a state and squared into a number. Neither structure alone gives the pair. A module without a form has no numbers; a form without an action has no observables.

The two structures separate the two physical data of a spinor.

  • The spin is read on the module. The double cover acts faithfully on the module, $-e_t$ acts as $-\mathrm{id}$, and that is where spin-$\tfrac12$ lives.
  • The ray is a Hilbert datum. The form is blind to the sign and to the phase, so the state is a ray and not a vector.
  • The vacuum is a module datum. The choice of idempotent $\tilde\Pi_+$ selects the module, and the module is fixed only up to the equivalence of idempotents.
  • The normalisation is a Hilbert datum. $\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{*}=1$ is a condition on the form and makes the amplitudes into probabilities.

The state space is normalised by the metric and selected by the vacuum, and the two acts are of different kinds: one is a pairing, the other is a choice of generator. The two structures also meet the corpus's word quantisation. The insertion of the star into the second slot, the step from $\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2$ to $\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2^{*}$, is what turns the composition pairing into the probability pairing. Read on the carrier, the spinor space is compositional before the step and probabilistic after it. The step is not an added axiom; it is the choice of what to put in the second slot.

Physical Readings

  • Three carrier types, three spins. The centre, the Peirce plane and the traceless part carry $0$, $\tfrac12$ and $1$. Boundary: the classification is by carrier and action; it does not assign a particle to a carrier.
  • The elementary list is closed on $\mathbb B$ alone. The elementary carriers are the isomorphism classes of nonzero irreducible invariant subspaces under left multiplication or the commutator; the associative ones give only $\tfrac12$, the adjoint ones give $0$ and $1$, and there is no fourth value. Boundary: closure is for $\mathbb B$ alone; tensor products give higher spin.
  • The elementary list matches the observed list. The spins $0$, $\tfrac12$ and $1$ are the spins of all observed elementary particles. Boundary: a label match, not a derivation of the particle content.
  • The two planes are one carrier type. $S_+$ and $S_-$ are module-isomorphic copies of the same spin-$\tfrac12$ carrier. Boundary: two copies, not two particles.
  • One carrier, two structures, two jobs. The state space is not one structure but two: the composition structure that makes the algebra act, and the probability structure that makes the states pair. Boundary: a statement about the algebraic structures, and it adds no identity between composition and probability.
  • The module is composition. The plain product composes two elements, and the module is that composition read with one factor held fixed. Boundary: an interpretation of the algebra's law, not a dynamics.
  • The spin is read on the module; the ray is a Hilbert datum. The double cover acts faithfully on the module, while the Hilbert structure is blind to the phase and yields the ray. Boundary: a separation of two known facts by the two structures.
  • The observable algebra is the endomorphism algebra. $\mathbb{B}\cong\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(S)$, so the observables are the Hermitian elements of the endomorphism algebra of the carrier and the trace formula is the pairing of the two structures. Boundary: the identification of the spinor and the qubit is not made.
  • The carrier has no norm of its own. The algebra's form is degenerate on $S$, so an unnormalised spinor is what the module gives, and normalisation is added by the metric. Boundary: a statement about the two forms.
  • The Born rule is a product, not a postulate. The probability pairing is the scalar part of the plain sesquilinear product, so positivity is the definiteness of a form and the trace formula is the scalar pairing. Boundary: the derivation and its bounds are The Born Rule from the Hermitian Form.
  • The spinor and the qubit are one carrier read twice. The Weyl spinor of the Lorentz action and the two-level state of the informational sector inhabit one complex plane, once as the defining module and once as the Hilbert space. Boundary: the carrier is one; the physics of the two readings is not thereby identified.
  • Quantisation is the second slot. The step from the plain product to the plain sesquilinear product is exactly the step from the module structure to the Hilbert structure. Boundary: the algebra supplies the pairing and no equation of motion and no measure.
  • The state space is doubled. The algebra offers two copies of the same state space, module-isomorphic and Hilbert-orthogonal. Boundary: a counting statement; it adds no second particle.
  • The copy flip takes a state to its complement. It is the right multiplication by the nilpotent $\vec\Upsilon_x$, which carries $S$ onto $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$ and changes the column. Boundary: it is not the chiral mass; the mechanism is algebraic, and the mass value and the spectrum are not in the article.
  • The copy-flip generator is null and off-diagonal. The coupling cannot be a left multiplication, because the left action preserves the ideals; its natural generator is the nilpotent matrix unit, so the generator is not a unit, while the map it induces is an isometry. Boundary: a statement about the shape of the coupling, not its strength.
  • The two ideals are the two levels of one mode. The columns are the empty and the occupied ideal of a single fermionic mode, and the nilpotents that exchange them are the raising and lowering operators. Boundary: the same pair reads as two Peirce copies and as two levels; the article does not choose.
  • The second vacuum is the other idempotent. The two one-mode ideals are the two vacuum choices, and no algebraic fact selects one. Boundary: the module is unique; only the vacuum is a choice.
  • The module and Hilbert complements coincide. The decomposition into the two ideals is at once a module decomposition and an orthogonal decomposition of Hilbert spaces. Boundary: the coincidence uses the Hermiticity of the vacuum idempotent; it fails for a general idempotent.
  • The algebra's own form does not see the split. $S^{\perp_B}$ is a third subspace, sharing only the nilpotent with $S$ and the idempotent with the module complement, so the orthogonal reading of the doubling needs the metric and not the composition form. Boundary: a statement about the two forms.

Limits

  • No prediction of the particle content. The three spins match the observed elementary spins, but the algebra does not say which particle carries which spin, nor why the list is realised.
  • No empirical derivation. The match is a structural alignment of labels; the Standard Model assigns the spins independently.
  • No spin-statistics derivation. The split between the associative and adjoint carriers is algebraic; the link to Fermi and Bose statistics is a separate theorem.
  • No fourth elementary spin. The closure is for $\mathbb B$ alone; higher spin requires a tensor product and is not excluded.
  • No measurement postulate and no Born derivation. The Born rule is cited from its owner; the article states where it sits among the structures.
  • No identification of the two physics. The carrier is one, but the spinor index is not identified with the qubit's two levels.
  • No mass value and no spectrum. The article states the copy flip's algebraic shape; the number is dynamical.
  • No choice between the interpretations of the doubling. The second Peirce copy, the occupation ladder and the conjugate state space are three readings of one algebraic fact.
  • The coincidence is conditional. The module and Hilbert complements coincide for the Hermitian vacuum pair. For a general idempotent they separate.
  • One pair of ideals only. The general idempotent generates a minimal left ideal for every unit vector, and its complement is a different subspace.
  • Not the dual and not a sector. The dual is the right ideal reached by ${}^{*}$; the sectors cut across both ideals.
  • The algebra's form is not the metric. It is indefinite and degenerate on $S$; the positive definite form is $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{*}$.
  • One ideal is one Peirce copy. The two ideals are isomorphic; the two Lorentz chiralities are the defining representation and its conjugate, $S$ and $S^{\mathrm{c}}$.
  • No dynamics. Nothing here says how a state moves between the copies in time, or why the chiral mass has the value it has.

Summary

$\mathbb{B}$ alone has three elementary carrier types, represented by four spaces, and exactly three elementary spins: the centre carries $0$, each Peirce plane carries $\tfrac12$, and the traceless part carries $1$. The associative modules give only $\tfrac12$, because $\mathbb{B}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$ has a single simple module of dimension two; the adjoint modules give $0$ and $1$, because $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}e_t\oplus\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$. There is no fourth elementary value. Higher spin requires a tensor product.

The three values are the spins of all observed elementary particles: $0$ for the Higgs, $\tfrac12$ for the twelve quarks and leptons, $1$ for the photon, the $W^\pm$, the $Z$ and the eight gluons. No observed elementary particle carries another spin. The graviton of spin $2$ is not observed, and the higher-spin hadrons are composites. The match is a match of labels, not a prediction of the particle content.

Within the spin-$\tfrac12$ carrier, the spinor space $S=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_y\}$ is the Peirce carrier of the plain algebra, and it is a minimal left ideal, with a forced basis of one idempotent and one nilpotent. As a module it is the unique simple module, with $\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{B}}(S)=\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{B}\cong S\oplus S$ and $\mathbb{B}\cong\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(S)$; the left action is the defining representation of the double cover, and $-e_t$ acts as $-\mathrm{id}$, which is where spin-$\tfrac12$ lives.

The same carrier carries a second structure from the plain sesquilinear product: a positive definite Hermitian form of Gram matrix $\tfrac12 I_2$, the Born pairing and the normalisation. The two structures do not coincide: the algebra's own bilinear form is degenerate on the carrier, $\operatorname{diag}(\tfrac12,0)$, with radical the nilpotent line. The module is composition and the Hilbert space is probability; the spin is a module datum and the ray a Hilbert datum; the vacuum is a module choice and the normalisation a metric condition.

The algebra also splits into two minimal left ideals, the second column $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_-,\vec\Upsilon_x\}$, module-isomorphic to $S$ through right multiplication by $\vec\Upsilon_x$ and Hilbert-orthogonal to it. The copy-flip coupling is the pair of right multiplications by the two nilpotents, off-diagonal and column-changing; it is not the chiral mass. The natural generators are null, the induced maps are isometries, and the nilpotence is a choice of generator and not an invariant. The complement is not the dual, not a second representation and not a sector. Three complements must be kept apart: the module and Hilbert complements coincide, the algebra's-own-form complement $S^{\perp_B}=\mathbb{C}\{\vec\Upsilon_y,\tilde\Pi_-\}$ is a third subspace, and the coincidence of the first two is conditional on the Hermiticity of the vacuum idempotent.
