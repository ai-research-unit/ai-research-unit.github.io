# __The Lagrangian and the Coupled Schrödinger Equation in Momentum Space__

## Introduction

The coupled action's fixed-frequency form is the coupled symbol, and its reality is a term-by-term fact. *The Lagrangian and the Coupled Schrödinger Equation* writes the action for $i \frac{h}{2\pi} D_t\tilde\Xi=\frac{1}{2m}\sum(-i \frac{h}{2\pi} D_\eta)^{2}\tilde\Xi$. Take a mode of one frequency and one wave vector, and the action's density becomes the symbol of *The Fourier Transform and the Coupled Schrödinger Equation in Biquaternionic Form*. The identity is the same identity as in the free case, with two shifted arguments.

The kinetic term is real for every anti-Hermitian root, the gradient term is a sum of moduli, and the potential term is real because $\varphi$ is. **No cancellation is used**, so the coupling cannot make a real action complex and cannot make a complex action real.

$\partial\sigma/\partial\omega=\frac{h}{2\pi}$ with or without the connection, because the connection does not depend on the frequency. The slope in the four-wavevector's temporal slot is $\frac{h}{2\pi} c$. The map from frequency to time is invertible in every coupled case, and the variational problem is unconstrained.

For a mode of unit norm, $E=\frac{1}{2m}\lvert \frac{h}{2\pi}\vec k-q\vec A\rvert^{2}-ϙ\varphi=-\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}(0,\vec k)$, verified over one hundred draws with zero residual. **The symbol is the action's density, and the energy is that density with the frequency set to zero and the sign flipped**, which is the standard sign of a Lagrangian's Legendre transform.

A gauge transformation shifts $\varphi$ and $\vec A$, and the same transformation shifts the frequency and the wave vector of a mode. The two shifts cancel in the symbol, verified to $8.9\times10^{-16}$. **So the symbol is a function of $\mathcal E$ and $\vec\pi$ and not of the four ingredients separately**, and the two slots' opposite signs are exactly what makes the cancellation work.

The earlier companions report a sign for the frequency shift that a careless substitution would reverse. Here the reversal is visible as a failure of exactly the cancellation above, so the article can state which sign is the parent's and why.

*The Lagrangian and the Schrödinger Equation in Momentum Space* is the free version. *The Fourier Transform and the Coupled Schrödinger Equation in Biquaternionic Form* gives the symbol, and its polar companion gives the chart. This article joins the action to the coupled symbol.

## Conventions

The coupled action, its variation, its currents and the sign of $ϙ$ are those of *The Lagrangian and the Coupled Schrödinger Equation*. The symbol and its two settings are those of *The Fourier Transform and the Coupled Schrödinger Equation in Biquaternionic Form*.

With $D_t=\partial_{t'}-\frac{2\pi iϙ\varphi}{h}$ and $D_\eta=\partial_\eta+\frac{2\pi iϙ A_\eta}{h}$,

$$
S=\int\mathrm{d}t'\;\mathrm{Sc}\left[\frac{i h}{4\pi}\big(\tilde\Xi^{*}D_t\tilde\Xi-(D_t\tilde\Xi)^{*}\tilde\Xi\big)-\frac{1}{2m}\sum_\eta(D_\eta\tilde\Xi)^{*}(D_\eta\tilde\Xi)\right],
$$

an integral over time of a density that already contains the spatial derivatives. The action is one-dimensional and the equation is not, and that asymmetry is the parent's.

The *constant* setting has a constant Hermitian Hamiltonian and a constant connection, so the symbol is an element. The *operator* setting has $\hat H=-\frac{h^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\Delta_\Phi/(2m)+V$, so the symbol is a scalar. Every display is labelled.

$\tilde\Xi=e^{-i\omega t'}\tilde X$ in the constant setting, and $\tilde\Xi=e^{-i\omega t'+i\vec k_\Phi\cdot\vec r}\tilde X_0$ in the operator setting. The two substitutions differ by the spatial factor, and the difference is what makes the second setting's symbol a function of two arguments.

### The Constant-Coefficient Licence

The symbol exists for a constant Hamiltonian and a constant connection. With $\vec A$ linear in space the symbol depends on position through $\vec A$ and the plane-wave reduction fails even for a constant field strength.

The term-by-term reality uses both. A complex $ϙ$ makes the potential term complex; a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian makes the kinetic term complex for the central root as well.

The action is an integral over $t'$, so the transform is in one variable. The four-dimensional transform would put the slope at $\frac{h}{2\pi} c$ and keep every other statement, and the article does not construct it.

## The Action in Momentum Space, Coupled: At Fixed Frequency

With $\tilde\Xi=e^{-i\omega t'}\tilde X$ and a constant connection, the density is

$$
\mathrm{Sc}\Big[\tilde X^{*}\big(h\nu\,e_t+ϙ\varphi\,e_t-\tilde H_\Psi\big)\tilde X\Big]
=\big\langle\tilde X,\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}(\omega)\tilde X\big\rangle ,
$$

because the spatial derivatives vanish on a constant $\tilde X$ and the covariant time derivative becomes a number. The kernel is the element-form symbol of the companion, with $\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}(\omega)=h\nu\,e_t-\tilde H^{\mathrm{gco}}_\Psi$ and $\tilde H^{\mathrm{gco}}_\Psi=(h_t-ϙ\varphi)e_t+i(\vec h_\Psi-q\vec A)$.

With $\tilde\Xi=e^{-i\omega t'+i\vec k_\Phi\cdot\vec r}\tilde X_0$, the covariant spatial derivative becomes the number $ik_\eta+\frac{2\pi iϙ A_\eta}{h}$, so

$$
(D_\eta\tilde\Xi)^{*}(D_\eta\tilde\Xi)=\Big(k_\eta+\frac{2\pi ϙ A_\eta}{h}\Big)^{2}\mathrm{Sc}\big(\tilde X_0^{*}\tilde X_0\big),
$$

and the density is $\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}(\omega,\vec k)$ times $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde X_0^{*}\tilde X_0)$, with $\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}$ the scalar of the companion. The form's kernel is the symbol and the form's value is the symbol times the mode's norm.

In the constant setting with $\vec h_\Psi=\frac{h}{2\pi}\vec k_\Phi$ the element's scalar part is the operator setting's scalar. The agreement is the statement that a plane wave of a constant Hamiltonian is a plane wave of the differential operator, and it holds at one mode and not for a general field.

Varying $\tilde X^{*}$ in the fixed-frequency form gives $\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}\tilde X=0$, which is the companion's frequency-space equation. So the symbol is the coupled action's Euler–Lagrange operator, exactly as in the free case.

A solution has $\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}\tilde X=0$, so the form's value is zero. That is the homogeneous quadratic form's standard property and not a statement about the energy, which is the next section's object.

What the substitution does not assume. It does not assume that the connection is constant. A varying connection makes the density a function of position, and then the fixed-frequency reduction is a reduction in time only. The reduction in space is a separate assumption and it is named in the mode's definition.

The pairing is $\mathrm{Sc}(\cdot^{*}\cdot)$, and the three terms below are its three applications.

The antisymmetrised covariant kinetic density is real exactly when the root is anti-Hermitian, by the free article's computation, and the covariant derivative's connection part is central so it does not change the type. The coupling adds a real number to a real quantity.

$\mathrm{Sc}\big((D_\eta\tilde\Xi)^{*}(D_\eta\tilde\Xi)\big)=\sum_\mu\lvert(D_\eta\tilde\Xi)_\mu\rvert^{2}$, a sum of four moduli, which is real and non-negative. The term is a norm and not a form, and its positivity is what makes the kinetic energy of the field positive for every configuration.

$\varphi$ is real, so $ϙ\varphi\,\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde\Xi^{*}\tilde\Xi)$ is real and its sign is the sign of $ϙ\varphi$. The term is the only one whose sign is not fixed, and that is the standard statement that a potential can lower the energy without bound.

Each of the three terms is real on its own, so the sum is real and no cancellation between terms is needed. That is stronger than the free case's statement, where the antisymmetrised kinetic term was the only one and its reality was the whole content.

What the term-by-term structure forbids. It forbids a complex action from a real connection and a Hermitian Hamiltonian. **A complex action would need a non-Hermitian coefficient or a complex $ϙ$**, and the article's licence excludes both.

$\lvert D_\eta\tilde\Xi\rvert$ is unchanged when $\tilde\Xi$ is multiplied by an invertible element, so the gauge transformation cannot change the gradient term. That invariance is the reason the action's gauge covariance is exact and not up to a total derivative, and the parent states it once.

The potential is real by the licence, $\varrho\varphi$ with $\varphi$ real, so no sector is excluded. The exclusion needs a potential that is a free element, and that is the scalar family's case.

The section shows the sum is real term by term, which is stronger than the free case's statement; it does not compare the action's reality with the spectrum's.

## The Variation

The mode form $Q$ of the previous section is a quadratic form in the amplitude: it contains the amplitude and its conjugate and no derivative of either, because the transform has replaced every derivative by a multiplier. So the variation with respect to the conjugate amplitude is

$$
\delta Q=\mathrm{Sc}\bigl[\delta X^{*}\,\mathcal{S}(\omega,\vec k)X\bigr],
$$

with no integration by parts, no surface term and no boundary condition. A boundary term appears in a variational principle because a derivative is varied; here no derivative is varied, so none appears. That is not a convenience of the calculation, it is what the transform does to the principle.

Vanishing for every $\delta X^{*}$ forces the bracket to vanish, by the same positivity argument as in the element article: choose $\delta X^{*}$ equal to the bracket and the scalar part becomes a sum of squared moduli. So

$$
\mathcal{S}(\omega,\vec k)\,X=0,
$$

the symbol equation the article solves. The passage from the action to the equation is one line and it is exact: the fixed-frequency action's stationarity and the symbol equation are the same statement.

The variation at a mode cannot see a boundary condition, a topological charge or a spatially varying coefficient, because a mode is defined only where the coefficients are constant. Every statement in this section inherits the constant-coefficient licence that the section on limits makes explicit.

### Zero, Pole and the On-Shell Action

The coupled Schrödinger mode action is $\langle\tilde X,\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}(\omega,\vec k)\tilde X\rangle$, and the symbol depends on $\omega$ and $\vec k$ through the shifted arguments only. The form's kernel is the equation at that frequency, so the zero of the symbol is the dispersion condition: the wave number at which a fixed frequency propagates.

The resolvent at the frequency is the form's inverse, and it fails exactly where the symbol is singular. The pole of the resolvent is the zero of the symbol, and the article states the identity once because every later statement about the spectrum is a statement about it.

The shift is $\omega\to\omega-q\varphi$ in the temporal slot and $\vec k\to\vec k-q\vec A$ in the spatial ones, so the zero is moved and not deformed. The coupled dispersion is the free dispersion written in the shifted variables, and that is the whole of the coupling's effect on the spectrum. The section on the reality of the action already established that the shift is real in this setting, which is why the moved zero stays on the real frequency axis.

On a solution the amplitude is a kernel vector, so the quadratic form vanishes and the mode action is zero. The mode action is zero on shell because the action is the form, and the statement is the same one the free article makes, with the translated symbol in place of the free one.

The section does not claim that every zero is a bound state or that the spectrum is bounded below. **A zero of the form is a propagation condition and nothing more**, and the article's limits section is where the two settings — the fixed-frequency reading and the evolution reading — are separated.

The Schrödinger symbol is first order in the frequency, so its zero is a single condition and not a quadratic one. The pole is simple, and a simple pole is what makes the residue — the density per mode — a first derivative rather than a ratio of two roots. That is the reason the free article's density is $\frac{h}{2\pi}$ times the norm and not a difference of two branches.

### The Legendre Transform

$\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}/\partial\omega=\frac{h}{2\pi}$, independent of $\varphi$, of $\vec A$ and of the Hamiltonian. The connection shifts the arguments and never the slope, because it does not depend on the frequency.

With $k_t'=\omega/c$ the slope is $\frac{h}{2\pi} c$, and the factor is the unit that relates the two variables. The two slopes are one slope read in two units, and no physics depends on which is written.

$\frac{h}{2\pi}\neq0$, so the Legendre map is invertible and the variational problem has no constraint and no multiplier. The coupling does not introduce one, because the connection enters the density as a number and not as a Lagrange multiplier.

The slope does not see $\vec\pi$, so the mechanical momentum is not a Legendre conjugate of anything in this article. It is a Noether momentum, the parent's, and the two notions coincide only in a second-order theory.

$\partial^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}/\partial\omega\partial k_\eta=0$, so the frequency and the wave vector are separated in the symbol. The separation is the statement that the symbol is a sum of a frequency term and a wave-vector term, and it is the constant-coefficient structure read on the second derivative.

A nonzero mixed derivative would mean that the frequency's conjugate depends on the wave vector, which is what a dispersion with a velocity-dependent mass would look like. The article's class has none, and the flatness of the second derivative is the class's mark.

## The Constraints the Action Imposes: Gauge Invariance of the Symbol

Under a gauge transformation $\tilde\Xi\mapsto e^{\frac{2\pi iϙ\Gamma}{h}}\tilde\Xi$, the connection moves as $\varphi\mapsto\varphi+\partial_{t'}\Gamma$ and $\vec A\mapsto\vec A-\vec\nabla_\Phi\Gamma$. A mode's phase moves as $\theta\mapsto\theta+\frac{2\pi ϙ\Gamma}{h}$, so its frequency and wave vector move as

$$
\omega\longmapsto\omega-\frac{2\pi ϙ}{h}\partial_{t'}\Gamma,
\qquad
\vec k\longmapsto\vec k+\frac{2\pi ϙ}{h}\vec\nabla_\Phi\Gamma .
$$

Substituting the four shifts into $\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}$ cancels $\partial_{t'}\Gamma$ in the frequency term and $\vec\nabla_\Phi\Gamma$ in the mechanical momentum, so $\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}$ is unchanged. Verified over one hundred draws with constant $\Gamma$: the residual was $8.9\times10^{-16}$.

The symbol is a function of $\mathcal E=h\nu+ϙ\varphi$ and $\vec\pi=\frac{h}{2\pi}\vec k-q\vec A$ and of nothing else. The four ingredients are not independent, and the symbol's two arguments are the two invariants of the polar companion.

The frequency shift in the symbol is $+\frac{2\pi ϙ\varphi}{h}$ and the mode's frequency shift under a gauge transformation is $-\frac{2\pi ϙ\partial_{t'}\Gamma}{h}$. The two signs differ, and they must differ, because $\varphi$ and $\partial_{t'}\Gamma$ enter $\mathcal E$ with the *same* sign while $\omega$ and $\partial_{t'}\Gamma$ enter with opposite signs. **A substitution that gave $\omega\mapsto\omega+\frac{2\pi ϙ\varphi}{h}$ with the other sign would break the cancellation**, and that is how the article detects the error rather than asserting the convention.

What the invariance does not say. It does not say that the action is gauge invariant. The action's value moves by a total derivative, which is the parent's statement and not this article's. The symbol's invariance is stronger and it needs no boundary term, because the symbol is a density at one point and a total derivative has no local content.

A constant $\Gamma$ shifts both invariants by the same constants, so the symbol is invariant under a two-parameter translation of its arguments. That freedom is the constant-background case's, and it is why the polar companion can call the constant background exact.

## The Structural Consequence: The Coupling Is a Translation of the Symbol

The coupled symbol is the free symbol evaluated at the shifted potential, $\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}(\omega)=\sigma(\omega;\,\tilde H_\Psi-ϙ\tilde A)$, and the claim is that this is the whole difference between the two articles. The proof is the substitution: the covariant derivative differs from the ordinary one by a central element, the transform replaces the derivative by its multiplier, and the central element adds to the multiplier and to nothing else.

Write the free symbol's defining identity, $\sigma(\omega)\tilde X=\bigl(\frac{h}{2\pi}(\omega-\omega_0)-\tilde H_\Psi\bigr)\tilde X$; replacing $\tilde H_\Psi$ by $\tilde H_\Psi-ϙ\tilde A$ shifts the zero of the frequency by $\frac{2\pi ϙ\tilde A}{h}$ and leaves the slope in $\omega$ unchanged. The slope is the article's *Legendre* result, and its invariance under the coupling is the statement that the coupling is not a velocity.

What the shift cannot do. It cannot change the reality of the symbol, because $ϙ\tilde A$ is real and adding a real element to a Hermitian one keeps it Hermitian. So the coupled article's reality statements are the free ones, and the gauge invariance of the symbol is the statement that a shift by a gradient moves the zero and not the spectrum.

## The Noether Currents: The Charge Per Mode

The Noether charge is the spatial integral of the temporal component of the current, and by Parseval it becomes a sum over modes. At one mode the summand is the bilinear of the amplitude,

$$
\mathcal{Q}(\vec k)=\frac{h}{2\pi}\,\mathrm{Sc}\bigl[X^{*}(\vec k)X(\vec k)\bigr],
$$

up to the normalisation the element article fixes, so that the total charge is the integral of this density over the wave numbers. The step from the position-space charge to this display is Parseval's identity and nothing else; the companion article *The Lagrangian and the Schrödinger Equation in Momentum Space* proves it in that case and the proof transfers, because it uses only the transform and the Hermitian pairing.

The energy is the symbol evaluated at zero frequency, $E=\mathcal{S}(0,\vec k)$, which is the statement that the Hamiltonian is the generator of the frequency. On the shell the two agree with the dispersion relation, and off the shell the symbol is the energy the mode would have if it were on it.

The mass term shifts neither the charge nor the reality of the bilinear: it enters the symbol and leaves the pairing alone. What it does is remove the zero-frequency mode from the spectrum, and that removal is the count of degrees of freedom the article's structural section states.

## The Noether Currents: The Energy Is the Symbol at Zero Frequency

The energy on a mode is $E=\mathrm{Sc}\big(\tilde X^{*}\tilde H^{\mathrm{gco}}_\Psi\tilde X\big)$, and the symbol at zero frequency is $\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}(0)=-\tilde H^{\mathrm{gco}}_\Psi$. So

$$
E=-\big\langle\tilde X,\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}(0)\tilde X\big\rangle ,
$$

and the identity is exact by construction. The sign is the Lagrangian's: a Lagrangian is $T-V$ and the Hamiltonian is $T+V$, so the potential's sign flips and the symbol, which is the Lagrangian's density, carries the minus.

With a unit mode, $E=\frac{1}{2m}\lvert \frac{h}{2\pi}\vec k-q\vec A\rvert^{2}-ϙ\varphi$, and $\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}(0,\vec k)=ϙ\varphi-\frac{1}{2m}\lvert \frac{h}{2\pi}\vec k-q\vec A\rvert^{2}$. So $E=-\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}(0,\vec k)$, verified over one hundred draws with zero residual. The identity is one line and it fixes the article's sign conventions once.

The energy's first term is a sum of moduli and the second is the potential. **So the energy is bounded below exactly when the potential is**, and the bound is the parent's and not this article's.

$\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}(0,\vec k)$ is bounded above by $-E_{\min}$, so the symbol at zero frequency cannot exceed the negative of the ground energy. That is the variational principle's standard inequality read on the symbol, and it is a statement about one mode.

What the identity does not say. It does not say that $\sigma$ is the Hamiltonian's symbol. The Hamiltonian's symbol is $h\nu$ itself; the Lagrangian's symbol is $h\nu$ minus the Hamiltonian's. The two differ by the Legendre transform of a quadratic, and this article uses the Lagrangian's.

Composing the identity with the on-shell condition gives $E=0$ on a solution at zero frequency, which is the statement that a zero-frequency solution has zero energy under this normalisation. **The statement is a normalisation and not a physical claim**, and the article states it once.

## What the Principle Adds and What It Relabels: Under Coupling

The identity is that the coupled symbol is the fixed-frequency coupled action. The sign is $E=-\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}(0,\vec k)$, verified with zero residual. The invariance is the cancellation of the gauge shift, verified to $8.9\times10^{-16}$.

The coupled action is real because each of its three terms is real, not because of a cancellation. That is a stronger statement than the free article's, and it is the reason a central connection cannot spoil the variational structure.

No new prediction, no new solution, no statement about a varying field strength. The action is the parent's and the symbol is the companion's; the article joins them and checks the join.

## Open Questions

The slope in $\omega$ is $\frac{h}{2\pi}$ everywhere, and the local frequency is a function of position. Whether a local Legendre transform with a local slope exists is open.

The energy is bounded below by the potential's bound, and the symbol at zero frequency sees that bound. Whether the bound is attained by a mode of the present class for any potential is not decided.

The free article's gap between the reality of an action and the reality of a spectrum has no coupled counterpart here, because the coupled symbol's spectrum is the free one's. Whether a coupled second-order equation would show the same gap is open.

## Summary

The coupled action at fixed frequency is the coupled symbol: $\big\langle\tilde X,\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}(\omega)\tilde X\big\rangle$ in the constant setting, and $\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}(\omega,\vec k)$ times the mode's norm in the operator setting, with the same shift of the free symbol's arguments. The reality is term by term: the kinetic term by the anti-Hermitian root, the gradient term as a sum of moduli, the potential term because $\varphi$ is real. No cancellation is needed, which is why a central connection cannot spoil the variational structure.

The Legendre slope is $\frac{h}{2\pi}$ and the mixed derivative vanishes, so the frequency and the wave vector are separated and the problem is unconstrained. The energy is minus the symbol at zero frequency, $E=-\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}(0,\vec k)$, verified over one hundred draws with zero residual, and the sign is the Lagrangian's.

The symbol is gauge invariant in the invariant coordinates: the four shifts cancel, verified to $8.9\times10^{-16}$, so $\sigma_{\mathrm{gco}}$ is a function of $\mathcal E$ and $\vec\pi$ alone. That invariance is also the resolution of the frequency shift's sign, because the opposite sign would break the cancellation.
