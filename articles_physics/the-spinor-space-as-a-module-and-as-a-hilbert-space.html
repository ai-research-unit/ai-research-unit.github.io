The Spinor Space as a Module and as a Hilbert Space

Introduction

The spinor space of the biquaternion framework is the minimal left ideal $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_1$ generated by the idempotent $\tilde\Pi_1=\tfrac12(e_0+ie_3)$; its elements are the spinors, and the algebra acts on them by left multiplication. Two companion articles build this space. The Spinor Module in Biquaternionic Form and Its Lorentz Action constructs the module, its Lorentz action and its bilinear pairings, and The Four General Products and Their Physical Readings maps the four general products of the space and names the physical job of each. The present article joins the two and states what they leave implicit: the spinor space carries two structures at once, and the two structures belong to two different products.

The space is a left module of the general plain algebra (GPA), the algebra of the plain bilinear product $\tilde P\tilde Q$; and it is a Hilbert space of the general plain sesqualgebra (GPS), the structure of the plain sesquilinear product $\tilde P\tilde Q^{*}$. Neither structure contains the other. The module is what the algebra acts by; it is the structure of a representation, and it carries the Lorentz action and the double cover. The Hilbert space is what the states are paired by; it is the structure of a metric, and it carries the norm, the normalisation and the Born rule.

The names of the two corpus groups are the two jobs. The plain algebra is the group of composition, the plain sesqualgebra the group of probability (physics.md). The spinor space is the place where the two are one carrier under two structures, and this article states the split, tests it against the four products, and reads its consequences for the qubit, the Born rule and the spinor calculus.

The Product Is Part of the Definition

The Unqualified Product Is the Plain Product

Every class of element in the corpus is defined by an equation whose symbol is a product: an idempotent by $\tilde\Pi\star\tilde\Pi=\tilde\Pi$, a nilpotent by $\tilde\Upsilon\star\tilde\Upsilon=0$, a zero divisor by $\tilde Q\star\tilde R=0$. The definitions article Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures states the rule: the product is part of the definition and not a background convention, so a class is always relative to a product. The space carries four general products,

$$ \tilde P\tilde Q,\qquad \tilde P^{\natural}\tilde Q,\qquad \tilde P\tilde Q^{*},\qquad \tilde P^{\natural}\tilde Q^{*}, $$

and the unqualified reading is the first, the plain bilinear product, the multiplication of the algebra. A sentence such as "$\tilde\Pi_1$ is an idempotent" is therefore a sentence about the plain product unless another product is named.

This is the point the present article turns on. The two structures of the spinor space are two products of the same space, and the words "module" and "Hilbert space" are well defined only once the product is fixed.

The Two Structures the Space Uses

Among the twelve algebraic structures of the space, two are the ones the spinor space needs. The general plain algebra is the plain product read in both slots, $\tilde P\tilde Q$; its menu label is composition. The general plain sesqualgebra is the plain product with the second slot read through the Hermitian conjugation ${}^{*}$, $\tilde P\tilde Q^{*}$; its menu label is probability. The four general products, their two slots and their physical jobs are the subject of The Four General Products and Their Two Slots: the Two Algebras and the Two Sesqualgebras and The Four General Products and Their Physical Readings; this article uses only the two structures named above.

The Space and Its Two Structures

The Carrier

Let

$$ \tilde\Pi_1=\tfrac12(e_0+ie_3),\qquad \tilde\Pi_2=\tfrac12(e_0-ie_3),\qquad \tilde\Upsilon_1=\tfrac12(ie_1-e_2),\qquad \tilde\Upsilon_2=\tfrac12(ie_1+e_2). $$

The pair $\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Pi_2$ is orthogonal and complete in the plain product,

$$ \tilde\Pi_1^2=\tilde\Pi_1,\qquad \tilde\Pi_2^2=\tilde\Pi_2,\qquad \tilde\Pi_1\tilde\Pi_2=\tilde\Pi_2\tilde\Pi_1=0,\qquad \tilde\Pi_1+\tilde\Pi_2=e_0, $$

and each of the two generates a minimal left ideal. The spinor space is the first of them,

$$ S=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_1=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Upsilon_2\}, $$

of complex dimension two, the defining module of $\mathbb{B}$. The elements $\tilde\Upsilon_1$ and $\tilde\Upsilon_2$ are the off-diagonal matrix units, and the four together are a $\mathbb{C}$-basis of $\mathbb{B}$ whose products are the matrix units $E_{11},E_{12},E_{21},E_{22}$ in the identification of Ideals and Peirce Decomposition of the General Plain Algebra:

$\cdot$ $\tilde\Pi_1$ $\tilde\Pi_2$ $\tilde\Upsilon_1$ $\tilde\Upsilon_2$
$\tilde\Pi_1$ $\tilde\Pi_1$ $0$ $\tilde\Upsilon_1$ $0$
$\tilde\Pi_2$ $0$ $\tilde\Pi_2$ $0$ $\tilde\Upsilon_2$
$\tilde\Upsilon_1$ $0$ $\tilde\Upsilon_1$ $0$ $\tilde\Pi_1$
$\tilde\Upsilon_2$ $\tilde\Upsilon_2$ $0$ $\tilde\Pi_2$ $0$

Why the Basis Is One Idempotent and One Nilpotent

The corner-adapted basis $(\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Upsilon_2)$ of $S$, the basis with one element per Peirce corner, is not a choice: the Peirce decomposition forces exactly one idempotent and one nilpotent. The idempotents of $S$ are the affine family $\tilde\Pi_1+c\tilde\Upsilon_2$ with $c\in\mathbb{C}$, so a basis of $S$ need not have this shape; it is the corner-adapted basis that does. Because $\tilde\Pi_1+\tilde\Pi_2=e_0$, every element of the left ideal expands as

$$ \mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_1=(\tilde\Pi_1+\tilde\Pi_2)\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_1=\tilde\Pi_1\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_1\oplus\tilde\Pi_2\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_1, $$

the sum of the $(1,1)$ corner and the $(2,1)$ corner of the Peirce decomposition of $\mathbb{B}$. Each corner is one-dimensional over $\mathbb{C}$ because $\mathbb{B}$ is a simple algebra of rank two, so the ideal, of complex dimension two, holds exactly one basis element per corner:

$$ \tilde\Pi_1\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_1=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_1,\qquad \tilde\Pi_2\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_1=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Upsilon_2 . $$

The diagonal corner contains the idempotent $\tilde\Pi_1$, which is the identity of that corner; the off-diagonal corner contains the nilpotent $\tilde\Upsilon_2$. The nilpotency is not a property of $\tilde\Upsilon_2$ in particular: for orthogonal idempotents $e,f$, every element $x=fae$ of an off-diagonal corner satisfies

$$ x^2=(fae)(fae)=fa(ef)ae=0, $$

so every off-diagonal corner consists of square-zero elements, and the off-diagonal basis element of a minimal left ideal is necessarily a nilpotent. The count is therefore structural. A minimal left ideal is one column of the matrix model; its basis holds one element from the diagonal corner, the idempotent that is the column's own projector, and one element from each off-diagonal corner of that column, and in rank two there is exactly one such corner. In the general rank-$n$ algebra $M_n(\mathbb{C})$ the same argument gives one idempotent and $n-1$ nilpotents, one per off-diagonal corner; the basis of $S$ is the case $n=2$. The general count, the normalisation of the diagonal element and the affine family of idempotents are The Column Ideals of a Matrix Ring: One Idempotent and $n-1$ Nilpotents. The complementary ideal is the mirror case, $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Upsilon_1\oplus\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_2$, with one nilpotent and one idempotent in the same two-corner shape.

The point is the one the Introduction announces: the shape of the basis is a fact of the ideals and the Peirce decomposition, and it needs the associative unital plain product in which the idempotents and the corners are defined. Neither the quaternionic product nor either sesquilinear product has the idempotents and the corners, so neither can produce this basis.

The Structure of the Module

The plain product makes $S$ a left $\mathbb{B}$-module: for $\tilde Q\in\mathbb{B}$ and $\tilde\psi\in S$ the product $\tilde Q\tilde\psi$ lies again in $S$, and the action is compatible with the product, so that $\tilde Q(\tilde R\tilde\psi)=(\tilde Q\tilde R)\tilde\psi$. Written on the basis $(\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Upsilon_2)$, the left multiplications by the four basis elements are

$$ L_{\tilde\Pi_1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad L_{\tilde\Pi_2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad L_{\tilde\Upsilon_1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad L_{\tilde\Upsilon_2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}. $$

These are the matrices of the matrix model $\mathsf{M}_2$ with $\mathsf{M}_2(e_k)=-i\sigma_k$, so the module is literally the column space $\mathbb{C}^2$ on which the algebra acts by matrix multiplication. The module is simple, its endomorphism ring is $\operatorname{End}_\mathbb{B}(S)=\mathbb{C}$ by Schur's lemma, and it is the unique simple left module; the left regular module is the direct sum $\mathbb{B}\cong S\oplus S$ of two copies. The module theory is that of Modules over the General Plain Algebra of Biquaternions, and the biquaternion coordinate form is that of The Spinor Module in Biquaternionic Form and Its Lorentz Action.

The Structure of the Hilbert Space

The plain sesquilinear product pairs the elements, and its scalar part is the general plain sesquilinear form

$$ \langle u,v\rangle_*=\mathrm{Sc}(uv^{*}), $$

which is positive definite on the whole algebra, $\langle\tilde Q,\tilde Q\rangle_*=\sum_\mu|Q_\mu|^2$. Restricted to the basis $(\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Upsilon_2)$ it has the Gram matrix

$$ G=\begin{pmatrix}\langle\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Pi_1\rangle_*&\langle\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Upsilon_2\rangle_*\\[2pt]\langle\tilde\Upsilon_2,\tilde\Pi_1\rangle_*&\langle\tilde\Upsilon_2,\tilde\Upsilon_2\rangle_*\end{pmatrix}=\tfrac12 I_2, $$

so the restriction is a positive definite inner product and $S$ is a Hilbert space. The pairing is complex-linear in its first argument and conjugate-linear in its second, and it is Hermitian, $\langle v,u\rangle_*=\overline{\langle u,v\rangle_*}$. It is the spinor-side form whose module-level origin is the canonical Hermitian form $h(\tilde P,\tilde R)=\tilde P\tilde R^{*}$ of the regular module (The Canonical Hermitian Form on the Regular Module of the Biquaternion Algebra), of which $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_*$ is the scalar part.

Why the Two Structures Do Not Coincide

The Module Needs an Associative Product with a Unit

A Peirce decomposition, a minimal left ideal and a module structure all presuppose an associative product with a two-sided unit. An idempotent is idempotent only in such a product, the Peirce corners are built from it, and the module axioms use the associativity and the unit. Of the four products of the space only the plain one is associative with a two-sided unit: the two sesquilinear products have no unit at all, and the two quaternionic ones are neither associative nor unital. The module structure is therefore a fact about the plain product alone, and every statement about an ideal belongs to it.

The Hilbert Structure Needs the Antilinear Slot

Positivity is not a property of the plain product. The general plain bilinear form $B(\tilde Q,\tilde R)=\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde Q\tilde R)$ is bilinear and complex-valued, and on the whole real space it is indefinite, of signature $(4,4)$; its diagonal $B(\tilde Q,\tilde Q)$ is not a sum of squared moduli. The inner product needs the second slot read through ${}^{*}$, which is conjugate-linear and turns the diagonal into $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde Q\tilde Q^{*})=\sum_\mu|Q_\mu|^2$, a sum of squared moduli. So the Hilbert structure is a fact about the sesquilinear product alone, and it exists because the second slot is available.

The Four Products Tested on the Space

For a product $\star$, an element $x$ is an idempotent of $\star$ when $x\star x=x$ and square-zero of $\star$ when $x\star x=0$. The four products give four different answers on the same four elements.

plain sesquilinear, $\tilde P\tilde Q^{*}$ yes no yes no no

The first four columns identify the plain product as the only one that carries the whole module package. The last column is the sharpest statement of the split: the carrier $S$ is closed under the plain left action, and it happens also to be closed under the quaternionic left action, but that product is neither associative nor unital, so it supports no module structure. The two sesquilinear products do not preserve $S$ at all; they pair the space, they do not act on it. That is the structural reason the module and the Hilbert space are two structures and not two views of one object.

The Two Jobs of the Space

Composition: the Module

The label composition belongs to the general plain algebra because the plain product composes two elements of the algebra, and the module structure is that composition read with one factor held fixed. The physical action carried by the module is the Lorentz action: a unit-norm biquaternion $\tilde\Lambda$, $\tilde\Lambda\tilde\Lambda^{\natural}=e_0$, acts by plain left multiplication,

$$ \psi\longmapsto\tilde\Lambda\psi,\qquad \tilde\Lambda\in SL(2,\mathbb{C}),\quad\psi\in S . $$

The action is one-sided, it is faithful, and it does not descend to the Lorentz group, because $-e_0$ acts as $-\mathrm{id}$ on $S$ while it acts trivially on the four-vectors: the double cover appears on the module side and not on the four-vector side. It is also where the chirality is organised, since the defining module is the left-handed half and its complex conjugate the right-handed half.

Probability: the Hilbert Space

The label probability belongs to the general plain sesqualgebra because the plain sesquilinear product is the probability pairing. A state is a rank-one projector $\tilde\rho$ of $\mathbb{M}_+$, an observable is a Hermitian element $\tilde H$, and the pairing is the trace formula

$$ \mathrm{Tr}(\tilde\rho\tilde H)=2\,\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde\rho\tilde H), $$

which is the framework's Born rule as a pairing on the algebra (The Hermitian Subspace $\mathbb{M}_+$ as the Informational Sector, The Born Rule as a Trace Formula — Derivation and Comparison). On the spinor space the same structure is the inner product of the spinor calculus, $h(\psi,\phi)=\psi^{\dagger}\phi$ in module coordinates, and its restriction to a single chiral half is invariant exactly on the compact subgroup $SU(2)$.

The Gram Matrix and the Two Amplitudes

The Basis and the Gram Matrix

A general spinor of $S$ is $\tilde\psi=\psi_1\tilde\Pi_1+\psi_2\tilde\Upsilon_2$ with $\psi_1,\psi_2\in\mathbb{C}$, and the coordinate map $\tilde\psi\leftrightarrow(\psi_1,\psi_2)^{T}$ is an isomorphism of modules. The Gram matrix $G=\tfrac12 I_2$ says that the two basis spinors are orthogonal and of equal length,

$$ \langle\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Upsilon_2\rangle_*=0,\qquad \langle\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Pi_1\rangle_*=\langle\tilde\Upsilon_2,\tilde\Upsilon_2\rangle_*=\tfrac12 . $$

The Norm and the Born Pairing

The norm of the spinor follows from the Gram matrix,

$$ \langle\tilde\psi,\tilde\psi\rangle_*=\tfrac12\bigl(|\psi_1|^2+|\psi_2|^2\bigr), $$

a sum of squared moduli over the two amplitudes. The factor $\tfrac12$ is the corpus normalisation $\mathrm{Tr}(e_0)=2$ and not a defect; the content is the positivity, and it is what makes the amplitude bilinear in the state. The reason the state space of the framework reads as $\mathbb{C}^2$ with its standard inner product is therefore algebraic: the two amplitudes are the coefficients of the two basis spinors, and the metric on them is the restriction of the plain sesquilinear form, which is positive definite and diagonal on the ideal's own basis.

What the Split Explains

The Born Rule as a Product

The Born rule is a trace pairing because the Hilbert structure is a product-based form. The canonical Hermitian form of the regular module is $h(\tilde P,\tilde R)=\tilde P\tilde R^{*}$, whose scalar part is $\langle\tilde P,\tilde R\rangle_*$, and the probability pairing is the scalar part of one of the four general products. It is not an extra axiom laid on a module; it is a product, and the framework's claim that the Born rule is algebraic rather than postulated rests on exactly this. The same fact is why the state space is positive definite before any interpretation: the positivity is the definiteness of the plain sesquilinear form, and it needs no probability language to hold.

The Two Kinds of Form on One Carrier

The carrier $S$ holds both a bilinear form and a sesquilinear one, and the two are different in kind. The invariant symplectic form $\varepsilon(\psi,\phi)=\psi^{T}\epsilon\phi$ is bilinear, so it belongs to the algebra side of the two slots, and it is invariant under all of $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$; the Hermitian form $h(\psi,\phi)=\psi^{\dagger}\phi$ takes its second slot through ${}^{*}$, so it belongs to the sesqualgebra side, and it is invariant only on the compact subgroup $SU(2)$. The first is the pairing that raises and lowers Weyl indices and gives self-duality $S^{*}\cong S$; the second is the pairing that gives probabilities. The two-slots mechanism — one product for each choice of an involution in each slot — is the origin of the coexistence, and the carrier of both is the same $\mathbb{C}^2$.

The Qubit and the Spinor

The informational reading and the spinor reading of the framework are readings of one carrier under the two structures. Under the module structure, $S$ is the defining representation of the double cover and its elements are Weyl spinors. Under the Hilbert structure, the same $S$ is the state space, its rays are the pure states and its unit vectors are the qubit states, and the operators of $\mathbb{M}_+$ act on it as observables. What is not claimed is that the spinor index of the Lorentz group and the two levels of the qubit are one physical degree of freedom; that identification is a separate physical question, and this article leaves it where the companion articles leave it. What is claimed is narrower and algebraic: the carrier and the two structures coincide, and the two structures are the two products.

The Physical Meaning of the Two Structures

The Arena and the Probability

The module structure is what makes the space an arena: $\mathbb{B}\cong\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(S)$, so the algebra is the algebra of operators and the module is what the operators act on. The Hilbert structure is what makes the arena a probability space: the sesquilinear form is what can be evaluated on a state and squared into a number. A physical theory of states and observables therefore needs both, and the framework supplies both on one carrier — the states are the Hilbert vectors, the observables are the module endomorphisms, and the pairing of the two is the trace formula of §Probability: the Hilbert Space. Neither structure alone gives the pair: a module without a form has no numbers, a form without an action has no observables.

Quantisation Is the Second Slot

The corpus reads the insertion of the star into the second slot as quantisation (The Four General Products and Their Physical Readings, The Quantum–Relativity Tension and the Biquaternion Framework). Read on the spinor space, this is exact and local: the plain product gives the module of operations, and the star in the second slot gives the pairing of probabilities. The two structures of the space are therefore the two sides of the one step the corpus calls quantisation. The physical reading is that the spinor space is a compositional object before the step and a probabilistic object after it, and that the step is not an added axiom but the choice of what to put in the second slot.

Spin Lives on the Module, the Ray Lives on the Hilbert Space

The double cover acts on the module: the action $\psi\mapsto\tilde\Lambda\psi$ is faithful, $-e_0$ acts as $-\mathrm{id}$, and this is why the framework carries spin-$\tfrac12$ and not only a vector representation. The Hilbert structure does not see the sign: $\psi$ and $-\psi$ define the same functional on every state, hence the same ray and the same probabilities. The two facts are the two structures. The spin is a module datum, because it is a property of how the algebra acts; the ray is a Hilbert datum, because it is an equivalence class for the phase, and the phase is invisible to the form. This is the algebraic form of the standard statement that a spinor state is a ray in a two-dimensional complex space and a spin-$\tfrac12$ object at once.

Normalisation and the Vacuum

Two further physical data attach to the two structures separately. Normalisation is a Hilbert datum: $\langle\tilde\psi,\tilde\psi\rangle_*=1$ is a condition on the form, and it makes the amplitudes into probabilities. The vacuum is a module datum: the choice of idempotent $\tilde\Pi_1$ selects the module as the ideal it generates (The Biquaternion Vacuum as a Minimal Idempotent), and the module is fixed only up to the equivalence of idempotents. The physical reading is that the state space is normalised by the metric and selected by the vacuum, and that the two acts are of different kinds — one is a pairing, the other is a choice of generator.

The Complementary Half

The module structure carries a second object that the Hilbert structure pairs with $S$: the complementary minimal left ideal $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2$, which is at once the other Peirce summand of $\mathbb{B}=S\oplus\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2$ and the orthogonal complement of $S$ under the form of §The Structure of the Hilbert Space. Its own two structures, and its physical meaning, are the subject of the companion article The Complementary Space of the Spinor Space and Its Physical Meaning.

Conventions and Traps

The Products of the Space

The four general products of the biquaternion space are the plain bilinear $\tilde P\tilde Q$, the quaternionic bilinear $\tilde P^{\natural}\tilde Q$, the plain sesquilinear $\tilde P\tilde Q^{*}$ and the quaternionic sesquilinear $\tilde P^{\natural}\tilde Q^{*}$. The generic product is written $\star$, and a class is taken in the plain bilinear product when no product is named. The idempotent and nilpotent glyphs are $\tilde\Pi$ and $\tilde\Upsilon$, and the idempotents here are the standard pair $\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Pi_2$ of The idempotent convention.

The Traps

  • The words move with the product. Idempotent and square-zero are relative to a product: $\tilde\Pi_1$ is idempotent in the plain product and not in the quaternionic one, and $\tilde\Upsilon_2$ is square-zero in the two bilinear products and not in the two sesquilinear ones. A statement that does not name its product is read in the plain product.
  • Positivity is not the plain product. The plain bilinear form is indefinite, of signature $(4,4)$ on the real space; the positive form needs the second slot read through ${}^{*}$. The Hilbert structure must not be attributed to the general plain algebra.
  • The module and the pairing are not two normalisations of one object. The module is closed under the plain left action and is a submodule; the sesquilinear product does not act on $S$ at all, it pairs it. A reader who replaces one by the other loses the distinction the article is about.
  • The two minimal left ideals carry the same representation. The ideals $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_1$ and $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2$ are both isomorphic to $S$ and carry the same defining representation of $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$, so any statement that the two ideals carry different physics is a statement about a different product or a different real structure, not about the plain module. The module-level chirality of the Lorentz group (the two Weyl halves $S$ and $\bar S$) is invisible to the complex algebra and appears only through the real structure or on complexification. A second, different use of the word chirality occurs in the corpus: the grading operator of the spectral-triple and chirality-off-diagonal-mass readings, which distinguishes the two minimal left ideals (The Spectral Triple of the Biquaternion Algebra, Bosonization in Biquaternionic Form). The two conventions coexist and are not the same object; a reader should check which one an article means.

Boundaries

  • Cited rather than re-derived. The four products and their slots are The Four General Products of the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space, The Four General Products and Their Two Slots and The Four General Products and Their Physical Readings; the module theory is Modules over the General Plain Algebra of Biquaternions, and the Peirce decomposition, the corners and the off-diagonal nilpotency are Ideals and Peirce Decomposition of the General Plain Algebra; the sesquilinear form of the module is The Canonical Hermitian Form on the Regular Module of the Biquaternion Algebra; the spinor module, its Lorentz action and its pairings are The Spinor Module in Biquaternionic Form and Its Lorentz Action; the Born rule and its derivation are The Hermitian Subspace $\mathbb{M}_+$ as the Informational Sector and The Born Rule as a Trace Formula — Derivation and Comparison.
  • Not claimed. The article adds no measurement postulate and identifies no probability with a frequency; it does not identify the spinor index with the qubit's two levels; and it states the two structures of one carrier, not a new structure of the algebra.

Worked Verification

The claims of the article are recomputed on the basis $(\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Upsilon_2)$ and on the four elements.

  • Idempotents and nilpotents. $\tilde\Pi_1^2=\tilde\Pi_1$, $\tilde\Pi_2^2=\tilde\Pi_2$, $\tilde\Upsilon_1^2=\tilde\Upsilon_2^2=0$ in the plain product, $\tilde\Upsilon_1\tilde\Upsilon_2=\tilde\Pi_1$, $\tilde\Upsilon_2\tilde\Upsilon_1=\tilde\Pi_2$.
  • Closure of the carrier. $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_1=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Upsilon_2\}$ is preserved by plain left multiplication on every basis element, and not preserved by the plain sesquilinear left multiplication.
  • The one idempotent and one nilpotent. $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_1=\tilde\Pi_1\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_1\oplus\tilde\Pi_2\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_1=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_1\oplus\mathbb{C}\tilde\Upsilon_2$, each corner one-dimensional, and every element of the off-diagonal corner satisfies $(\tilde\Pi_2 a\tilde\Pi_1)^2=0$ for a generic $a$.
  • Gram matrix. With $\langle u,v\rangle_*=\mathrm{Sc}(uv^{*})$ one has $\langle\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Pi_1\rangle_*=\langle\tilde\Upsilon_2,\tilde\Upsilon_2\rangle_*=\tfrac12$ and $\langle\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Upsilon_2\rangle_*=0$, so $G=\tfrac12 I_2$.
  • Four-product table. The idempotency, the square-zero property, the orthogonality, the unit and the closure are as the table of §The Four Products Tested on the Space records, each read in the named product.
  • Hermiticity and the complement. The form is Hermitian, $\langle\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Upsilon_2\rangle_*=\overline{\langle\tilde\Upsilon_2,\tilde\Pi_1\rangle_*}=0$, and the four matrix units $\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Upsilon_2,\tilde\Upsilon_1,\tilde\Pi_2$ are mutually orthogonal of norm $\tfrac12$, so the complementary ideal $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2$ is the orthogonal complement of $S$ and the form is the normalised Hilbert–Schmidt form of the matrix model.

Physical Readings

  • One space, two structures, two jobs. The reading is that the framework's state space is not one structure but two: the composition structure that makes the algebra act and the probability structure that makes the states pair. The two are separated by the product, and the separation is exact. Boundary: a statement about the algebraic structures, and it adds no identity between the composition and the probability.
  • The Born rule is a product, not a postulate. The reading is that the probability pairing is the scalar part of the plain sesquilinear product, so the positivity of probabilities is the definiteness of a form and the trace formula is the scalar pairing. This is the reading the article The Born Rule as a Trace Formula — Derivation and Comparison develops and bounds; the present article only locates it among the structures.
  • The spinor and the qubit are one carrier read twice. The reading is that the Weyl spinor of the Lorentz action and the two-level state of the informational sector inhabit one complex plane, once as the defining module and once as the Hilbert space. Boundary: the carrier is one; the physics of the two readings is not thereby identified.
  • Two forms, two slots. The reading is that the coexistence of the invariant symplectic form and the Hermitian form on the module is the two-slot mechanism of the algebra, the bilinear form on the algebra side and the sesquilinear form on the sesqualgebra side. Boundary: an organisation of known pairings, and it adds no new invariant.
  • Quantisation is the second slot, read locally. The reading is that the step from the plain product to the plain sesquilinear product — the corpus's quantisation — is exactly the step from the module structure to the Hilbert structure, so that the spinor space is compositional before the step and probabilistic after it. Boundary: the algebra supplies the pairing and no equation of motion and no measure, so the reading is a shape and not a derivation (The Quantum–Relativity Tension and the Biquaternion Framework).
  • The spin is a module datum, the ray is a Hilbert datum. The reading is that the double cover acts faithfully on the module, which is where spin-$\tfrac12$ lives, while the Hilbert structure is blind to the sign of a vector and yields the ray, which is where the state lives. Boundary: a separation of two known facts by the two structures, and it adds no new representation.
  • States are Hilbert, observables are module endomorphisms. The reading is that the framework's state-observable pair is a pair of structures, $\mathbb{B}\cong\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(S)$ acting on the Hilbert space $S$, so the observables are the module's endomorphisms and the trace formula is the pairing of the two. Boundary: the identification of the two readings of $S$ — spinor and qubit — is not made; only the algebra of the pair is stated.

Summary

The spinor space $S=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_1=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Upsilon_2\}$ of the biquaternion algebra carries two structures, and they belong to two different products of the space. It is a left module of the general plain algebra, the algebra of the plain bilinear product $\tilde P\tilde Q$: the product makes $S$ a minimal left ideal, an idempotent needs an associative product with a unit to be one, and the plain product is the only one of the four that is associative and unital, so the Peirce decomposition, the ideals and the module structure are facts about the plain product alone. It is a Hilbert space of the general plain sesqualgebra, the structure of the plain sesquilinear product $\tilde P\tilde Q^{*}$: the scalar form $\langle u,v\rangle_*=\mathrm{Sc}(uv^{*})$ restricts to the basis $(\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Upsilon_2)$ with Gram matrix $G=\tfrac12 I_2$ and is positive definite, while the plain bilinear form $B$ is indefinite on the real space, of signature $(4,4)$, so positivity needs the second slot read through ${}^{*}$ and is a fact about the sesquilinear product alone.

The two jobs are the two menu labels: the module is the group of composition and carries the Lorentz action and the double cover; the Hilbert space is the group of probability and carries the norm, the normalisation and the Born rule. On the four products tested against the four elements, only the plain product carries the whole module package, and neither sesquilinear product preserves the carrier at all, since it pairs the space rather than acting on it. This is the structural reason the module and the Hilbert space are two structures and not two views of one object.

The Gram matrix $G=\tfrac12 I_2$ makes the amplitudes the two coefficients of the two basis spinors, with $\langle\tilde\psi,\tilde\psi\rangle_*=\tfrac12(|\psi_1|^2+|\psi_2|^2)$, so the state space reads as $\mathbb{C}^2$ with its standard inner product for an algebraic reason. The split explains why the Born rule is a trace pairing — the probability structure is the scalar part of a product — and why a bilinear and a sesquilinear form coexist on one carrier, the symplectic form on the algebra side and the Hermitian form on the sesqualgebra side. The qubit and the spinor are one carrier read twice; that the physics identifies the two readings is not claimed.
