The Two Masses Above $M_C$: One Traceless Block, One Trace and No Third

Introduction

The question. Rows $4$ and $5$ are the only masses the phase carries. Why two, what does each of them give a mass to, and why is there no third?

The answer.

$$ \boxed{\;-\mu_{\Phi}^{2}\operatorname{tr}\Xi^{2}-\mu_{2}^{2}\bigl(\operatorname{tr}\Xi\bigr)^{2} \;\Longrightarrow\; m^{2}=\mu_{\Phi}^{2}\ \text{on the fifteen},\qquad m^{2}=\mu_{\Phi}^{2}+4\mu_{2}^{2}\ \text{on the trace}.\;} $$

Two numbers, two blocks, and the second block is one real field. The fifteen traceless directions are degenerate at $\mu_{\Phi}^{2}$; the trace is split away by $4\mu_{2}^{2}$, and the four is $\operatorname{tr}\mathbb 1$.

And there is no third because the two are the whole of degree two. The invariant ring of a Hermitian $4\times4$ matrix has exactly two quadratic monomials — $\operatorname{tr}\Xi^{2}$ and $(\operatorname{tr}\Xi)^{2}$ — and the scalar article counts them as the $2$ in $1,2,3,5$. A third quadratic would have to be built from the same two.

What this article is not about. These are masses of the phase and not of the broken one: no field has a mass term with a vacuum expectation in it, no $W$ or $Z$ appears, and $\mu_{\Phi}^{2}$ is not $m_{h}^{2}$. The masses of the six leptoquarks and the nine massless connections belong to the restructuring.

The Two Rows

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{quad}}=-\mu_{\Phi}^{2}\operatorname{tr}\Xi^{2}-\mu_{2}^{2}\bigl(\operatorname{tr}\Xi\bigr)^{2}. $$

Both signs are negative and both coefficients are positive above $M_C$. A positive coefficient with a negative sign gives a minimum at $\Xi=0$, which is the symmetric phase; a negative coefficient would give a maximum and there would be no phase to describe at all. The region is $\mu_{\Phi}^{2}>0$ and $\mu_{2}^{2}>0$, the primordial potential gives the cone and the branch where a sign flips, and the region is a region and not a tuning: every point in it is a phase.

The rows are one quadratic form read in two blocks, and the decomposition is orthogonal. With $s\equiv\operatorname{tr}\Xi$ and $\Xi_{0}=\Xi-\tfrac14 s\,\mathbb 1$,

$$ \operatorname{tr}\Xi^{2}=\operatorname{tr}\Xi_{0}^{2}+\tfrac14 s^{2}, \qquad \bigl(\operatorname{tr}\Xi\bigr)^{2}=s^{2}. $$

There is no cross term, because $\operatorname{tr}\Xi_{0}=0$: the traceless part and the trace are orthogonal under the trace form. So the quadratic form is already block-diagonal and the two masses need no diagonalisation — the fifteen are degenerate, the trace is one field, and the two blocks do not mix.

One Form, Two Blocks, and Where the Four Comes From

The fifteen. With $\Xi_{0}=\chi_{a}t_{a}$ and $\operatorname{tr}(t_{a}t_{b})=\tfrac12\delta_{ab}$, $\operatorname{tr}\Xi_{0}^{2}=\tfrac12\sum_{a}\chi_{a}^{2}$, so the potential is $-\tfrac12\mu_{\Phi}^{2}\sum\chi_{a}^{2}$ and the mass squared is $\mu_{\Phi}^{2}$ on all fifteen. The degeneracy is exact and it is the group's: fifteen directions at one number is what a single invariant of degree two means, and any split of the fifteen would need a second structure that this phase's potential does not have.

The trace. Along $\Xi=c\,\mathbb 1$ the two monomials are in the ratio

$$ \frac{\bigl(\operatorname{tr}\Xi\bigr)^{2}}{\operatorname{tr}\Xi^{2}}\Bigg|_{\Xi=c\mathbb 1}=\frac{(4c)^{2}}{4c^{2}}=4=\operatorname{tr}\mathbb 1 , $$

which is the origin of the four in $\mu_{\Phi}^{2}+4\mu_{2}^{2}$: the trace monomial counts the four lines squared, the quadratic counts them once.

The factor of two that lives in the coordinate, not in the physics. The free parts shows $\operatorname{tr}(\partial\Xi)^{2}=\tfrac12\sum(\partial\chi_{a})^{2}+\tfrac14(\partial s)^{2}$, so the canonical trace field is $\tau=s/\sqrt2$ and not $s$. In $\tau$ the quadratic potential is

$$ -\Bigl(\tfrac{\mu_{\Phi}^{2}}{4}+\mu_{2}^{2}\Bigr)s^{2} =-\tfrac12\bigl(\mu_{\Phi}^{2}+4\mu_{2}^{2}\bigr)\tau^{2}, $$

so the physical mass on the trace is $\mu_{\Phi}^{2}+4\mu_{2}^{2}$ and the Hessian in the $s$ coordinate is half of it. The two statements do not disagree; one is a mass and the other is a second derivative with respect to a field that is not canonically normalised, and a reader who differentiates the potential in $s$ and finds $\mu_{\Phi}^{2}/2+2\mu_{2}^{2}$ has found the coordinate.

Why two numbers and not one. If $\mu_{2}^{2}=0$ the trace joins the fifteen and the scalar is a degenerate sixteen at one mass. The ratio $\mu_{2}^{2}/\mu_{\Phi}^{2}$ is the phase's one dimensionless number in the scalar sector, and it is a parameter and not a convention: no field redefinition removes it, the canonical basis moving both masses together rather than their ratio.

The Vector Mass That Cannot Be Written

A mass term for a connection is invariant under the group and not under the gauge. $\operatorname{tr}A_{\mu}A^{\mu}$ is untouched by $A\mapsto UAU^{\dagger}$, and destroyed by $A\mapsto UAU^{\dagger}+\tfrac{i}{g}U\partial_{\mu}U^{\dagger}$: the cross term is linear in the derivative and is not a total derivative. So the obstruction is locality and not invariance, and that distinction is the whole reason a massive vector needs a scalar.

Which is why the mass of the fifteen is a broken-phase object with a vacuum in it. Below $M_C$ the mass form is

$$ m^{2}(X)=v^{2}\operatorname{tr}\bigl(X^{\dagger}[D,[D,X]]\bigr), $$

one number for the six that feel the vacuum and zero for the nine that do not, and the $32v^{2}$ of the six is the boson classification's. Above $M_C$ there is no $v$, so there is no such form: all fifteen are massless, exactly and not approximately, and the row is absent because invariance forbids it rather than because the corpus declined it.

The Fermion Mass That Is Declined

A fermion mass is invariant and is not written. The content is $2\cdot\mathbf4\oplus2\cdot\bar{\mathbf4}$, vector-like, so $\bar{\mathbf4}\otimes\mathbf4$ contains a singlet and $m_{\alpha\beta}\bar\xi^{\alpha}\xi^{\beta}$ exists; it is row $12$, its coefficient is zero, and the zero is a decline. The boundary gives the warrant: the phase has no vacuum, and the masses are made below $M_C$ by the Yukawa matrix.

The two zeros are not the same zero and this is the place they are easiest to confuse. The vector mass is a zero that invariance forces — no coefficient would make it a row. The fermion mass is a zero the author chooses — every coefficient would make it a row, and the choice buys a Yukawa sector with one matrix index instead of a bare mass with none.

What the decline costs. The sixteen are massless and the phase has no flavour structure at all: no mixing matrix, no phase, no hierarchy, and the Yukawa block is where all eight numbers wait. The decline is the reason the category has a Yukawa article and would otherwise not need one.

No Direction Among the Fifteen

The quadratic form is built from the trace, so it is invariant under the whole of $\mathrm{SU}(4)$ and distinguishes no generator. That is the same statement as the absence of a vector mass, written on the scalar side: a potential with one number on the fifteen cannot prefer a direction, because preferring one would need a second invariant carrying an index.

The direction exists in the algebra and is invisible to the Lagrangian. The corner

$$ iD=\operatorname{diag}(-3i,i,i,i),\qquad \operatorname{tr}D^{2}=12, $$

is the traceless part of the symmetric sum of the three diagonal generators — the three diagonal words $iM_{x},iM_{y},iM_{z}$ of the fifteen, whose sum is diagonal with one line distinguished, up to the trace that is removed and the factor the sum carries. Its norm $12$ is the number that reappears as the vacuum direction's trace norm, and it is the direction the restructuring will pick.

The free parts cannot name it. A massless connection propagates in every direction of the adjoint equally, and the degeneracy of the fifteen at $\mu_{\Phi}^{2}$ is the scalar image of the same fact. So this article stops at the algebra: the direction becomes an object only at $M_C$, where a vacuum sets one component of $\Xi$ and the fifteen split into nine and six. Above $M_C$ the number $12$ is a property of an array, not a mass.

What the Two Masses Are Not

They are not $v$ and not $m_{h}$. $\mu_{\Phi}^{2}$ is a mass squared of a scalar that has no expectation value; $m_{h}^{2}=2\mu^{2}$ is a broken-phase object with a different field content and a different normalisation.

They are not tunings. The symmetric phase needs both positive, and every positive pair is a symmetric phase; the tuning question belongs to the branch where one of them crosses zero, which is the restructuring's.

And there is no tadpole to cancel. A linear term $c\operatorname{tr}\Xi$ is row $13$ with a zero coefficient, and the zero is forced: a linear term is not stationary at $\Xi=0$, so a symmetric vacuum sets it to zero rather than choosing it. A mass row and a tadpole row are different kinds of row, and the census separates them for this reason.

Summary

Two masses, one quadratic form, two blocks. Fifteen traceless directions at $\mu_{\Phi}^{2}$, degenerate by the group; the trace at $\mu_{\Phi}^{2}+4\mu_{2}^{2}$, split by $4=\operatorname{tr}\mathbb 1$; the canonical trace field is $\operatorname{tr}\Xi/\sqrt2$ and in the unnormalised coordinate the Hessian is half the physical mass.

No third mass exists and two are absent for different reasons. The vector mass is forbidden by local invariance — the quadratic form is globally invariant, which is why a scalar is needed — and the fermion mass is declined, with row $12$ carrying the zero.

And no direction is distinguished among the fifteen, which is the same fact as the missing vector mass, and which is why the corner $iD$ at trace norm $12$ is an array above $M_C$ and a vacuum below it.
