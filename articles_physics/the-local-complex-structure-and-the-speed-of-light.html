The Local Complex Structure and the Speed of Light

Introduction

The biquaternion framework reads the Lorentzian signature of spacetime as a consequence of a complex structure rather than as an independent postulate. The minus sign in the Minkowski interval $$ ds^2=-d{r'_{t}}^2+dr_x^2+dr_y^2+dr_z^2 $$ is, in this interpretation, the sign of $i^2$; it is not put into the metric by hand. That much is inherited. The interval is written in the length coordinate, where the four coefficients are lengths and the metric is the unit metric; the same interval in seconds is $ds^2=-c^2\,dt'^2+dr_x^2+dr_y^2+dr_z^2$, and the coefficient $c^2$ appears with the seconds and not before. This article isolates the structure that does the work and asks what, precisely, it is a structure on. The answer turns on a distinction that is easy to blur: no global complex structure compatible with the Minkowski metric is available on spacetime, but a pointwise one is — on the complexified tangent space, with a locally chosen temporal unit, and the speed of light $c$ is the single conversion that carries the local structure into seconds and there produces the Minkowski metric.

The parent of this article is Electromagnetism in Media — The Local Complex Structure at Work, where the local structure is used in a material medium. There the medium supplies the general case and the vacuum is its limit. Here the subject is the structure itself and its relation to the speed of light; the medium appears only as the setting in which the local structure is seen to vary from point to point. The distinction between the global and the local statement is the organising theme, and every step below says which of the two is being claimed.

Throughout, the notation is inherited unchanged from the read-list articles. The algebra is $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_\mathbb{R}\mathbb{H}$, with basis $e_t=1,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z$ satisfying $e_t^2=e_t$ and $\vec e_\eta^2=-e_t$ for $\eta=x,y,z$, and scalar imaginary $i$ commuting with the quaternion units. The two complementary four-dimensional real subspaces are $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ (anti-Hermitian: imaginary scalar, real vector; the material sector) and $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ (Hermitian: real scalar, imaginary vector; the informational sector), with $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}\oplus\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$. The real-quaternion subspace is $\mathbb{B}_{H}$, the scalar subspace is $\mathbb{B}_{C}$, and the trace formula is $\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2)=2\,\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2)$. The biquaternion norm is $\langle\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural}=\tilde{Q}\tilde{Q}^{\natural}=\sum_\mu Q_\mu^2$. The speed of light in the medium is $c=1/\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}$ and the vacuum speed is $c_0=1/\sqrt{\epsilon_0\mu_0}$; in vacuum $c=c_0$. Two symbols are introduced here: $J$ denotes an abstract complex structure, and $J_{\mathbb{B}}$ the specific map $\tilde{Q}\mapsto i\tilde{Q}$ on $\mathbb{B}$.

Two Senses of "Complex Structure"

A complex structure on a real vector space $V$ is a real-linear map $J:V\to V$ with $$ J^2=-I . $$ Its effect is to make $V$ a complex vector space: defining $(a+bi)v=av+b\,Jv$ gives a product that is complex-linear in the scalar $a+bi$. A complex structure therefore forces $\dim_{\mathbb R}V$ to be even; on $\mathbb R^4$ it is the algebraic content of simultaneous rotations by $90^\circ$ in two independent planes.

The biquaternion algebra carries one such map, and it is part of the algebra rather than a choice imposed on it: left multiplication by the scalar imaginary, $$ J_{\mathbb{B}}:\tilde{Q}\longmapsto i\,\tilde{Q}, \qquad J_{\mathbb{B}}^2=-e_t . $$ This $J_{\mathbb{B}}$ is defined on all of $\mathbb{B}$ and is the same everywhere. On the eight-real-dimensional algebra it is a genuine complex structure, global in the strongest sense: it depends on no medium and no frame, and it is exactly the $i$ of the $i r'_t$ convention.

The question of this article is whether $J_{\mathbb{B}}$ — or any other complex structure — is a complex structure on spacetime. Three distinct levels must be kept apart, because a statement at one level is easily mistaken for a statement at another.

  1. The algebra $\mathbb{B}$. Here $J_{\mathbb{B}}$ is a complex structure, globally and exactly.
  2. Spacetime, the material sector $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$. This is a four-dimensional real vector space carrying the biquaternion norm of signature $(3,1)$. Here the question is whether a complex structure exists that is compatible with that form.
  3. The complexified local tangent space. Here multiplication by $i$ is again a complex structure, but on a larger space; it is not a structure on the real tangent space.

A local statement can be true while the corresponding global statement is false, and the two are not interchangeable. The next two sections separate the levels.

Why the Global Structure Is Not Available

The algebra's complex structure does not preserve spacetime

The material sector $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ consists of the elements with imaginary scalar part and real vector part, so a real basis is $\{ie_t,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z\}$. Multiplication by $i$ exchanges the two sectors: $$ i\,\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}=\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}, \qquad i\,\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}=\mathbb{B}_{\Phi} . $$ A general element $i q_te_t+\vec q$ of $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ is sent to $-q_te_t+i\vec q$, which has a real scalar part and an imaginary vector part, hence lies in $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ and not in $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ unless it vanishes. Therefore $J_{\mathbb{B}}$ is not an endomorphism of $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$: it does not map spacetime to itself, so it cannot be a complex structure on spacetime. Geometrically it is the map that exchanges the material and informational sectors.

The Minkowski metric admits no compatible complex structure

The second obstruction is independent of the algebra and concerns any real complex structure on the material sector. Suppose a real-linear $J$ on $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ satisfied both $J^2=-I$ and the compatibility $$ \langle J\tilde{Q},J\tilde Q_3\rangle_{\natural}=\langle\tilde{Q},\tilde Q_3\rangle_{\natural} \qquad \text{for all } \tilde{Q},\tilde Q_3, $$ which is the condition that the complex structure be an isometry of the biquaternion norm. Two elementary identities follow. Taking $\tilde Q_3=J\tilde{Q}$ and using $J^2=-I$ and the symmetry of $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\natural}$, $$ \langle J\tilde{Q},J^2\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural}=\langle\tilde{Q},J\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural} \;\Longrightarrow\; -\langle\tilde{Q},J\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural}=\langle\tilde{Q},J\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural} \;\Longrightarrow\; \langle\tilde{Q},J\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural}=0 , $$ so a vector and its image under $J$ are orthogonal. Taking $\tilde Q_3=\tilde{Q}$, $$ \langle J\tilde{Q},J\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural}=\langle\tilde{Q},\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural}. $$ Hence on the two-dimensional plane $\mathrm{span}\{\tilde{Q},J\tilde{Q}\}$, whenever $\langle\tilde{Q},\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural}\neq0$, the form is definite with the sign of $\langle\tilde{Q},\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural}$: the plane has signature $(2,0)$ or $(0,2)$, never $(1,1)$. The orthogonal complement of a $J$-invariant plane is itself $J$-invariant, because for $\tilde Q_3$ orthogonal to the plane and $Z$ in the plane, $$ \langle J\tilde Q_3,Z\rangle_{\natural}=\langle J^2\tilde Q_3,JZ\rangle_{\natural}=-\langle\tilde Q_3,JZ\rangle_{\natural}=0, $$ using compatibility in the first equality and $JZ$ in the plane in the last. Iterating, a four-dimensional space is an orthogonal direct sum of two such planes, so its signature is $(4,0)$, $(2,2)$ or $(0,4)$. The biquaternion norm of $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ has signature $(3,1)$: three positive directions and one negative. It is not in the list. Therefore no complex structure compatible with the Minkowski metric exists, neither globally nor at a single point.

The obstruction is easy to check directly. In two dimensions, writing $g=\begin{pmatrix}p&q\\q&r\end{pmatrix}$ and $J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, the condition $J^{\mathsf T}gJ=g$ forces $p=r$ and $q=0$: the compatible form is a multiple of the identity, hence definite, of signature $(2,0)$ or $(0,2)$, never $(1,1)$. In four dimensions the forms compatible with a complex structure have eigenvalues each of multiplicity two, so each sign occurs an even number of times — the signature is $(4,0)$, $(2,2)$ or $(0,4)$; a symbolic solution of $J_0^{\mathsf T}GJ_0=G$ for the standard $J_0$ returns a form whose eigenvalues each occur twice. A count of three positives and one negative cannot arise, and the signature $(3,1)$ is exactly the excluded case.

The way out is not to look for a better $J$ but to change the space: complexify. On the complexified tangent space the form is complex, and multiplication by $i$ is a complex structure there. This is what the $i r'_t$ notation does, and it is why the framework complexifies spacetime rather than placing a real $J$ on it. The distinction is not pedantic: a complex structure on the complexified tangent space is a different object from a complex structure on spacetime compatible with the Minkowski metric, and the second does not exist. Calling $i r'_t$ "a complex structure on spacetime" is the conflation this article is written to avoid.

The global $i r'_t$ is a coordinate device

The $i r'_t$ convention is often described as making the complex structure global. It is worth being exact about what is global there. The substitution $x_0=i r'_t$ is a change of coordinates, and it converts the interval into a sum of four squares, $$ ds^2=(i\,dr'_t)^2+dr_x^2+dr_y^2+dr_z^2 . $$ The display is then the same at every point and in every medium: the four coefficients are lengths, and no factor stands inside the parentheses. What is not generally available is the unit of the temporal coordinate. The corpus writes $r'_t=c\,t'$, with $c$ the local speed of light at the point of the element, so a metre of $r'_t$ is the distance light travels in $1/c$ seconds at that point, and the identification of the physical time axis with the imaginary axis is pointwise because the conversion is. Flat spacetime with a fixed inertial frame supplies a single conversion, which is why the convention works in the vacuum of special relativity. It fails for a curved metric, it sits awkwardly with spinors, and — the case that matters here — it fails when the speed is a field rather than a constant: in a medium with $\epsilon=\epsilon(r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z)$ and $\mu=\mu(r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z)$ one has $c=c(r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z)$ and $t'=r'_t/c$, defined only pointwise. The global $i r'_t$ is therefore the constant-$c$, flat-space limit of a local structure; it is not a global complex structure on spacetime. The companion article Why Complexify Spacetime? records the first two limitations.

The Local Structure That Is Available

What is available at every point is the following. The material tangent space at a point has the real basis $\{ie_t,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z\}$. Choose the temporal direction of the local frame and map it to the imaginary scalar generator by $$ \boxed{\;r'_t \;\longmapsto\; i r'_t\,e_t\;}, $$ the map carrying no factor, because $r'_t$ is a length as $r_x$ is. The unit is what the point supplies: $r'_t=c\,t'$ with $c$ the local speed of light, so that a metre of the imaginary axis is the light-travel metre of the medium at that point — and in a dispersive medium $c(\omega)$ sets it per frequency. This is the local complex structure: the phase is the algebra's fixed $i$, and the unit in which the temporal axis is measured is local.

Three features of this definition are the content of its locality.

  • The phase does not vary. The map $r'_t\mapsto i r'_t$ is the same at every point and in every medium; it is an identification of the imaginary axis, not a rotation of it, and the structure does not point in different directions in different places. What varies is the unit, and a unit is a real positive number. This is the precise sense, used in the parent article, in which the structure is local: the algebra is fixed, and the reading of physical time against the imaginary axis carries the local factor $t'=r'_t/c(r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z)$.
  • It is pointwise, not holomorphic. The local structure is an identification on the local tangent space at each point. It is not a system of holomorphic coordinates and it does not make spacetime a complex manifold, since by the previous section no such structure exists. In flat spacetime with a constant conversion the pointwise identifications fit together and the structure becomes global; in general they do not.
  • The scale sits in the unit, not in the map. The map takes a length to a length and needs no scale: nothing dimensional is supplied by $i$, and the statement "the temporal direction is imaginary" is already a statement about lengths. What needs a scale is the comparison against a clock, $t'=r'_t/c$, and that is the only place $c$ enters. The local complex structure is therefore a structure whose coordinate carries the scale; the scale is not a free parameter of the embedding.

At each point the local structure induces the quadratic form of the material sector. With $d\tilde R_\Phi=i\,dr'_t\,e_t+d(r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z)$, $$ \boxed{\;\langle d\tilde R_\Phi\rangle_{\natural}=(i\,dr'_t)^2+dr_x^2+dr_y^2+dr_z^2=-d{r'_{t}}^2+dr_x^2+dr_y^2+dr_z^2\;}, $$ the unit form on the four coefficients. The signature is produced by the complex structure and needs no scale: the minus sign is $i^2$. The scale appears at the next step only, when the temporal slot is read in seconds. With $dr'_t=c\,dt'$ the same form is $-c^2\,dt'^2+dr_x^2+dr_y^2+dr_z^2$, and $c^2$ is the coefficient of $dt'^2$ — the metric in seconds, with $g_{tt}=-c^2$ and the local $c$ as its one varying entry.

The Scale $c$ and the Minkowski Metric

The sentence "the complex structure produces the metric" has two parts, and it is worth separating them.

  1. The signature is algebraic. The sign of the coefficient on $d{r'_{t}}^2$ is $(i)^2$. It comes from the scalar imaginary and is the same at every point and in every medium, whatever the conversion is. In the metric formalism the signature is an input; here it is a consequence of the algebra.
  2. The magnitude is the conversion. In the length coordinate the coefficient is $-1$, and the metric is the unit metric $\delta_{\mu\nu}=(1,1,1,1)$ on the four $Q_\mu$: $$ ds^2=\sum_{\mu,\nu}\delta_{\mu\nu}\,dR_\mu dQ_\nu=(i\,dr'_t)^2+dr_x^2+dr_y^2+dr_z^2=-d{r'_{t}}^2+dr_x^2+dr_y^2+dr_z^2 . $$ The coefficient becomes $-c^2$ when the temporal slot is read in seconds, by $dr'_t=c\,dt'$.

So $c$ is the conversion factor between the temporal length the algebra carries and the seconds a clock reads. The phase $i$ says that the temporal direction is the imaginary one; the conversion $r'_t=c\,t'$ says how many seconds correspond to a metre of it, and therefore how the temporal axis is measured against the three spatial directions. Given the phase and the conversion, the metric in seconds is determined; the unit form needs neither.

For constant $c$ the unit form $-d{r'_{t}}^2+d\vec r^2$ has no factor in it, and writing $dr'_t=c\,dt'$ gives $c^2\left(-dt'^2+(dr_x^2+dr_y^2+dr_z^2)/c^2\right)$, which is $c^2$ times the Minkowski metric in the coordinates $(t',\vec r/c)$: the null structure is Minkowski's and the coordinate speed of light in seconds is $c$. For varying $c$ the unit form does not change — it is the form of the algebra and holds at every point — and the variation sits in the conversion, $$ ds^2=(i\,dr'_t)^2+dr_x^2+dr_y^2+dr_z^2=-d{r'_{t}}^2+dr_x^2+dr_y^2+dr_z^2=-c(r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z)^2\,dt'^2+dr_x^2+dr_y^2+dr_z^2 , $$ so that the null directions in seconds are those with $|d(r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z)/dt'|=c(r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z)$, while in the length coordinate they are the fixed $|d(r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z)/dr'_t|=1$. The metric in seconds is a field of local forms; the form in the length coordinate is one form. Neither is postulated alongside the complex structure: the first is the algebra, the second the algebra plus the local conversion.

Two cautions belong here. First, this is not a claim that a medium produces the Gordon-type effective metric of a moving dielectric. The local structure considered here is isotropic and is written in the local rest frame of the material; the constitutive relations of a moving or anisotropic medium carry more data than the local complex structure, and their geometric interpretation is a separate question. Second, the field of local forms is obtained pointwise. Nothing global is asserted about it: there is no claim that the pointwise forms are the components of a single smooth metric in a preferred global frame, nor that a global Lorentz invariance survives the variation of $c$. That last point is taken up again below.

Two Readings of the One Conversion

The local structure fixes the phase and leaves the unit open. The physics closes it, and two independent readings give the same unit. Neither is a derivation of the numerical value: each states what has to be true of the unit for the algebra and the medium to describe one light cone.

Route 1: the null cone of the biquaternion norm. The local quadratic form vanishes on $$ (i\,dr'_t)^2+dr_x^2+dr_y^2+dr_z^2=0 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; |d(r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z)|=|dr'_t| , $$ the $45^\circ$ cone of the length coordinate. These null directions are the zero divisors of the algebra at the point, and they are the local light cone. Read in seconds, with $dr'_t=c\,dt'$, the same cone is $$ |d(r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z)/dt'|=c , $$ so the aperture of the light cone in seconds is exactly $c$. The requirement is that the zero-divisor cone of the algebra be the cone of propagating signals; the conversion that meets it is the one whose metre of $r'_t$ is a metre of light travel. The null-cone route uses the quadratic form and the definition of $r'_t$, and nothing else.

Route 2: the characteristics of the wave operator. The biquaternionic gradient and its quaternion conjugate multiply to the d'Alembertian, $$ \Box_\Phi=\tilde\nabla_\Phi(\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}=\Delta_\Phi-\partial^2/\partial {r'_{t}}^2 , $$ since $\partial/\partial R_t=-i\,\partial/\partial r'_t$. Its characteristics in the length coordinate are the same $45^\circ$ cone. Maxwell's equations in a linear medium reduce to the same operator, $$ \Box_{em}=\Delta_\Phi-\partial^2/\partial {r'_{t}}^2 , \qquad c_{em}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}}, $$ and plane waves read in seconds satisfy $k_\Phi^2=\omega^2\epsilon\mu$, with $k_\Phi$ the momentum wave number of the medium, so the characteristic speed of the medium's operator, read in seconds, is $c_{em}$. The structure's operator and the medium's are one operator in the length coordinate; the single conversion that makes the second read as $c_{em}$ in seconds is $dr'_t=c_{em}\,dt'$, that is $$ \boxed{\;c=c_{em}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}}\;} $$ in the seconds writing. The wave-operator route uses the field equation and the constitutive relations, and nothing about the null cone of the biquaternion norm.

Independence and agreement. Route 1 is geometric: it reads the unit off a quadratic form. Route 2 is dynamical: it reads the same unit off a differential operator and the medium's constitutive relations. Neither determines the numerical value of $c$ — that is empirical, as it is in the metric formalism — but together they fix what $c$ is for: it is at once the conversion that sets the temporal unit and the characteristic speed of the medium's electrodynamics, and there is exactly one number doing both jobs. Neither reading leaves $c$ free once the medium is given, and neither produces the number. In the metric formalism one writes $-c^2dt'^2$ and, separately, $\vec D=\epsilon\vec E$ and $\vec B=\mu\vec H$; the appearance of the same $c$ in both places is a consistency to be checked. Here it is a single conversion with a single role.

Limits of the Local Reading

Four boundaries should be stated, so that the local claim is not mistaken for a global one.

  1. No empirical content is added. The algebraicity of the signature and the identity $c=1/\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}$ are both known. The local structure is a repackaging of them, not a new prediction, and it does not distinguish the framework from standard electromagnetism in a medium. Whether the framework has any empirical consequence remains the open question it is elsewhere in the corpus.
  2. The conversion must be real. In a transparent frequency window $c(\omega)$ is real and the conversion $t'=r'_t/c$ is a real rescaling of the time axis. Near an absorption line $\epsilon$ and $\mu$ become complex, so $c$ becomes complex, and the conversion becomes a complex rotation rather than a rescaling; the unit can no longer be read as a duration, and the cone of the algebra no longer has a real aperture in seconds. This is a boundary of the structure as written, recorded in the parent article and not repaired here.
  3. Only the pointwise Lorentz invariance survives when $c$ varies. For constant $c$ the form $-d{r'_{t}}^2+d\vec r^2$ is invariant under the full Lorentz group acting on $(r'_t,r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z)$, and that invariance is global. For $c=c(r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z)$ the form itself is unchanged — it is the algebra's and holds at every point — so the coordinate invariance is still exact, but a constant Lorentz transformation moves the field point, and the medium is a fixed function of the coordinates: the transformed configuration is a different medium, not the same one seen from another frame. What remains a symmetry of the physical problem is the pointwise invariance at each point, the local Lorentz group of the tangent space. No global Lorentz invariance of a variable medium is claimed here. This is the same local/global caution in group-theoretic form.
  4. Curved spacetime is a further step. The structure described here is a tangent-space statement at a point. Its extension to a curved manifold requires a tetrad or spin connection, and it is treated separately in the corpus; nothing in the present article assumes or establishes that extension.

Physical Readings

The two senses of complex structure read as two physical claims. Globally, the complex structure of the algebra is fixed and no physics follows from it, which is the article's negative interpretation. Locally, the structure is the medium's: $c$ is the conversion of the temporal unit, a change of the local structure moves the interval in seconds and the composition and leaves the probability form and the zero-divisor cone, and the cone of the algebra is therefore the invariant against which the physical wave cone is compared (Algebra and Element Conventions, Electromagnetism in Media — The Local Complex Structure at Work).

The medium interpretation extends in three directions. First, $\log\lvert\langle\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural}\rvert$ is read as a conformal (Weyl) factor: the norm is a scale and its logarithm a potential for a dilation, so the framework's scale is a conformal weight and its massless limit a conformal limit — the interpretation is the shape of Conformal Invariance and the Massless Limit in Biquaternionic Form, cited. Second, the local complex structure with $r'_t=c\,t'$ and $c=1/\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}$ is read as an emergent metric, the algebraic form of the analogue-gravity programme in which the wave cone is a medium property and not a fixed background. Third, the construction is read as a bundle of algebras: the biquaternion algebra is the typical fibre, the local complex structure a section of the frame bundle, and a medium a connection on it — the interpretation the series' Curved Spacetime and the Biquaternion Framework poses as a question and does not claim.

Summary

The Lorentzian signature of spacetime is algebraic in the biquaternion framework: the minus sign in $ds^2=-d{r'_{t}}^2+dr_x^2+dr_y^2+dr_z^2$ is the sign of $i^2$. What this article has separated is the sense in which the complex structure that produces it is, and is not, available.

A global complex structure on spacetime, in the sense the question requires — an endomorphism of $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ compatible with its biquaternion norm — is not available, on two independent grounds. First, the algebra's complex structure $J_{\mathbb{B}}:\tilde{Q}\mapsto i\tilde{Q}$ exchanges the two sectors, $i\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}=\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$, so it is not an endomorphism of the material sector. Second, and independently of the algebra, no real complex structure compatible with the Minkowski metric exists at all: compatibility with $J^2=-I$ forces the metric to be an orthogonal sum of definite two-planes, in which each sign occurs an even number of times, while the biquaternion norm of $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ has signature $(3,1)$. The $i r'_t$ convention is not a counterexample; it is a complexification of the coordinates, a different object, and its global use is limited to flat spacetime with a constant conversion.

A local structure is available, and it is the following pointwise data: the temporal direction is identified with the imaginary scalar generator with no factor in the map, $r'_t\mapsto i r'_t e_t$, and the unit of the temporal coordinate is local, $r'_t=c(r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z)\,t'$ — and $c(\omega)$ in a dispersive medium. The phase is the algebra's fixed $i$; only the unit varies, and because $c$ is real and positive the variation is a rescaling of the time axis and not a rotation of it. At each point the induced quadratic form is the unit form $-d{r'_{t}}^2+dr_x^2+dr_y^2+dr_z^2$; read in seconds it is $-c^2dt'^2+dr_x^2+dr_y^2+dr_z^2$, the local Minkowski metric with $c$ as its one varying entry.

The number $c$ is read in two places and the two agree. In the length coordinate the null cone of the biquaternion norm is the $45^\circ$ cone $|d(r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z)/dr'_t|=1$; in seconds it has aperture $c$. The characteristic speed of the local wave operator $\Box_\Phi=\Delta_\Phi-\partial^2/\partial {r'_{t}}^2$, read in seconds, must equal $c_{em}=1/\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}$ of the medium's Maxwell equations, so $c=c_{em}$. Neither reading derives the number; together they show that one number serves as the temporal unit and as the characteristic speed. The numerical value of $c$ remains empirical, as in the metric formalism; what is fixed is its role.

The interpretation closes no empirical gap and claims no global structure. Its content is that the signature is algebra, the unit of the temporal axis is local, and the one conversion is the speed of light.
