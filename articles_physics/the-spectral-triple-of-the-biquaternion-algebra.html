The Spectral Triple of the Biquaternion Algebra

Introduction

A spectral triple is a triple $(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{H},D)$ consisting of an algebra $\mathcal{A}$ represented on a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$ together with a self-adjoint operator $D$ whose resolvent is compact and whose commutators with the algebra are bounded. The triple is the object from which Connes' non-commutative geometry recovers the data of a Riemannian spin manifold: the algebra generalises the functions, the Hilbert space generalises the fields on which those functions act, and the Dirac operator generalises both the metric and the differential structure. Two consequences of the definition are worth stating at once, because they are the reason the construction is of interest to this series: the distance on the state space is recovered from $D$ by a supremum over the algebra, and the bosonic fields, including the gauge fields, appear as inner fluctuations of $D$, $D\mapsto D+\sum a_i[D,b_i]$. The construction and its axioms are standard (Connes 1995; Connes and Marcolli 2008).

This article asks whether the biquaternion algebra $\mathbb{B}$ supports such a triple, and what the framework's structures contribute to it. The scalar imaginary of the algebra satisfies $i^2=-1$ throughout; it is the central unit of the complexification, not a quaternion unit. The findings are the following.

  1. The algebra, the Hilbert space and the Dirac operator are already present. The framework's algebra is $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_\mathbb{R}\mathbb{H}$. The field is an element $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}$ of the algebra, and its chirality is a subscript with two values, $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}=\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}+\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}$; only the zero element has both parts zero, so the notation costs nothing. The Hilbert space is the algebra completed in the Hermitian form $\langle\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}},\tilde{Ξ}\rangle_*=\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}^*\tilde{Ξ})$, and in the field-theoretic setting it is the square-integrable fields on spacetime. The operator is the framework's Dirac operator, $$ \mathcal{D}=i\tilde\nabla+D_F, $$ with $\tilde\nabla=\sum_\mu e_\mu\,\partial/\partial R_\mu$ the biquaternion gradient, acting on the left, and $D_F$ the chirality-odd mass, which couples the two parts of the field through the conjugation. Self-adjointness for the Hermitian form is not automatic, $(i\tilde\nabla)^{\dagger}=i\tilde\nabla^{\natural}$, and the candidate fails it: the coefficient $ie_t$ is anti-Hermitian, as the criterion requires, and the three $i\vec e_k$ are Hermitian, so the operator is self-adjoint in the time direction and skew in the spatial ones. It is self-adjoint for the indefinite Dirac form instead. The algebra acts through its real form $\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}}$, by the left action $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\mapsto a\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}$; the conjugate of a field is reached by the conjugation, and the conjugation is the only route to it. A candidate triple therefore exists: $(\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}},L^2(\mathbb{B}),\mathcal{D})$, or its almost-commutative generalisation $C^\infty(M)\otimes\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}}$. Three of the axioms fail: the boundedness of the commutators with the non-central elements under the left action, the first-order condition wherever one of the two multipliers is not fixed by the conjugation, and the oddness of the operator under a grading of its own carrier. Self-adjointness fails as well, and it fails for a reason of the same kind: the gradient's coefficients are the four units, and the units are elements of the algebra.

  2. The framework's real-form involution $\flat$ is an antilinear involution, and this is the article's sharpest algebraic point. The spectral triple's real structure $J$ is an antilinear operator whose sign $J^2=\pm1$, together with the two signs of its commutation with $D$ and with the chirality, gives the triple's KO-dimension. The framework's real-form involution is $\flat=-{}^{*}$, and it was verified explicitly that $$ \flat^2 = +1 $$ on the algebra. The Euclidean frame's candidate charge conjugation is the left action $C:\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\mapsto-\vec e_z\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}$; it is skew for the Hermitian form, squares to $-1$, and commutes with the chirality, so the associated antilinear operator $J_C=CK$ has $J_C^2=-1$ and $J_C\xi=-\xi J_C$. That pair is the quaternionic class of Connes' table, $n\equiv2$ with $(\epsilon,\epsilon'')=(-1,-1)$, and it is the class the Standard-Model finite geometry uses at $n\equiv6$; four-dimensional Euclidean geometry is the real class $n\equiv4$ with $(\epsilon,\epsilon',\epsilon'')=(+1,+1,+1)$. So $\flat$ supplies the sign of the square that four-dimensional geometry has, and what it cannot supply is the quaternionic sign the Standard-Model finite geometry needs: the minus is carried by an antilinear operator of square $-1$, which the algebra carries as the composition of $C$ with the conjugation, and a central phase cannot supply it, because $(\lambda\flat)^2=|\lambda|^2\flat^2=+1$. Two further signs are worth separating from this one: $\flat$ composed with the chirality gives neither $+\xi$ nor $-\xi$, so the sign $\epsilon''$ is not defined for it at all, while $C$ gives $\epsilon''=-1$ outright. Stating this precisely is the article's main contribution, and it is a genuine, checkable statement about the algebra rather than a re-description.

  3. The spectral dimension is four, and the framework's metric-as-output is the spectral triple's distance formula read backwards. The triple's dimension is read from the eigenvalue growth of $D$: if the eigenvalues grow like $n^{1/d}$, the triple has dimension $d$. For the biquaternion Dirac operator on four-dimensional spacetime — that is, for the almost-commutative triple, the finite factor alone having spectral dimension zero — the eigenvalues grow like $n^{1/4}$ and the spectral dimension is four. The same Seeley–DeWitt data that control the heat-kernel coefficients of The Functional Determinant in Biquaternionic Form and The Trace Anomaly in Biquaternionic Form control this growth: the exponent $d$ of the small-$r'_t$ expansion is the spectral dimension, and the coefficient that expansion marks as divergent is $a_{d/2}$, which at $d=4$ is $a_2$. The framework's claim that the Minkowski signature is an output of the level-1 form read on the material sector is the same shape of statement as the spectral triple's claim that the distance is an output of $D$: in both cases the geometric data is extracted from the algebraic one.

  4. The framework is not a non-commutative geometry of the Standard-Model kind. Connes' and Chamseddine's spectral action program builds the Standard Model from an almost-commutative triple $\mathcal{A}=C^\infty(M)\otimes\mathcal{A}_F$ with a finite-dimensional factor $\mathcal{A}_F$, and it requires specific axioms — the first-order condition, orientability, Poincaré duality, and the KO-dimension — to select the finite geometry. The biquaternion algebra is a full complex matrix algebra, and its finite factor would have to be examined against those axioms; the boundedness and first-order conditions are examined in the body, and orientability, Poincaré duality, and the reproduction of the Standard Model's particle content are not. The Standard-Model spectral triple is therefore a neighbouring construction and not a result of this series. What this article establishes is the candidate, the real-structure obstacle, the dimension, and the three axiom failures; the model-building is elsewhere.

  5. The article stands apart from the rest of the subcategory, and is last for that reason. Everything before it has been quantum field theory: states, operator algebras, path integrals, finite-temperature and non-equilibrium machinery, and the two structural quantum effects. The spectral triple is a construction in non-commutative geometry, and its appearance here is as the algebraic interpretation of the framework's objects. It is included because the biquaternion algebra's non-commutativity and its action on itself make the construction natural, and it is placed last because it uses the objects the other articles establish without being used by them.

The sections that follow treat, in order: the definition and the axioms; the biquaternion candidate and its elementary properties; the real structure and the KO signs; the spectral dimension and the heat kernel; the distance formula and the metric as output; inner fluctuations and the spectral action; the almost-commutative triple and the finite geometry; the ledger of what is established and what is interpretation; and a closing section, The Legacy Pauli Gamma Formulation, where the two-sided frame is written out as gamma matrices, followed by a summary and a table of notation.

Spectral Triples and Their Axioms

The definition and the axioms are stated as the standard literature gives them.

The definition. A spectral triple $(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{H},D)$ consists of a unital $*$-algebra $\mathcal{A}$ of bounded operators on a separable Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$, together with a self-adjoint operator $D$ on $\mathcal{H}$ such that $(1+D^2)^{-1/2}$ is compact and $[D,a]$ is bounded for every $a\in\mathcal{A}$. The last two conditions are the analytic content: the compactness of the resolvent is what makes the eigenvalue spectrum discrete and gives the triple a dimension, and the boundedness of the commutators is what makes $D$ a first-order operator.

The axioms. The axioms of a non-commutative spin geometry are, in outline: dimension, the eigenvalue growth of $D$ fixing an integer $d$; regularity, that the algebra and its commutators with $D$ lie in the smooth domain of the derivation $[D,\cdot]$; finiteness, that the smooth domain of the algebra is a finitely generated projective module; reality, the existence of an antilinear $J$ with $$ J^2=\epsilon I, \qquad JD=\epsilon'DJ, \qquad J\xi=\epsilon''\xi J, \qquad \epsilon,\epsilon',\epsilon''\in\{\pm1\}, $$ whose signs give the KO-dimension of the triple, with $\xi$ the chirality operator in even dimensions; first order, $[[D,a],Jb^*J^{-1}]=0$; orientability, the existence of a Hochschild cycle representing the volume form; and Poincaré duality, the non-degeneracy of the intersection form on the $K$-theory. The list is standard (Connes 1995, 1996; Connes and Marcolli 2008) and is cited rather than re-derived. The sign convention is the one in which a closed spin manifold of dimension $m$ has KO-dimension $m\bmod8$, so four-dimensional Euclidean geometry has $(\epsilon,\epsilon',\epsilon'')=(+1,+1,+1)$ and the Standard-Model finite geometry, at $n=6$, has $(-1,+1,-1)$. The corpus's Spectral Triples and Noncommutative Geometry prints rows 4 and 6 of its table the other way round; the difference is reported in the section on the real structure, and the arithmetic below follows the convention just stated.

Which axioms are cheap and which are not. For a commutative algebra of functions on a spin manifold all of them hold, and that is the theorem that the construction generalises the manifold. For a finite-dimensional non-commutative algebra some of them are algebraic and checkable directly — reality, first order, orientability — while dimension, finiteness and Poincaré duality require structure that a bare finite algebra does not supply. Boundedness is the exception in the other direction: it looks analytic and harmless, and it is the condition the biquaternion algebra fails, for a reason that is algebraic — the coefficients of its gradient are elements of the algebra. The biquaternion algebra is finite-dimensional, and this division is what the next section reports.

The Biquaternion Candidate

The candidate is assembled from objects the rest of the series already uses, and the assembly is checked before any axiom is asserted.

The three actions and what decides the grading. An element $a$ acts on a field in three ways: on the left, $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\mapsto a\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}$; on the right, $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\mapsto\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}} b$; and on both sides at once, $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\mapsto a\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}} b$. The left and right actions commute, $(a\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}})b=a(\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}} b)$. Below, $a\cdot$ denotes the left action and $\cdot b$ the right action, and $K$ denotes the coefficient conjugation. The sixteen maps $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\mapsto e_\mu\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}} e_\nu$, one for each pair of units, are the elementary two-sided actions, and every product action this article writes is one of them or a sum.

The grading then decides what a product can be. Because $\xi$ is a right action, every left action is even under it, and a two-sided action is even or odd according to its right factor alone: $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\mapsto a\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}} b$ is odd exactly when $b$ anticommutes with $\vec e_z$, that is, exactly when $b$ lies in the span of $\vec e_x$ and $\vec e_y$. In words, $\cdot e_t$ and $\cdot\vec e_z$ keep each part of a field and $\cdot\vec e_x$, $\cdot\vec e_y$ exchange the two parts. The framework's mass is odd as well, and it is not a two-sided action: it is the conjugation composed with the mass scale, and its oddness is the conjugation's own, $K\xi=-\xi K$. That is the structural reason the mass sits apart from the gradient, and it is checked below on explicit elements.

The algebra and the field. The algebra is $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$ as a complex algebra, and its real form is $\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}}$. The field is an element of the algebra, $$ \tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}=\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}+\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}, $$ the two terms being its two chirality parts: $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}$ is the part of value $-1$ under the chirality and $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}$ the part of value $+1$, in the letters of the Dirac literature. The subscript is the whole of the definition, and the letters are not names of two carriers. The algebra acts on the left, $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\mapsto a\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}$, and the left action keeps the letter of the part it acts on; the conjugation exchanges the two letters.

The Hilbert space. The inner product is the Hermitian one, $$ \langle\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}},\tilde{Ξ}\rangle_* =\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}^*\tilde{Ξ}\bigr), $$ which is positive definite, $$ \langle\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}},\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\rangle_*=\sum_{\mu}\lvert\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},\mu}\rvert^2>0 \quad(\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\neq0). $$ It is the Hermitian form of Norm and Pairing Conventions, not the plain bilinear form: the plain form is indefinite, and it cannot be the Hilbert-space inner product. The completion of $\mathbb{B}$ in this form is the candidate $\mathcal{H}$, four-complex-dimensional in the finite case.

The operator. The Dirac operator is $$ \mathcal{D}=i\tilde\nabla+D_F, \qquad \tilde\nabla=\sum_\mu e_\mu\,\frac{\partial}{\partial R_\mu}, $$ with $D_F$ the mass of the next paragraph. The gradient is an element with derivative coefficients, and $\tilde\nabla\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}$ means its left action on $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}$; no other action is meant anywhere below. With respect to the Hermitian form the gradient's adjoint reverses its units, $$ \tilde\nabla^{\dagger}=-\tilde\nabla^{\natural}, \qquad (i\tilde\nabla)^{\dagger}=i\tilde\nabla^{\natural}, $$ so $i\tilde\nabla$ is not self-adjoint for that form. The criterion is fixed by the derivative: since $(\partial/\partial R_\mu)^{\dagger}=-\partial/\partial R_\mu$, an operator $\sum_\mu A_\mu\partial/\partial R_\mu$ with constant coefficients is self-adjoint exactly when every $A_\mu$ is anti-Hermitian. Here $ie_t$ is anti-Hermitian but the three $i\vec e_k$ are Hermitian, so the operator is self-adjoint on the time direction and skew on the three spatial ones, and the defect is the Minkowski signature itself. The Euclidean frame of The Legacy Pauli Gamma Formulation is the opposite case, all four of its actions being Hermitian; there the coefficients $-iE_\mu$ are anti-Hermitian and $-i\sum_\mu E_\mu\partial/\partial R_\mu$ is self-adjoint for the same form, with square $-\Box$, since $\Box=\tilde\nabla\tilde\nabla^{\natural}=\sum_\mu\partial^2/\partial R_\mu^2$ and the frame's four actions square to $+e_t$. That is Connes' normalisation, and it is the frame in which the signs are all of one kind. The framework's operator is self-adjoint for the Dirac (Krein) form, and whether the triple can use a positive-definite form and keep a self-adjoint $D$ is part of the open problem, not a consequence of the algebra.

The Dirac equation in two forms. The framework writes the Dirac equation in two forms, and the displays should be kept separate because they answer different questions. The defining form is the chiral pair, $$ \tilde\nabla\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}=m\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}, \qquad \tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}=m\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}, $$ with the gradient acting on the right-chirality part and its conjugate on the left-chirality part, and the mass coupling the two. The pair is one operator, the pair operator of Operator and Calculus Conventions, $$ \mathbb{D}(\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L},\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R})=\bigl(\tilde\nabla\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R},\ \tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}\bigr), \qquad \mathbb{D}^2=\Box e_t, $$ and the massive equation is $(\mathbb{D}-m)\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}=0$, whose two components are the pair. The two-sided form, from the two-sided operator theory of The Feynman Propagator in Biquaternionic Form, is $$ \mathcal{N}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}=\Bigl(-\tilde\nabla^{\natural}-mi\,\overline{\,\cdot\,}\,\vec e_z\Bigr)\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}=0, $$ with the mass acting on the right through conjugation. The three displays describe one physics: the first is the chirality form, the second the pair operator written once, and the third the two-sided form. The spectral triple's candidate operator is $\mathcal{D}=i\tilde\nabla+D_F$: the gradient in the same chirality basis, with the chirality-odd mass added.

The chirality. The chirality is the right action by the element $-i\vec e_z$, $$ \xi:\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\longmapsto-i\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\vec e_z=\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\,(-i\vec e_z), $$ the second form holding because $i$ is central. It is self-adjoint for the Hermitian form, its square is $e_t$, and its spectrum is $+1,+1,-1,-1$: two values, which are the two letters. Its central form is the sign of the double, $$ \chi=i\tilde{Ζ}_{\mathrm{ad}}, \qquad \chi^2=e_t, $$ and the two forms are one operator read in two ways — the right action on the algebra, and the sign of the complexification — not two gradings. There is no second grading in the algebra: a relabelling of two copies is not a chirality, and the only operator here that separates the field into two parts and squares to $e_t$ is $\xi$ itself.

What $\xi$ does not grade. The gradient is even under $\xi$ and the mass is odd, so $\mathcal{D}=i\tilde\nabla+D_F$ is neither, and the reason is algebraic rather than a choice of signs: $\xi$ is a right action and the gradient a left action, so they commute whatever the coefficients. The operator that differentiates therefore cannot see the grading, and within $\mathcal{D}$ only the mass sees it. This is why a graded triple needs $\Gamma=G_M\otimes\xi$: the oddness of $D$ is carried by the manifold chirality $G_M$ and not by $\mathcal{D}$ alone. The finite triple $(\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}},D_F)$ is graded and odd; it is the whole operator that has no grading. This is the same collapse seen from the other side: the framework has one carrier where the construction has two factors.

The mass. The mass couples the two letters, and the framework writes it through the conjugation: $$ D_F:\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\longmapsto m\,\overline{\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}}, $$ equivalently $\xi D_F:\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\mapsto-mi\,\overline{\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}}\,\vec e_z$, the chirality being a fixed right action. This is the framework's own mass, the one the two-sided Dirac equation carries. It is antilinear, it is not $\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}}$-linear for $m\neq0$, and its square is the scalar $$ D_F^2=\lvert m\rvert^2e_t, \qquad \xi D_F+D_F\xi=0 . $$ Because it is antilinear it has no eigenvalues, and the spectrum statement of a finite Dirac operator has to be replaced by the square: the mass scale is $\lvert m\rvert$, and both parts of the field carry it. What the mass does to the Hermitian norm is fixed in the same way, $$ \bigl\langle D_F\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}},D_F\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\bigr\rangle_*=\lvert m\rvert^2\bigl\langle\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}},\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\bigr\rangle_* . $$

The commutator with the algebra. For $a\in\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}}$ the mass term of the commutator is explicit and needs no array: $$ [D_F,a\cdot]\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}} =\bigl(m\bar a-am\bigr)\bar{\tilde{Ξ}}_{\mathrm{sp}}, $$ where $\bar a$ is the coefficient conjugate. The asymmetry between $a$ and $\bar a$ is the whole content: the left action carries $a$ and the mass carries the conjugate, and it is because the two differ that the mass fails to commute with the algebra. The commutator vanishes exactly when $m\bar a=am$, that is, for $m\neq0$, exactly when $\bar a=a$. The elements fixed by the coefficient conjugation are those of the real sector $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$, the elements whose four coefficients are real, and that set is not commutative: what the mass tolerates here is the real quaternion subalgebra and not an abelian one. The count differs from the one an array-based reading gives, where a dressed conjugation fixes a smaller set, and the difference is a property of the conjugation and not of the mass; the statement above is computed with the coefficient conjugation, which is the one the element formulation carries.

The order of the gradient commutator. The gradient term is not zeroth order, and this is the first place where the algebra meets its own coefficients. Because $a$ is a constant of the finite algebra, $\partial/\partial R_\mu(a\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}})=a\,\partial \tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}/\partial R_\mu$, so $$ [i\tilde\nabla,a\cdot]\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}=i\sum_\mu[e_\mu,a]\,\frac{\partial\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}}{\partial R_\mu}, $$ which carries a derivative whenever $a$ fails to commute with the units. The principal symbol states the criterion exactly: the symbol of $\tilde\nabla$ is multiplication by the element $\sum_\mu\zeta_\mu e_\mu$, so the commutator is bounded precisely when $$ \Bigl[\sum_\mu\zeta_\mu e_\mu,\,a\Bigr]=0\quad\text{for every }\zeta, $$ that is, precisely when $a$ commutes with all four units. The centralizer of the units in $\mathbb{B}$ is the complex line $\mathbb{C}e_t$, and the three cases follow.

  • A scalar multiplier $f$ commutes with the units, and the commutator is the zeroth-order $[i\tilde\nabla,f\cdot]=i\,(\tilde\nabla f)\cdot$, hence bounded. This is the classical statement for functions, and it is the reason the naive formula $[i\tilde\nabla,a\cdot]=i\,(\tilde\nabla a)\cdot$ holds for them and fails for the rest of the algebra.
  • A non-central element $a\in\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}}$ gives a first-order commutator, hence an unbounded one on the field-theoretic Hilbert space: the bounded-commutator condition fails for the left action of the non-central elements. On the finite carrier the condition is vacuous — every operator on a finite-dimensional space is bounded — so the failure is a statement about the square-integrable fields over spacetime, and not about the finite algebra. Note what makes it fail: in Connes' almost-commutative triple the continuum operator is $D_M\otimes I_F$ and carries no element of the finite algebra, so its commutators with the finite factor vanish. Here the coefficients of the gradient are themselves elements of the algebra and are four of them, and that is what turns the commutator first order.
  • The right action passes: for a constant factor $[\tilde\nabla,\cdot a]=0$ identically, and for a function the commutator is the bounded two-sided action $[\tilde\nabla,\cdot f]\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}=\sum_\mu e_\mu\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\,\partial f/\partial R_\mu$.

The asymmetry is the structural point. The four units are the coefficients of the gradient and elements of the algebra at once, so an action on the same side as the units is seen by them; the side they do not see is the right one. The symbol check is exact and is read on the elements themselves: at $\zeta=(0.3,-0.7,1.1,0.4)$ the quaternionic norm of $[\sum_\mu\zeta_\mu e_\mu,a]$ is $2.34$ for $a=\vec e_x$, $1.61$ for $a=\vec e_y$, and $2.61$ for $a=\vec e_z$, and exactly $0$ for $a=e_t$ and for the central scalars such as $ie_t$. The commutator is bilinear, so a general $a$ scales these numbers by its own coefficients; the units are the meaningful comparison, and none of the three non-central units gives zero.

The first-order condition. With the candidate real structure read from $\flat$, written $J_{\flat}$ on the field, the condition is $$ [[\mathcal{D},a\cdot],J_{\flat}b^{*}J_{\flat}^{-1}]=0. $$ The two minus signs from $\flat=-{}^{*}$ cancel in the combination, so $J_{\flat}b^{*}J_{\flat}^{-1}$ is the right action $\cdot b$. The gradient then contributes nothing at all, because a left action commutes with a right one and the derivative commutes with both: $$ \bigl[[i\tilde\nabla,a\cdot],\cdot b\bigr]=0 $$ identically. The condition is decided by the mass, and the mass gives one line, $$ \bigl[[D_F,a\cdot],\cdot b\bigr]\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}} =\bigl(m\bar a-am\bigr)\bar{\tilde{Ξ}}_{\mathrm{sp}}\,\bigl(\bar b-b\bigr), $$ so it holds exactly when one of the two multipliers is fixed by the conjugation — when $a$ or $b$ lies in the real sector $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$. This is the element-level form of the axiom, and it differs from the array-based count in the size of the set and not in the shape of the failure: the condition isolates a proper subalgebra, and the subalgebra is the one the conjugation fixes.

The two candidates separate here, and they separate at different places.

  • The vector-like candidate takes the mass to be $m$ times the identity action, with $m$ central and real, which is self-adjoint for the Hermitian form. Then $[D_F,a\cdot]=0$ and $$ \boxed{\;[[\mathcal{D},a\cdot],\cdot b]=0\quad\text{for all }a,b\in\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}}\;} $$ Both terms vanish exactly rather than approximately, so the condition holds for this candidate. The bounded-commutator condition of the previous paragraph still fails for its left action. Nor is the success free of charge: the mass is a multiple of the identity and commutes with the chirality, as does the gradient, so the whole operator is even, and this candidate has no odd part at all. The two candidates therefore fail different axioms: the vector-like one keeps the first-order condition and loses every grading, and the chiral one keeps the odd mass and loses the first-order condition outside the fixed subalgebra.
  • The chiral candidate is the one above, with the mass through the conjugation. It keeps the odd finite part, $\{\xi,D_F\}=0$ exactly, and it satisfies the first-order condition whenever a multiplier is fixed by the conjugation. Its failure set is the complement of that subalgebra, and the failure is not a numerical accident: the mass is antilinear, so it can never commute with a left action that carries a complex scalar.

What the candidate does not supply. Five things are worth separating from what it does supply.

  • The side on which the non-central elements can act is constrained. Bounded commutators hold for the right action and fail for the left, while the left action is the one a representation of the algebra uses. No single side carries both roles, and the obstruction in both cases is the same: the coefficients of the gradient are elements of the algebra.
  • The operator has no grading on its own carrier. $\xi$ commutes with the gradient and anticommutes with the mass, so $\mathcal{D}$ is neither even nor odd under $\xi$; the grading of a full triple is $G_M\otimes\xi$, which grades the product of the manifold factor with the finite one, and not the finite factor alone.
  • The finite operator is antilinear. A spectral triple asks for a self-adjoint linear $D$. The algebra does carry linear odd actions, the two-sided ones whose right factor is $\vec e_x$ or $\vec e_y$, but their squares are negative multiples of the identity, since $\vec e_x^2=\vec e_y^2=-e_t$, so they cannot stand where a mass with a positive square stands. The framework's mass, whose square is $+|m|^2e_t$, is antilinear.
  • The Hilbert space and the operator do not decide the finite geometry. In Connes' construction the finite algebra and the finite Dirac operator are constrained by the axioms and by the requirement that the spectral action reproduce the Standard Model; here the finite operator is the framework's mass, and the finite algebra is $\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}}$, but the constraints are not imposed and the particle content is not derived.
  • The real structure is the algebra's involution rather than the carrier's quaternionic structure. The involution is what the framework's $\flat$ gives, and the antilinear operator of square $-1$ is an additional datum; the next section states the difference and its consequence for the signs.

The Real Structure and the KO-Dimension

The real structure is where the framework's algebra and the spectral triple's axioms meet, and the meeting is a sign.

The involution. The framework's real-form involution is $$ \flat=-{}^{*}, \qquad \flat(\tilde Q)=-\tilde Q^{*}, $$ and its fixed space is the material sector $\mathbb{M}_-$. It is conjugate-linear, because ${}^{*}$ is, and therefore an antilinear map on the algebra. Its square is computed directly: $$ \flat(\flat(\tilde Q)) =\flat\bigl(-\tilde Q^{*}\bigr) =-\bigl(-\tilde Q^{*}\bigr)^{*} =\tilde Q, $$ so $$ \boxed{\;\flat^2=+1\;}. $$ The framework's involution is therefore an antilinear involution, so the candidate real structure has $\epsilon=+1$. This was verified on explicit elements and is quoted in the established list below.

The global minus and the order-one condition. The map $\flat$ is $-1$ times the star anti-automorphism, and the product rule is $$ (\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2)^\flat =-\tilde Q_2^\flat\tilde Q_1^\flat. $$ On the field that involution is the antilinear operator $J_{\flat}(\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}})=-\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}^*$, of square $+1$, and the two minus signs cancel in the order-one combination: $$ J_{\flat}b^{*}J_{\flat}^{-1}=\cdot b, $$ the right action. The global minus is therefore immaterial for the first-order condition, but it must be kept in the signs of the algebra and in the product rule above.

Two signs, and the one $\flat$ does not reach. The sign of the square is $+1$. The sign of the commutation with the chirality is not available for $J_{\flat}$ at all, and the reason is visible in one line: $\xi$ is a right action and the conjugation is taken coefficient by coefficient, so $J_{\flat}\xi$ is neither $+\xi J_{\flat}$ nor $-\xi J_{\flat}$ — the two compositions multiply the field on different sides. A real structure must give a definite sign; $J_{\flat}$ gives none. The chirality-odd real structure therefore has to be built from an operator of the algebra that acts on the other side, and the Euclidean frame supplies one.

The Euclidean candidate. The Euclidean frame's four actions are $$ E_4:\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\longmapsto i\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\vec e_x, \qquad E_k:\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\longmapsto-\vec e_k\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\vec e_y \quad(k=1,2,3), $$ and the four are not of one kind: $E_4$ is a right action, while the three $E_k$ act on both sides. All four are Hermitian, square to $+e_t$, and pairwise anticommute. Their product is the chirality, $$ E_1E_2E_3E_4=\xi, \qquad \chi^2=e_t, $$ a right action; the same four actions taken in the cyclic order $E_4E_1E_2E_3$ give $-\xi$, because moving $E_4$ past three anticommuting actions costs three signs. The sign of the product is the order and nothing else. The candidate for the charge conjugation is the left action $$ C:\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\longmapsto-\vec e_z\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}=E_1E_2, \qquad C^2=-e_t, \qquad [C,\xi]=0, $$ which is skew for the Hermitian form, $C^{*}=-C$. The antilinear operator built from it is $J_C=CK$, with $K$ the coefficient conjugation, and because $C$ is coefficient-real, $$ J_C^2=C\bar C=C^2=-1. $$ The operator $C$ conjugates the four frame actions to themselves with a sign, and the signs are not uniform in this frame: $$ CE_1C^{-1}=-E_1, \quad CE_2C^{-1}=-E_2, \quad CE_3C^{-1}=+E_3, \quad CE_4C^{-1}=+E_4. $$ Two minus signs and two plus. Uniformity of those signs, not the sign of the square, is where this operator fails the definition of a charge conjugation.

Two of the three signs are reached here, and they come out as a pair. The chirality is a right multiplier by $-i\vec e_z$, so the coefficient conjugation sends it to its negative, $K\xi K^{-1}=-\xi$; and $C$ is coefficient-real, so $C\xi C^{-1}=\xi$ and $$ J_C\xi J_C^{-1}=C\bar\xi C^{-1}=-\xi, \qquad \epsilon''=-1 . $$ The sign is not an accident of the frame: an antilinear map changes frame by conjugating its defining action, $J\mapsto U^{-1}J\bar U$, and not by transforming its two factors separately, and with that rule $J_C^2$ and $J_C\xi J_C^{-1}$ are carried to themselves. Reading the sign off one factor alone, as though $J_C$ were a product of two linear maps, gives a different antilinear map and in general a different sign; that is why the sign is read from the operator's action and not from its factors.

Two properties of $J_C$ are then immediate. The operator $C$ preserves the Hermitian norm, so $J_C$ is an isometry; and $J_C^2=C\bar C=C^2=-1$ because $C$ is coefficient-real. A rescaling $J_C\mapsto\lambda J_C$ with $\lvert\lambda\rvert=1$ changes neither $J_C^2$, where the factor is $\lvert\lambda\rvert^2=1$, nor $J_C\xi J_C^{-1}$, where the phase cancels. Both signs are therefore properties of the pair $(C,\xi)$, and the pair $(\epsilon,\epsilon'')=(-1,-1)$ is the $n\equiv2$ line of Connes' table, not the $n\equiv4$ one.

What is compared with the framework's $\flat$ is consequently $\epsilon$ alone, the sign of $J_C^2=C\bar C$, which for coefficient-real $C$ is the sign of $C^2$; the sign $\epsilon'$ of $J_CD=\epsilon'DJ_C$ involves $D$ itself and is not computed.

Which table. The two dictionaries that matter here disagree, and the disagreement is not about this frame. Connes' table gives $(+1,+1,+1)$ for $n\equiv4$ and $(-1,+1,-1)$ for $n\equiv6$, so four-dimensional Euclidean spin geometry is of the real class and the Standard-Model finite geometry, which sits at $n=6$, is of the quaternionic one. The table of Spectral Triples and Noncommutative Geometry prints $(-1,+1,+1)$ at $n=4$ and $(+1,+1,-1)$ at $n=6$, that is the two rows exchanged: its $\epsilon$ at $n=4$ is minus where Connes' is plus, and its $\epsilon$ at $n=6$ is plus where Connes' is minus. Under Connes' table the four-dimensional sign is $+1$, the same as $\flat^2$, and this is what the geometry says: a four-dimensional Euclidean triple has $J^2=+1$.

What $C$ shows. The pattern settles the question of what it is. $C$ has $C^2=-1$ and commutes with $\xi$, and its conjugation signs are not uniform; a charge conjugation proper needs a single sign for all four actions and, in four dimensions, $J^2=+1$. This operator is therefore not the dimension-four charge conjugation, and the frame's $-1$ is not a normalisation slip to be corrected: it is the quaternionic sign, and it is the sign the Standard-Model finite geometry needs. So the comparison resolves, and it resolves against the corpus's row rather than against the frame.

The reconciliation. The framework's $\flat^2=+1$ is the real sign, and the sign it does not give is the quaternionic one, $\epsilon=-1$. The difference is a fact about which object carries the conjugation rather than a relabelling. The algebra carries an antilinear operator of square $-1$ — the composition of $C$ with the coefficient conjugation — and it is this object, not the central circle, that supplies the missing minus. A central phase $\lambda$ cannot: composing $\flat$ with it gives $$ (\lambda\flat)^2=\lvert\lambda\rvert^2\flat^2=+1. $$ The exact relation between $\flat$ and the algebra's own antilinear operator of square $-1$ is not constructed here, and the article marks that as open; what is established is that the algebra's involution has $+1$, that the antilinear operator of square $-1$ exists and is an element action composed with the conjugation, and that the central phase is not a third candidate.

The consequence. The biquaternion algebra's natural real-form involution has the $+$ sign, and the quaternionic sign has to be carried by a second object, the left action $C$ composed with the conjugation. This is a precise statement about where the framework's data lives, and it is the kind of distinction the conventions article's separation of the levels is designed to keep straight: the algebra's involution, the antilinear operator of square $-1$, and the central phase are three different objects, and only the second supplies the quaternionic sign.

The Spectral Dimension and the Heat Kernel

The triple's dimension is read from the operator's spectrum, and the framework's heat-kernel data are the same data.

Dimension from the spectrum. If the eigenvalues $\lambda_n$ of $D$ are ordered by magnitude, the spectral dimension is the $d$ for which the counting function grows as $N(\Lambda)\sim\Lambda^d$. The growth is a statement about the principal symbol, and the biquaternion gradient's is exact. The symbol of $\tilde\nabla$ at $\zeta$ is multiplication by the element $\sum_\mu\zeta_\mu e_\mu$, whose quaternionic norm is $\zeta_t^2+\zeta_x^2+\zeta_y^2+\zeta_z^2$: the four generators contribute $+\zeta_\mu^2$ each, because $e_t^2=+e_t$ and $\vec e_k^2=-e_t$ while $\vec e_k^{\natural}=-\vec e_k$. Hence $$ \sigma\sigma^{\natural}=|\zeta|^2e_t, $$ nonzero off $\zeta=0$, and the symbol is invertible there. Note that $\sigma^2$ is not a multiple of $e_t$ — its scalar part is $\zeta_t^2-|\vec\zeta|^2$ — so the ellipticity is read from the quaternionic norm, not from the square. It is the square that is scalar in the Euclidean frame, where the four actions anticommute: there $D=-i\sum_\mu E_\mu\partial/\partial R_\mu$ gives $D^2=-\sum_\mu\partial^2/\partial R_\mu^2$, of Laplace type, and the $r'_t^{-2}$ growth that gives $d=4$ is the symbol's and not an assumption. Equivalently the trace of the heat kernel grows as $$ \boxed{\;\mathrm{Tr}\,\exp(-r'_t D^2)\;\sim\;{r'_{t}}^{-d/2}\quad(r'_t\to0^+)\;}, $$ and the coefficients of the small-$r'_t$ expansion are the Seeley–DeWitt coefficients $a_n$ of the determinant article, in its normalisation $$ K(r'_t)\sim(4\pi r'_t)^{-d/2}\sum_{n\ge0}a_n\,{r'_{t}}^{n}. $$ The dimension is the exponent of the prefactor $(4\pi r'_t)^{-d/2}$, of which $a_0$ is the constant part; the coefficient of the logarithmic divergence is $a_{d/2}$, which in four dimensions is $a_2$, the quantity the determinant and anomaly articles evaluate. So the two statements are one: the growth fixes $d=4$, and $d=4$ names which of their coefficients is the divergent one. The framework adds its multiplicity: the trace runs over the field, whose four complex dimensions multiply the density of eigenvalues, and the anisotropy of the level-2 metric does not enter because the heat-kernel statement is the Euclidean one, the Euclidean rotation being the framework's sector identification of the functional-integral article.

What the framework contributes. The dimension four is not derived from the algebra; it is the dimension of the manifold the operator differentiates. The framework's contribution is the same as in the determinant and anomaly articles: it names the space on which the trace is taken and counts its dimensions. It is worth saying plainly, because the spectral triple's literature sometimes reads a dimension out of a finite geometry, that here the finite carrier is four-complex-dimensional and the four-dimensionality comes from the differential operator.

The finite part. A finite spectral triple — one with finite-dimensional $\mathcal{H}$ and bounded $D$ — has a compact resolvent trivially and a spectral dimension zero, since its spectrum is finite. The biquaternion algebra's finite triple therefore has spectral dimension zero; a dimensionful triple requires the algebra $C^\infty(M)\otimes\mathbb{B}$ and a first-order elliptic operator. This is an important qualification: the framework's algebra alone does not give a four-dimensional spectral triple, and only the almost-commutative version does.

The Distance Formula and the Metric as Output

The construction's most distinctive feature is that the metric is recovered from the operator, and the framework has its own version of the same move.

The distance formula. For a spectral triple with a commutative algebra the distance between two states is $$ d(\xi_{\mathrm{alt}},\omega) =\sup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\bigl\{\lvert\xi_{\mathrm{alt}}(a)-\omega(a)\rvert:\lVert[D,a]\rVert\le1\bigr\}, $$ and for $\mathcal{A}=C^\infty(M)$ with $D$ the Dirac operator this reproduces the geodesic distance. The metric is therefore an output of the operator and the algebra, not an input. This is standard (Connes 1995; Connes and Marcolli 2008).

The framework's analogous move. The framework's metric is likewise an output: the Lorentzian signature is not postulated but is the level-1 biquaternion norm read on the material sector with the time coefficient written $i c r'_t$, and the minus sign comes from $i^2=-1$ alone. The two constructions are different — one recovers a distance from a non-commutative algebra, the other recovers a signature from a biquaternion norm — but they have the same shape, and it is worth stating the similarity and its limit. The limit is that the framework's derivation is algebraic and finite-dimensional (the levels of the metric are the four complex coefficients of the biquaternion norm), whereas the spectral triple's is analytic and requires the operator's commutator norms. A reader should not conflate the two: the framework derives a signature, not a distance, and the spectral triple derives a distance, not a signature.

The almost-commutative case. If one takes $\mathcal{A}=C^\infty(M)\otimes\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}}$, then the triple is almost commutative, and the distance formula recovers the product geometry $M\times F$ for a finite geometry $F$. Whether the biquaternion finite factor satisfies the axioms that select a physically interesting $F$ is the question of the next section, and this article does not answer it.

Inner Fluctuations, Gauge Fields, and the Spectral Action

The model-building content of the construction is stated, and its status in this series is fixed.

Inner fluctuations. A spectral triple admits a modification of the operator by an inner fluctuation, $$ D\;\longrightarrow\;D_A=D+\sum_i a_i\,[D,b_i], $$ with $a_i,b_i\in\mathcal{A}$ and the sum self-adjoint, and the fluctuation is what plays the role of a gauge field. For $\mathcal{A}=C^\infty(M)$ it is a one-form and produces the Maxwell field; for an almost-commutative triple it produces the Yang–Mills fields and the Higgs field. The gauge group is read off from the unitaries of the algebra modulo the central phase, which acts as a multiple of the identity and is not seen by the states. This is standard (Connes 1996; Chamseddine and Connes 1997) and is cited.

The spectral action. The bosonic action is the spectral action $$ S_{\mathrm{spec}}=\mathrm{Tr}\,f\!\left(\frac{D_A}{\Lambda}\right), $$ which expands in the heat-kernel coefficients as the sum of the volume term, the Einstein–Hilbert term, the Yang–Mills terms, and the scalar potential, with the coefficients read from the $a_n$ of the previous section; the expansion is the standard result of the Chamseddine–Connes program. The framework's connection to it is exactly the heat-kernel connection: the same coefficients that the functional-determinant and trace-anomaly articles evaluate expand the spectral action. This is a genuine structural link, and it is the reason the spectral triple is included in this subcategory at all.

The framework's status regarding it. The series does not construct the Standard Model from the biquaternion spectral triple, and this article does not either. The gauge-field and particle-physics categories are the appropriate place for the gauge group, the fermion content, and the anomaly-cancellation conditions, and the almost-commutative triple's axioms would have to be checked there. What this article establishes is that the framework's objects can be assembled into a candidate triple, that the algebra's involution has the $+$ sign and needs a second object for the $-$ sign, and that the heat-kernel coefficients the framework already uses are the spectral action's coefficients.

The Almost-Commutative Triple and the Finite Geometry

The physical form of a spectral triple is almost commutative, and the framework's mass term turns out to sit exactly where the finite geometry's operator belongs.

The product geometry. An almost-commutative triple takes the algebra, Hilbert space, and operator as $$ \mathcal{A}=C^\infty(M)\otimes\mathcal{A}_F, \qquad \mathcal{H}=L^2(M)\otimes\mathcal{H}_F, \qquad D=D_M\otimes I_F+G_M\otimes D_F, $$ with $D_M$ the Dirac operator of the manifold, $G_M$ the chirality of its manifold factor, written with a capital to distinguish it from the finite chirality $\xi$, $\mathcal{H}_F=\mathbb{B}$, $D_F$ the finite operator of the previous sections acting on it, and the second term the only coupling to the finite geometry. The two gradings must not be confused: $G_M$ grades the manifold factor, while $\xi$ grades the two parts of the finite field, and it is $G_M$ that makes $D$ odd. The construction's content is in the two fluctuation channels: inner fluctuations of $D_M$ give the gauge fields, and the off-diagonal part of $D_F$ gives the scalar (Higgs) field, while the spectral action of the previous section expands the whole in the heat-kernel coefficients. This is the standard almost-commutative framework (Connes 1996; Chamseddine and Connes 1997).

The finite operator is the framework's mass term. For the biquaternion candidate, $\mathcal{A}_F=\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}}$ and $\mathcal{H}_F=\mathbb{B}$, and the finite operator is the mass of the section The Biquaternion Candidate, $$ D_F:\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\longmapsto m\,\overline{\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}}, \qquad \xi D_F+D_F\xi=0 . $$ The requirement that $D_F$ be odd under the chirality — that it anticommute with $\xi$ — is met, and it is met for the structural reason stated there: the mass acts through the conjugation, which anticommutes with the chirality, $K\xi=-\xi K$, and that is what makes it odd. This was verified explicitly: the anticommutator vanishes identically, and $D_F^2=\lvert m\rvert^2e_t$ gives one mass scale $\lvert m\rvert$, carried by both parts, the expected particle and antiparticle masses. The identification is exact: the series' off-diagonal mass is a finite Dirac operator of an almost-commutative spectral triple, and it is odd under the chirality because it couples the two chirality parts. It does not couple two copies of the algebra: a copy label is not a chirality, and the mass does not see one.

Consequences. Three consequences follow and are worth stating.

  • The framework's mass term is not an added ingredient of its spectral triple; it is the triple's finite part, and the triple's product structure is what relates the mass to the chirality.
  • The gauge fields are inner fluctuations of $D_M$ and the scalar field is the off-diagonal part of $D_F$, so the framework's gauge-field and mass content sit in the two channels the construction provides, without a further postulate.
  • The gauge group would be the unitaries of the algebra modulo the central phase, which acts as a scalar and is invisible on states; with the framework's involution there are no unitaries to take in the first place. Since $\flat=-{}^{*}$, the condition $u^{\flat}u=e_t$ reads $-u^{*}u=e_t$, that is $u^{*}u=-e_t$, which no element satisfies because the Hermitian norm is non-negative. The pair $(\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}},\flat)$ is therefore not a $C^{*}$-algebra, and $\flat$ is an antilinear involution on the algebra rather than a positive one. Under the $C^{*}$ involution ${}^{*}$ the unitaries of $\mathbb{B}$ are $\mathrm{U}(2)$, and the quotient by the central $\mathrm{U}(1)$, a scalar phase that no state sees, is $\mathrm{PU}(2)\cong\mathrm{SO}(3)$, which is not the Standard-Model group. Either way the physical model-building — the fermion content, the anomaly-cancellation conditions, and the group — belongs to the gauge-field category and is not pursued here.

The scale. The finite operator's entries have the dimensions of mass, and the spectral action's expansion builds the scalar potential out of them, so the framework's $m$ is the finite geometry's scale. In the Standard-Model triple this is the mechanism that ties the scalar sector to the finite geometry instead of postulating it, and it produces, at tree level, a relation between the Higgs mass and the gauge couplings at the unification scale. Whether the biquaternion factor produces a comparable relation is not computed here; what is established is only that $m$ is where a finite geometry's scale would sit.

What Is Established and What Is Interpretation

Established (framework and algebra).

  • The biquaternion candidate triple: algebra $\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}}$ (or $C^\infty(M)\otimes\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}}$), Hilbert space the completion of $\mathbb{B}$ in the Hermitian form, operator $\mathcal{D}=i\tilde\nabla+D_F$. The Hilbert space and the operator are the series' own objects, and no new structure is introduced; $\tilde\nabla^{\dagger}=-\tilde\nabla^{\natural}$, so $i\tilde\nabla$ has adjoint $i\tilde\nabla^{\natural}$, and $i\tilde\nabla$ is not self-adjoint for the Hermitian form: the criterion asks for anti-Hermitian coefficients, which $ie_t$ is and the three $i\vec e_k$ are not.
  • The framework's real-form involution satisfies $\flat^2=+1$ exactly: $\flat(\tilde Q)=-\tilde Q^{*}$ gives $\flat(\flat(\tilde Q))=\tilde Q$; verified on explicit elements, so the candidate real structure $J_{\flat}$ has $\epsilon=+1$. The product rule is $(\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2)^\flat=-\tilde Q_2^\flat\tilde Q_1^\flat$, and on the field the two minus signs cancel in $J_{\flat}b^{*}J_{\flat}^{-1}=\cdot b$, the right action. The chirality sign is not defined for $J_{\flat}$: $J_{\flat}\xi$ is neither $+\xi J_{\flat}$ nor $-\xi J_{\flat}$.
  • The Euclidean frame's antisymmetric operator of square $-1$ is $C:\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\mapsto-\vec e_z\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}=E_1E_2$ in the frame $E_4:\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\mapsto i\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\vec e_x$, $E_k:\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\mapsto-\vec e_k\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\vec e_y$. The four frame actions are Hermitian, square to $+e_t$, and pairwise anticommute; their product is the chirality, $E_1E_2E_3E_4=\xi$, while the other cyclic order gives $-\xi$, $E_4E_1E_2E_3=-\xi$; $C$ is skew for the Hermitian form, $C^2=-e_t$, and $[C,\xi]=0$. $C$ conjugates each frame action to itself up to sign, and the signs are not uniform in this frame: $-,-,+,+$. The antilinear operator $J_C=CK$ therefore has $J_C^2=C^2=-1$ and $J_C\xi=-\xi J_C$, the quaternionic pattern $(\epsilon,\epsilon'')=(-1,-1)$. Both signs are fixed by the pair $(C,\xi)$ alone: they are invariant under a rescaling of $J$ and under a change of frame, so neither is a normalisation. $\epsilon'$ is not reached, because it involves $D$. The pattern is Connes' $n\equiv2$ (equivalently $n\equiv6$), while four-dimensional Euclidean geometry is $n\equiv4$ with $(+1,+1,+1)$; the table of Spectral Triples and Noncommutative Geometry has these two rows exchanged, and that disagreement is reported at the end of the section. The framework's own sign is $+1$: it agrees with the four-dimensional Euclidean sign and is not the quaternionic one, and the quaternionic sign can come only from an antilinear operator of square $-1$, which the algebra carries as $C$ composed with the conjugation, not from a central phase, since composition with a central phase leaves $\epsilon=+1$.
  • The order of the gradient commutator is decided by the algebra's own coefficients: $[i\tilde\nabla,a\cdot]=i\sum_\mu[e_\mu,a]\partial/\partial R_\mu$ is zeroth order exactly when $a$ commutes with all four units, so the left action of the non-central elements fails the bounded-commutator condition and only the central scalars and the functions pass it. The right action passes it identically for a constant factor and by a two-sided action for functions. The symbol check is exact: at $\zeta=(0.3,-0.7,1.1,0.4)$ the quaternionic norm of $[\sum_\mu\zeta_\mu e_\mu,a]$ is $2.34$ for $\vec e_x$, $1.61$ for $\vec e_y$, $2.61$ for $\vec e_z$, and exactly $0$ for $e_t$ and $ie_t$. Being bilinear in $a$, the commutator scales with the coefficients of $a$, so only the units give a scale-free comparison.
  • The first-order condition is decided by the mass, and the mass gives one line. The gradient contributes nothing, because its commutator is a left action and a left action commutes with a right one: $[[i\tilde\nabla,a\cdot],\cdot b]=0$ identically. The mass contributes $[[D_F,a\cdot],\cdot b]\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}=(m\bar a-am)\bar{\tilde{Ξ}}_{\mathrm{sp}}(\bar b-b)$, so the condition holds exactly when $a$ or $b$ is fixed by the conjugation, that is, when one of the two lies in the real sector $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$. The set is a subalgebra and it is not commutative; the array-based reading fixes a smaller set, and the difference is the conjugation, not the mass.
  • The mass, being the conjugation composed with the mass scale, is odd under the chirality: $\{\xi,D_F\}=0$ (verified, residual $0$); $\mathcal{D}$ on $\mathbb{B}$ is neither even nor odd under $\xi$, since the gradient is even and the mass odd. The finite triple $(\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}},D_F)$ is graded and odd; the grading of a full triple is $G_M\otimes\xi$ with $G_M$ the manifold chirality. The oddness of a two-sided action is decided by its right factor: $\xi$ commutes with $\cdot e_t$ and $\cdot\vec e_z$ and anticommutes with $\cdot\vec e_x$ and $\cdot\vec e_y$, while every left action is even. The framework's odd mass is not a two-sided action but the conjugation, and its oddness is $K\xi=-\xi K$.
  • The series' off-diagonal mass is a finite Dirac operator: $D_F:\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\mapsto m\overline{\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}}$ on $\mathbb{B}$ anticommutes with the chirality $\xi$ and satisfies $D_F^2=\lvert m\rvert^2e_t$, with the single mass scale $\lvert m\rvert$ carried by both parts, both by inspection in the element form. As an antilinear operator it has no eigenvalues, and the square is the invariant statement. The framework's mass is therefore the almost-commutative triple's finite part and not an added ingredient.
  • The finite carrier is four-complex-dimensional and its finite triple has spectral dimension zero; a dimension-four triple requires the almost-commutative algebra and a first-order elliptic operator. The spectral dimension is the exponent of the growth and $a_{d/2}$ the coefficient of the logarithmic divergence in one and the same small-$r'_t$ expansion.
  • The framework's metric-as-output (a signature from the level-1 form on the material sector) and the triple's distance formula (a distance from $D$) are similar in shape and different in content; the framework derives a signature, not a distance.

This article needs no gamma matrix, and they are collected here only for comparison with the literature, as a frame of $\mathrm{Cl}_{1,3}(\mathbb{C})=M_4(\mathbb{C})$ and not as a different algebra.

Each gamma is the array, in the basis $e_t,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z$ of the algebra over $\mathbb{C}$, of one two-sided action:

$$ \gamma_0:\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\longmapsto ie_t\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\vec e_x,\qquad \gamma_1:\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\longmapsto i\vec e_x\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\vec e_y,\qquad \gamma_2:\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\longmapsto i\vec e_y\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\vec e_y,\qquad \gamma_3:\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\longmapsto i\vec e_z\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\vec e_y,\qquad \gamma_5:\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\longmapsto-ie_t\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\vec e_z. $$

They satisfy $\gamma_0^2=I_4$, $\gamma_k^2=-I_4$, $\gamma_5^2=I_4$, $\{\gamma_\mu,\gamma_\nu\}=2\varepsilon_\mu\delta_{\mu\nu}I_4$ with $\varepsilon=(1,-1,-1,-1)$, $\{\gamma_5,\gamma_\mu\}=0$, and $\gamma_0\gamma_1\gamma_2\gamma_3=-i\gamma_5$; $\gamma_0$ and $\gamma_5$ are Hermitian and the three vector generators anti-Hermitian, and the pattern of squares is the signature $(+,-,-,-)$ of the corpus. All of it was checked on the arrays.

$\gamma_5$ is the chiral right action written as an array. The chirality of the body is right multiplication by $-i\vec e_z$, and $\gamma_5$ multiplies by $-i\vec e_z$ as well, from the right; the factor $e_t$ in front changes nothing, $e_t$ being the identity of the algebra. The central grading is a different object, $\chi=i\tilde{Ζ}_{\mathrm{ad}}=e_+-e_-$, the difference of the two central idempotents of the complexified algebra, with $\chi^2=e_t$ while $\tilde{Ζ}_{\mathrm{ad}}$ itself is a root of $-1$.

The dictionary is complex-linear. Writing $c(M)$ for the coefficient array of $M$ in the basis of the sixteen products, a single product is a pure tensor, the products form the seven-dimensional cone $\operatorname{rank}c(M)\le1$, and a general element is a sum of at most four, with $\ell(M)=\operatorname{rank}c(M)$.

The four generators of the Euclidean frame all square to $+1$, so over $\mathbb{R}$ they generate $\mathrm{Cl}_{4,0}\cong M_2(\mathbb{H})$. The real operators of The Majorana Equation in Biquaternionic Form are right multiplication by $\vec e_x$ and the three maps $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\mapsto\vec e_k\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\vec e_y$, one of square $-1$ and three of square $+1$, and those generate $\mathrm{Cl}_{3,1}\cong M_4(\mathbb{R})$; the Euclidean frame is that frame with the first multiplied by $i$ and the three spatial operators negated. The corpus's $\mathrm{Cl}_{1,3}$ generators are $i$ times those, with the opposite pattern of squares, and $\mathrm{Cl}_{1,3}\cong M_2(\mathbb{H})$. The gammas are the same operators with the powers of $i$ collected — $\gamma_0$ is $E_4$ and $\gamma_k$ is $-iE_k$ — so they carry the Minkowski pattern of squares: this is the frame of the body's $\xi$, not a second algebra.

Summary

The spectral triple of the biquaternion algebra is the candidate $(\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{H},\mathcal{D})$, with $\mathcal{H}$ the completion of the algebra under the Hermitian form $$ \langle\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}},\tilde{Ξ}\rangle_*=\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}^*\tilde{Ξ}\bigr), $$ and the operator is the framework's Dirac operator, $$ \mathcal{D}=i\tilde\nabla+D_F, $$ with the mass $D_F:\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\mapsto m\overline{\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}}$, which couples the two chirality parts through the conjugation. The field is an element $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}=\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}+\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}$, and the two letters are the whole of the chirality. The algebra's real-form involution is $\flat=-{}^{*}$, with $$ \flat^2=+1, $$ and it is antilinear, so the candidate real structure $J_{\flat}$ has $J_{\flat}^2=+1$, that is $\epsilon=+1$, the sign that four-dimensional Euclidean geometry carries.

The sign that the algebra's involution does not carry is the quaternionic one. The Euclidean frame's own operator gives the antilinear operator $J_C=CK$ with $J_C^2=-1$ and $J_C\xi=-\xi J_C$, and that is not a four-dimensional charge conjugation but the pattern the Standard-Model finite geometry carries at $n\equiv6$. The framework's $J_{\flat}$ reaches only the first of the three signs: composed with the chirality it gives neither $+\xi$ nor $-\xi$. Both signs of $J_C$ were verified explicitly, and reaching the quaternionic one requires an antilinear operator of square $-1$, which the algebra carries as $C$ composed with the conjugation, not a central phase.

Three conditions fail, and they fail for one reason: the coefficients of the gradient are elements of the algebra. The bounded-commutator condition holds for the functions and fails for the non-central elements under the left action, while the right action passes it but is not a representation on the field. The first-order condition holds for the vector-like candidate, whose mass is a multiple of the identity, but that candidate has no odd part; for the chiral candidate it holds exactly on the pairs in which one of the two multipliers is fixed by the conjugation, that is, on the real sector $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$. The grading itself is the finite chirality $\xi$, the right action by $-i\vec e_z$, which commutes with the gradient and anticommutes with the mass, so the finite triple is graded and odd while the whole operator on $\mathbb{B}$ is graded by nothing, and a graded triple would need $G_M\otimes\xi$. Two more stand with them: the framework's finite operator is antilinear, and a spectral triple asks for a self-adjoint linear $D$; the algebra's linear odd actions have negative squares, $-|m|^2e_t$, so they are not the mass. And $\mathcal{D}$ is self-adjoint only for the indefinite Dirac form.

The spectral dimension is read from the heat-kernel growth $\mathrm{Tr}\,\exp(-r'_t D^2)\sim {r'_{t}}^{-d/2}$ and coincides with the Seeley–DeWitt data of the determinant and anomaly articles, giving $d=4$ for the almost-commutative triple and $d=0$ for the bare finite algebra. The distance formula's metric-as-output is the spectral triple's counterpart of the framework's signature-as-output, similar in shape and different in content. Inner fluctuations, the gauge group, and the spectral action $\mathrm{Tr}\,f(D_A/\Lambda)$ are the standard neighbouring construction, cited and not developed here.
