## The Action in Momentum Space: The Form on a Mode

$\mathcal{F}$ is a square of a linear form in $A$; $\mathcal{M}$ is a Hermitian square. A square of a linear form is bilinear; a Hermitian square is sesquilinear, and the whole of the phase behaviour follows: the first picks up the transformation's factor twice and the second cancels it.

The split is the parent's and it is the parent's complaint in one line. The parent's §*The Field Term and the Charge* observes that a complex bivector has twelve real components and the algebra's field strength has six, so the field term cannot be stored in one field strength. At a mode the same observation is the statement that $\mathcal{F}$ depends on $A$ only through the four numbers $[\,\pi A\,]$ and its square, so the field term of a complex amplitude is one complex number and its conjugate is not in the term.

$\mathcal{M}$ is a function of all four components and their conjugates; $\mathcal{F}$ is a function of one linear combination. That asymmetry is why the mass term is the one that fixes the normalisation and the field term is the one that leaves a direction free, and it is the frequency-space form of the parent's "the field term's status is stated precisely rather than assumed".

$\mathcal{F}$ is complex for a complex amplitude and real for a material one; $\mathcal{M}$ is real always. **So the action at a mode is $\mathrm{Re}(\mathcal{F})+\mathcal{M}$**, and the imaginary part of $\mathcal{F}$ is the part the action discards — the same discard the parent records at $\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Sc}$.

What the imaginary part does is carry the phase's mismatch. ### Zero, Pole and the On-Shell Action

The coupled Proca mode action is $\mathcal F+\mathcal M$ with $\mathcal F=\tfrac12[\pi A]^{2}$ and $\mathcal M=\tfrac124\pi^{2}\nu_m^{2}A^{\dagger}A$, and $\pi_{\imath}=h\nu_{\imath}-qA_{\imath}$. Its stationarity is the coupled symbol, so the kernel of the form is the symbol again; what differs from the scalar case is that **the mass term makes the form positive on the transverse directions and indefinite on the longitudinal one**, so the vanishing of the form is one condition with a different character on each subspace.

The propagator at the wave number is the form's inverse, and the inverse fails exactly where the form is singular. For the Proca field the singularity is at the mass shell and nowhere else, because the mass term removes the longitudinal degeneracy that the massless form has: the determinant is $\nu_m^{2}$ times the transverse determinant, so the only zeros are the two mass-shell branches. That is the precise content of the article's observation that the two terms have different algebraic characters.

The on-shell action vanishes, and here that is a stronger statement. On a solution the amplitude is a kernel vector, so $\mathcal F+\mathcal M=0$; but on shell $\mathcal F=-\mathcal M$ term by term, because the mass term is what the gradient term contributes at the pole. The mode action is zero because the two halves cancel, not because each is zero, and the cancellation is the Proca field's own signature.

At a constant connection the shift $\pi_{\imath}=h\nu_{\imath}-qA_{\imath}$ moves the zero exactly as in the scalar case: the mass-shell locus is translated, not deformed. The mass does not move, which is why the coupled article's section on the charge and the energy says that the charge has no mass and the energy has mass-squared.

It does not claim that a zero at negative frequency is a negative energy. The transform's two branches are the article's Legendre section, and the sign distinction belongs there. Zero and pole are statements about the propagator; the branch is a statement about the transform.

Because the determinant carries $\nu_m^{2}$ as a factor, the longitudinal mode's pole is at a fixed coefficient and not at a running one. The longitudinal coefficient is the mass alone, and the article's measurement of it — $0.245$ at $2\pi\nu_m=0.7$ and $0.045$ at $2\pi\nu_m=0.3$, at every frequency in the range — is a numerical restatement of that factor.

## The Structural Consequence: The Two Terms Transform Differently

**Under a constant central phase the mass term does not move and the field term rotates at twice the angle.** With $\alpha$ a constant and $A\mapsto e^{i\alpha}A$,

$$
\mathcal{M}\bigl(e^{i\alpha}A\bigr)=\mathcal{M}(A) ,
\qquad
\mathcal{F}\bigl(e^{i\alpha}A\bigr)=e^{2i\alpha}\,\mathcal{F}(A) ,
$$

the first because $e^{-i\alpha}e^{i\alpha}=1$ and the second because the two factors of the square each carry one power. Measured on a random complex amplitude at $\pi_t=1.2$, $\vec\pi=(0.3,0.2,0.1)$, $2\pi\nu_m=0.7$:

| $\alpha$ | $\mathcal{M}$ | $\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert$ | $\arg\bigl(\mathcal{F}(e^{i\alpha}A)/\mathcal{F}(A)\bigr)$ | $2\alpha$ mod $2\pi$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $0$ | $0.169050$ | $0.158799$ | $0.000000$ | $0.000000$ |
| $0.7$ | $0.169050$ | $0.158799$ | $+1.400000$ | $+1.400000$ |
| $1.9$ | $0.169050$ | $0.158799$ | $-2.483185$ | $+3.800000$ |
| $3.1$ | $0.169050$ | $0.158799$ | $-0.083185$ | $+6.200000$ |

The mass term's value is identical in every row to six digits, and the field term's modulus is identical in every row while its argument advances by exactly twice the phase. So the field term is invariant in modulus and not in argument, which is the precise statement the parent makes in prose.

The parent's "only when the phase's square is one" is the case $\alpha\in\{0,\pi\}$. At $\alpha=\pi$ the factor is $e^{2i\pi}=1$ and the field term returns to itself; at every other angle it does not. **So invariance holds on a discrete two-element set and nowhere else**, and the article's table is that sentence with numbers.

The mass term alone would give a conserved charge for every constant $\alpha$; the sum gives one only at $\alpha\in\{0,\pi\}$, which is no continuous symmetry at all. So the coupled action's phase symmetry is not a symmetry of the action as written, and the parent's record of the field term's exact non-invariance is the same statement.

What an invariant field term would need is a sesquilinear structure, and the algebra's single field strength does not supply one. A sesquilinear term would pair $\tilde F$ with its conjugate, which is the parent's $\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde F\overline{\tilde F'})$, and in frequency space that pairing needs a second field strength to conjugate. The missing second half is the parent's twelve-minus-six, and the article's display of $e^{2i\alpha}$ is the arithmetic of the same deficit.
