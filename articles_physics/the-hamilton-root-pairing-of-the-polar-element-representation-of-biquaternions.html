The Hamilton Root Pairing of the Polar Element Representation of Biquaternions

Introduction

The Hamilton root pairing is the axis-angle reading of the polar word. It writes a biquaternion as a modulus times a complex cosine plus a complex unit axis times the complex sine,

$$ \tilde Q=\rho\bigl(\cos\Theta+\vec\Xi\,\sin\Theta\bigr), \qquad \rho=\sqrt{\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}}, $$

with $\vec\Xi$ a complex unit vector, $\vec\Xi^2=-1$, and $\Theta$ a complex angle. The modulus carries the scale and the phase; the axis and the angle carry the boost and the rotor.

The construction reads the data from the element. Write

$$ \tilde Q=Q_te_t+\vec Q, \qquad B^2=\vec Q\cdot\vec Q=Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2. $$

Then, when $B\neq0$ and the norm does not vanish,

$$ \vec\Xi=\frac{\vec Q}{B}, \qquad \cos\Theta=\frac{Q_t}{\rho}, \qquad \sin\Theta=\frac{B}{\rho}. $$

The name records the root: $B$ is the square root of the vector square, and $\vec\Xi$ is the root direction. The angle is complex, and its real and imaginary parts are the rotation-like and boost-like data of the element.

Conventions. The algebra is $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$ with basis $e_t,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z$ and central $i$. An element is $\tilde Q=Q_te_t+\vec Q$, with $\vec Q=Q_x\vec e_x+Q_y\vec e_y+Q_z\vec e_z$ and complex coefficients. The norm is $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=\tilde Q\tilde Q^{\natural}=Q_t^2+\lvert\vec Q\rvert^2$, where $\lvert\vec Q\rvert^2=Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2$ is the complex Euclidean square. The polar word is $\tilde Q=r\exp(i\alpha)\tilde U_{\mathrm b}\tilde u_{\mathrm r}$, with $\rho=r\exp(i\alpha)$. The symbols $r,\alpha,\theta,\vec u_{\mathrm r}$ are those of The Polar Element Representation of Biquaternions; $\psi$ is the parameter of the Hermitian boost factor, defined by its eigenvalues $e^{\pm\psi/2}$, and $\vec u_{\mathrm b}$ is its axis.

The vector part $\vec Q$ is a complex vector; its square $B^2$ is complex. The square root $B$ is defined up to sign, and the sign is correlated with the sign of $\Theta$.

The Vector Part and Its Root

The vector part of the biquaternion is

$$ \vec Q=Q_x\vec e_x+Q_y\vec e_y+Q_z\vec e_z, \qquad \vec Q\cdot\vec Q=Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2. $$

In the quaternion algebra the square of a pure vector is minus its Euclidean square,

$$ \vec Q^2=-\bigl(Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2\bigr)e_t=-B^2e_t. $$

The complex number $B^2$ is the root datum. Its square root $B$ is the complex length of the vector part. The unit axis is

$$ \vec\Xi=\frac{\vec Q}{B}, \qquad \vec\Xi^2=-e_t, $$

because $\vec Q^2=-B^2e_t$. The axis is a complex unit vector: its components are complex, and its square is $-1$ in the algebra. It is not a point of the real axis sphere unless $\vec Q$ is a complex multiple of a real vector.

The two extreme cases are worth naming.

  • A real vector part. If the coefficients $Q_x,Q_y,Q_z$ are real, then $B^2=Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2\ge0$, so $B$ is real for $B^2>0$ and zero for $B^2=0$; a real vector part never gives an imaginary root. An imaginary root requires complex coefficients with $B^2<0$.
  • A null complex vector part. If $B^2=0$ but $\vec Q\neq0$, the axis cannot be normalized. The vector part is a null complex vector; it has a direction but no length. This is the boundary of the Hamilton construction, and it is not the same as the null cone of the whole element.

The Root Pairing

Theorem (the Hamilton form). Let $\tilde Q=Q_te_t+\vec Q$ with $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}\neq0$ and $\vec Q\cdot\vec Q\neq0$. Put

$$ B=\bigl(\vec Q\cdot\vec Q\bigr)^{1/2}, \qquad \vec\Xi=\frac{\vec Q}{B}, \qquad \cos\Theta=\frac{Q_t}{\rho}, \qquad \sin\Theta=\frac{B}{\rho}. $$

Then

$$ \tilde Q=\rho\bigl(\cos\Theta+\vec\Xi\sin\Theta\bigr). $$

Proof. The definitions give $\vec\Xi B=\vec Q$ and $\rho\cos\Theta=Q_t$. The identity $\cos^2\Theta+\sin^2\Theta=1$ is the norm identity $Q_t^2+B^2=\rho^2$, which is $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=\rho^2$. Hence

$$ \rho(\cos\Theta+\vec\Xi\sin\Theta)=Q_t+\vec Q. $$

The two equations for $\Theta$ determine it up to the correlated sign of $B$ and $\Theta$, which is the branch ambiguity of the square root. $\square$

The form is the biquaternion analogue of the quaternion axis-angle representation. In the quaternion algebra, a unit quaternion is $\tilde q=\cos(\theta/2)+\mu\sin(\theta/2)$ with $\mu$ a real unit pure quaternion. The Hamilton form replaces the real angle by a complex angle, the real axis by a complex axis, and the unit modulus by the complex modulus $\rho$. When the element is a real quaternion, the complex data collapse to the real data and the form is the quaternion axis-angle form.

The four factors in the Hamilton data. The modulus $\rho=r\exp(i\alpha)$ carries the scale and the phase. The axis $\vec\Xi$ carries the direction of the boost and the rotor. The angle $\Theta$ carries their magnitudes. The pairing is therefore a different split of the same four factors: the central pair on the modulus, the non-central pair on the axis and angle.

The Angle and the Two Kinds of Rotation

The angle $\Theta$ is complex, and the two parts have different physical meanings. The clean cases are the pure rotor and the pure boost.

A pure rotor. Take $\tilde Q=\tilde u_{\mathrm r}=\cos(\theta/2)e_t+\sin(\theta/2)\vec u_{\mathrm r}$ with $\vec u_{\mathrm r}$ real and unit. Then

$$ \rho=1, \qquad B=\sin\frac{\theta}{2}, \qquad \vec\Xi=\vec u_{\mathrm r}, \qquad \Theta=\frac{\theta}{2}. $$

The angle is real and is half the rotation angle. The axis is real. The Hamilton form is the ordinary quaternion axis-angle form.

A pure boost. Take $\tilde Q=\tilde U_{\mathrm b}=\cosh(\psi/2)e_t+i\sinh(\psi/2)\vec u_{\mathrm b}$. Then

$$ \rho=1, \qquad B=i\sinh\frac{\psi}{2}, \qquad \vec\Xi=\vec u_{\mathrm b}, \qquad \Theta=+i\frac{\psi}{2}. $$

The angle is imaginary and its imaginary part is plus the half-rapidity. The axis is real. The boost is the imaginary-angle case of the same form.

A general element. The axis is complex and the angle is complex. The real part of $\Theta$ is the rotation-like angle and the imaginary part is the boost-like angle; when the axis is complex the two are mixed, and they are not simply the polar $\theta/2$ and $\psi/2$ separately. The polar data are recovered from the modulus and from the complex axis-angle pair, not by reading the two parts of $\Theta$ as independent angles.

The sign conventions are the branch conventions of the square root. The replacement $B\mapsto-B$ sends $\vec\Xi\mapsto-\vec\Xi$ and $\Theta\mapsto-\Theta$, which leaves $\vec\Xi\sin\Theta$ unchanged. The replacement $\rho\mapsto-\rho$ sends $\Theta\mapsto\Theta+\pi$, which leaves both terms unchanged. The form is unique once the principal modulus and the branch of $B$ are fixed.

The Physical Reading

The Hamilton form is the axis-angle reading of a Lorentz element. The complex axis $\vec\Xi$ is the direction of the transformation, and the complex angle $\Theta$ is its magnitude. The real part of the angle is the rotation, the imaginary part is the boost, and the complex axis is the common direction in which the two are combined.

In the physics normalisation, the Lorentz rotor is $\tilde U_L=\exp(i\tfrac{\psi}{2}\vec u_{\mathrm b})\tilde u_{\mathrm r}$. Its Hamilton data have

$$ \rho=1, \qquad \vec\Xi\ \text{complex}, \qquad \Theta\ \text{complex}. $$

The pure boost and the pure rotation are the two real sections of the complex angle: the real angle is the rotation, the imaginary angle is the boost. A general Lorentz element is a point of the complexified rotation group, and the Hamilton form is its axis-angle coordinate.

The reading is a dictionary, not a new dynamics. It says that the four factors of the polar word can be read as a modulus and a complex axis-angle pair, and that the axis-angle pair is the natural coordinate for the Lorentz group. It does not say that the complex angle is a physical angle of a single frame; the frame data are the real and imaginary parts separately.

The Domain and the Degenerate Cases

The Hamilton construction has two conditions.

  • $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}\neq0$. The modulus $\rho$ is nonzero. This is the domain of the polar word.
  • $\vec Q\cdot\vec Q\neq0$. The root $B$ is nonzero and the axis can be normalized. This is the condition that the vector part has a complex length.

The two conditions are independent.

A pure scalar. If $\vec Q=0$, then $B=0$ and the axis is undefined. The element is $Q_te_t$, a central complex number, and the polar word is the complex polar form $Q_t=r\exp(i\alpha)$. The Hamilton form degenerates to the scalar case; the axis-angle data are empty and the modulus carries everything.

A null complex vector part. Take

$$ \tilde Q=e_t+\vec e_x+i\vec e_y. $$

The vector part is $\vec Q=\vec e_x+i\vec e_y$, and

$$ \vec Q\cdot\vec Q=1+i^2=0. $$

The root $B$ vanishes and the Hamilton construction fails, although the norm is

$$ \langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=1+1+i^2=1\neq0. $$

The polar word exists; the Hamilton axis-angle form does not. The vector part is a null complex vector: it has a direction but no complex length. The scalar part carries the norm. The example is the boundary of the Hamilton construction, and it is not a zero divisor of the algebra.

A purely imaginary element. Take $\tilde Q=i(\vec e_x+\vec e_y)$. The vector part is $i(\vec e_x+\vec e_y)$, whose square is $-2$, so $B=i\sqrt2$ and the Hamilton construction works:

$$ \vec\Xi=\frac{\vec e_x+\vec e_y}{\sqrt2}, \qquad \Theta=\frac{\pi}{2}, \qquad \rho=i\sqrt2. $$

The real quaternion part of this element vanishes, so the quaternion construction fails while the Hamilton construction succeeds. The two domains are different: the Hamilton form needs the vector part, the quaternion form needs the real part.

The null element. Take $\tilde Q=i e_t+\vec e_x$. The norm is zero, so the polar word fails. The Hamilton construction also fails, at $\rho=0$. The failure of the norm is the failure of both.

A Worked Example

Take the reference element

$$ \tilde Q=(1+i)e_t+\vec e_x+2\vec e_y. $$

The vector part is $\vec Q=\vec e_x+2\vec e_y$, with

$$ B^2=1+4=5, \qquad B=\sqrt5=2.236067977, \qquad \vec\Xi=\frac{\vec e_x+2\vec e_y}{\sqrt5}. $$

The norm is $5+2i$, with $\rho=2.320595787\exp(0.190253189i)$. The angle is determined by

$$ \cos\Theta=\frac{1+i}{\rho}=0.504639332+0.341657461i, \qquad \sin\Theta=\frac{\sqrt5}{\rho}=0.946188580-0.182219271i. $$

Hence

$$ \Theta=1.080834538-0.378136610i. $$

The reconstruction is

$$ \rho\bigl(\cos\Theta+\vec\Xi\sin\Theta\bigr) =(1+i)e_t+\vec e_x+2\vec e_y, $$

to $4.5\times10^{-15}$. The real part of the angle is $1.080834538$, the imaginary part is $-0.378136610$, and the latter is $-\psi/2$ for this example, with the branch of $B$ fixed as in the table. The axis is real in this example; it is complex in general.

The example has $B\neq0$ and $\rho\neq0$, so the Hamilton construction and the polar word are both available. The modulus $\rho$ carries the scale and phase; the axis and angle carry the boost and rotor. The reconstruction is the test that the branch of the complex angle is the one that matches the principal modulus.

The Hamilton Pairing and the Other Pairings

The Hamilton form and the quaternion form are the two partial constructions of the polar word. They read different parts of the element.

  • The quaternion pairing reads the real and imaginary quaternion parts, $\tilde q_r$ and $\tilde q_i$, and constructs the modulus and the angle from their ratio. It needs $\tilde q_r\neq0$.
  • The Hamilton pairing reads the scalar and vector parts, $Q_t$ and $\vec Q$, and constructs the modulus and the complex axis-angle from their ratio. It needs $\vec Q\cdot\vec Q\neq0$.

The two domains are different. The element $e_t+\vec e_x+i\vec e_y$ has a real part and a quaternion construction but no Hamilton root; the element $i(\vec e_x+\vec e_y)$ has a Hamilton root but no quaternion construction. The polar word exists on both, because it needs only the norm.

The Cartan pairing reads the Hermitian and unitary factors, $\tilde H=\sqrt{\tilde Q\tilde Q^{*}}$ and $\tilde U=\tilde H^{-1}\tilde Q$. It is the matrix polar decomposition and needs only the norm. The three pairings are three readings of one word: the quaternion pairing separates scale and rotor from phase and boost; the Hamilton pairing separates the modulus from the complex axis-angle; the Cartan pairing separates the positive Hermitian factor from the unitary factor.

Summary

The Hamilton root pairing writes a biquaternion as $\rho(\cos\Theta+\vec\Xi\sin\Theta)$, with $\rho$ the complex modulus, $\vec\Xi$ the complex unit axis and $\Theta$ the complex angle. The construction is $B^2=\vec Q\cdot\vec Q$, $\vec\Xi=\vec Q/B$, $\cos\Theta=Q_t/\rho$, $\sin\Theta=B/\rho$, valid when the norm and the vector square are nonzero. The real angle is the rotation, the imaginary angle is the boost, and the complex axis is their common direction. A null complex vector part has no axis-angle form although the polar word may exist; a pure scalar has no axis at all. The form is the biquaternion analogue of the quaternion axis-angle representation and one of the three pairings of the polar word.
