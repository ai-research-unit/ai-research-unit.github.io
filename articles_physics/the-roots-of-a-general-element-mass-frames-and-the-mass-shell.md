# __The Roots of a General Element: Mass, Frames and the Mass Shell__

## Introduction

The root problem of the biquaternion algebra asks for the elements that square to a given element. The two companion articles answer it for the central values $0$ and $+1$. A general radicand $\tilde Q$ is the question of this article,

$$
\boxed{\;\tilde{Ζ}^2=\tilde Q\;}.
$$

**Notation, once.** The imaginary unit is the central $i$ of $\mathbb{B}$, with $i^2=-1$; every double $i$ is reduced before a formula is read. The star and the quaternion sign are the two conjugations of *Algebra and Element Conventions*, and the derivative is the gradient $\tilde\nabla$ of *Operator and Calculus Conventions*, with quaternion conjugate $\tilde\nabla^{\natural}$ and d'Alembertian $\Box$; on the material sector $\Box$ reads $\Delta-\partial^2/\partial {r'_{t}}^2$, a derived form. The side is position: $a\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}$ multiplies on the left and $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}} b$ on the right. The chirality is the operator $\Gamma$ of the corpus, a right action; the Dirac bilinear is $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}^*i\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\vec e_x$. For a radicand with vector part $\vec Q$, $B$ is the modulus of the vector part, $B^2=(\vec Q,\vec Q)$.

The answer depends on the product, and the first job of the article is to keep the products apart. The **plain square** $\tilde{Ζ}^2$ has a finite root set for a non-scalar radicand, generically four elements, falling to two or to none. The **quaternionic square** $\tilde{Ζ}\tilde{Ζ}^{\natural}=\langle\tilde{Ζ}\rangle_{\natural}e_t$ has the level set of the interval as its root set, and on the material sector that level set is the mass shell. The **Hermitian square** $\tilde{Ζ}\tilde{Ζ}^{*}$ produces a positive semidefinite value; the eigenvalues of the mass operator are the mass-squareds of a doublet. The three squares are three root problems, not one.

Two first-order writings are kept apart throughout. The **gradient equation** is one-sided: every derivative acts by left multiplication, and it carries the mass shell. The **pair equation** is that equation written once on the pair of fields. The chirality is a right multiplication of the field, and the condensate is a bilinear; neither is a term of the gradient.

The article interprets five physical consequences. The mass shell is a propagator pole; the mass can be a field; the mass can be generated by a condensate; the root can be a geometric map; and the roots are frames. Each interpretation is labelled, and each carries its boundary.

The article owns no theorem. It states the algebraic classification, with its source, and interprets it. The proofs, the case distinctions and the examples are in the mathematics article.

## The Root Problem by Band

### The Plain Square

Write the radicand and the root in scalar and vector parts,

$$
\tilde Q=Q_te_t+\vec Q,
\qquad
\tilde{Ζ}=Ζ_te_t+\vec{Ζ} .
$$

The plain square is

$$
\tilde{Ζ}^2=\bigl(Ζ_t^2-(\vec{Ζ},\vec{Ζ})\bigr)e_t+2Ζ_t\vec{Ζ} .
$$

For a radicand with $\vec Q\neq0$ a root has $Ζ_t\neq0$ and $\vec{Ζ}=\vec Q/(2Ζ_t)$, and $x=Ζ_t^2$ solves the reduced quadratic

$$
4x^2-4Q_tx-(\vec Q,\vec Q)=0 ,
$$

whose discriminant is $16n$, with $n=\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}$ the biquaternion norm of the radicand. The roots come in opposite pairs $\pm\tilde{Ζ}$. There are **four roots** when $(\vec Q,\vec Q)\neq0$ and $n\neq0$. There are **two** when the discriminant vanishes, $n=0$ with $Q_t\neq0$, and **two** in the null-vector row, $(\vec Q,\vec Q)=0$ with $Q_t\neq0$. There are **none** for a nonzero nilpotent, $(\vec Q,\vec Q)=0$ and $Q_t=0$. A complex scalar radicand $\tilde Q=Q_te_t$ has the two scalar roots $\pm\sqrt{Q_t}e_t$, joined by a **four-real-parameter family** of pure roots with $(\vec{Ζ},\vec{Ζ})=-Q_t$.

That count is the one given in *Biquaternion Elementary Functions*, §*The Roots*. The four plain roots are the four independent sign choices of the two eigenvalue square roots: the root carries one sign of $\sqrt{\lambda_+}$ where the radicand carries $\lambda_+$, and one sign of $\sqrt{\lambda_-}$ where it carries $\lambda_-$, the two chosen independently. Shifting the two logarithms by $2\pi i$ reproduces the same finite set.

The root set of $0$ is the origin and the nilpotent cone. In the quaternionic band the root set of a nonzero value is a level set, not a cone; in the plain band it is the classified set — finite for a non-scalar radicand, and a finite set together with a four-parameter family for a central scalar. For a **central** nonzero value, every root is a unit: the scalar roots are units, and a pure root has $(\vec{Ζ},\vec{Ζ})\neq0$, so its norm does not vanish. A nonzero element equal to its own square is its own root and is therefore a zero divisor; the unit statement is a statement about central values.

### The Quaternionic Square and the Shell

The quaternionic square of an element is its interval,

$$
\tilde{Ζ}\tilde{Ζ}^{\natural}=\langle\tilde{Ζ}\rangle_{\natural}e_t,
\qquad
\langle\tilde{Ζ}\rangle_{\natural}=Ζ_t^2+(\vec{Ζ},\vec{Ζ}).
$$

The root set of a central value $\lambda e_t$ is the level set $\langle\tilde{Ζ}\rangle_{\natural}=\lambda$. On the material sector $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$, write the four-momentum as

$$
\tilde P_{\Phi}=i\frac{E}{c}e_t+\vec p .
$$

Its norm is

$$
\langle\tilde P_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural}
=\left(\frac{iE}{c}\right)^2+(\vec p,\vec p)
=-\frac{E^2}{c^2}+(\vec p,\vec p),
$$

and the level set $\langle\tilde P_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural}=-m^2c^2$ is the mass shell,

$$
E^2=(\vec p,\vec p)c^2+m^2c^4 .
$$

The root set of the value $-m^2c^2$ in the quaternionic band is the whole shell, a three-dimensional two-sheeted hyperboloid, not a finite set. Every point of the shell is a four-momentum; the root is the four-momentum itself. This is the precise sense in which the root problem is the mass-shell problem: it is the quaternionic root problem, not the plain one.

The mass shell is not the plain root set of a central value. The plain roots of $-m^2c^2$ include the scalar elements $\pm imc\,e_t$, which are the two rest points of the shell, and a four-parameter family of pure roots with $(\vec{Ζ},\vec{Ζ})=m^2c^2$, which are not four-momenta of the shell. The two statements are different and both are correct in their own band.

### The Hermitian Square and the Mass Operator

The Hermitian square is

$$
\tilde{Ζ}\tilde{Ζ}^{*},
$$

a positive semidefinite element of $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$, with non-negative eigenvalues. For a positive semidefinite value $\tilde Q$ the roots are $\tilde Q^{1/2}$ times a $*$-unitary element, one root for each such element: a family of amplitudes that share the value and differ by a $*$-unitary right factor, the analogue of the sign of a scalar root. This is the band in which the mass-squared operator of a doublet lives. The three squares are three root problems: the plain square for the algebraic multiplicity, the quaternionic square for the interval, the Hermitian square for the mass operator.

## The Sign and the Causal Class

On the material slice the sign of a real central value is the causal class of its quaternionic level set. The value $-m^2c^2$ is negative and its level set is the timelike region; the value $0$ is null and its level set is the cone; the value $+m^2c^2$ is positive and its level set is the spacelike region. The magnitude sets the size of the shell: if the reference shell is $\langle\tilde P_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural}=-1$, the shell of mass $m$ is its image under multiplication by $mc$, because the norm is quadratic in the scale.

Invertibility divides the same way. A root of a nonzero central value is a unit, so a tachyon is invertible; among the central values, only $0$ has non-invertible roots. The cone is the wall of the units, and the mass shell never touches it for $m\neq0$.

The sign is not decoration. A mass term built with the wrong conjugation uses the positive-definite Hermitian norm, so its level set is a Euclidean sphere rather than the Lorentz hyperboloid; the choice of conjugation, not a numerical sign, is what puts the physical mass on the timelike side. The algebra supplies both level sets; the physics selects one by the conjugation it uses.

## The Mass Is the Modulus

The quaternionic root set is the level set of the norm; the Hamilton pairing names the invariant that the level set carries. For a four-momentum,

$$
\boxed{\;\tilde P_{\Phi}=\Rho e^{\vec U\Theta}\;},
$$

with the data of *Norm and Pairing Conventions*, §*The Four-Momentum*: $\Rho=imc$, $\vec U=\vec u_p$ and $\Theta=-i\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}$, where $\vec u_p=\vec p/\lvert\vec p\rvert$ for $\vec p\neq0$; at rest the direction drops out, because $\sinh\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}=0$ there. Since $\vec u_p^2=-e_t$ while $(\vec u_p,\vec u_p)=1$, the exponential is trigonometric,

$$
\exp(\vec u_p\Theta)=\cos\Theta+\vec u_p\sin\Theta ,
$$

and the imaginary angle $\Theta=-i\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}$ turns it into $\cosh\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}-i\vec u_p\sinh\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}$. Multiplying by $\Rho=imc$ and reducing $i^2=-1$,

$$
\tilde P_{\Phi}=imc\cosh\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}+mc\,\vec u_p\sinh\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}} .
$$

The mass is $m=\lvert\Rho\rvert/c$. The root set of the value $-m^{2}c^{2}$ is the union of the two sheets of the shell, the two orbits of the restricted Lorentz group: the orbit of $\Rho e^{\vec U\Theta}$ and the orbit of its central negative. Every point of the shell has the same $\lvert\Rho\rvert$ and its own pair $(\vec U,\Theta)$: the same modulus, a different direction and rapidity.

The mass is the modulus of the Hamilton amplitude, not the energy. A boost changes the rapidity $\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}$, hence the energy $mc^{2}\cosh\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}$, and it leaves the modulus $mc$ alone. The same split appears in the spectrum: $\lambda_+\lambda_-=\Rho^{2}$ is the invariant norm, while $\lambda_+/\lambda_-=e^{2\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}}$ carries the rapidity.

**The boundary.** The identification is licensed for the four-momentum chart and the principal branch. For a general element the Hamilton modulus is a complex amplitude, and the mass interpretation is an extra identification. The modulus is also not additive: it is multiplicative under composition, and the mass of a sum is not the sum of the masses.

### The Isospectral Family

Two elements are **isospectral** when their eigenvalues agree with multiplicity. The eigenvalues are $\lambda_\pm=Q_t\pm iB$ with $B^2=(\vec Q,\vec Q)$, so the spectrum as a multiset fixes $Q_t$ and $B^2$; the isospectral family of a generic element is

$$
\mathcal I(\lambda_+,\lambda_-)
=\left\{Q_te_t+\vec Q:\ \vec Q\in\mathbb{C}^{3},\ (\vec Q,\vec Q)=B^{2}\right\},
$$

a complex quadric of four real dimensions. On the material slice $\vec Q\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ and the family is the sphere $\lvert\vec p\rvert=B$: the directions of motion at fixed energy and momentum magnitude.

The family is the set that a spectrum-only measurement leaves open. It is also the common level set of the trace and the norm, the two spectral invariants. A dynamics that conserves both moves inside the family and does not leave it.

A rotation acts transitively on that sphere, so the material family is one rotation orbit. The mass shell is larger: it is the union of the two orbits of the restricted Lorentz group at fixed mass, and a boost changes the trace and the spectrum while preserving the norm. On the whole algebra the family also contains complex vector parts that no real Lorentz transformation reaches; those members are not four-momenta.

The degenerate case splits. At $B=0$ the eigenvalues coincide at $Q_t$, and the family is the isotropic quadric $(\vec Q,\vec Q)=0$ in the vector part, a cone with vertex at the origin of $\vec Q$-space. A repeated eigenvalue has two classes, the scalar $Q_te_t$ and $Q_te_t+\vec Q$ with $\vec Q\neq0$ and $\vec Q^2=0$. The two have the same spectrum and the same norm, and no conjugation of the algebra carries one into the other: the scalar satisfies the linear relation $X-Q_te_t=0$, the other only its square. The minimal polynomial, not the spectrum, separates them.

**The boundary.** The family is a set of elements, not a set of states and not a set of particles. Two isospectral elements need not be related by a physical transformation, and the article does not claim that they are. The interpretation is epistemic: it names what the spectrum leaves undetermined, and the algebra supplies no principle that selects one member.

## The Mass Shell Is a Propagator Pole

The two-point function of a scalar of mass $m$ has the momentum-space denominator

$$
\langle\tilde P_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural}+m^2c^2 ,
$$

up to a sign and a normalisation. The propagator is singular where the denominator vanishes, and that locus is the mass shell. The root set of the quaternionic band and the pole set of the propagator are the same set, seen once as an algebraic level set and once as an analytic singularity.

The Feynman prescription deforms the shell,

$$
m^2c^2\longmapsto m^2c^2-i\epsilon ,
$$

and the deformation is a displacement into the complex plane. The algebra supplies the complex plane in which the displacement lives; it does not select the contour. The retarded, advanced and Feynman propagators differ by which side of the real axis the poles are displaced to, and that choice is a boundary condition, not an algebraic identity.

**The boundary.** The pole is a property of the propagator, not of a single root. The root is a four-momentum; the propagator is a correlation function, and its pole is where the correlation function diverges. The $i\epsilon$ is a prescription, not a derivation; the algebra gives a home for the $\epsilon$ but no principle that chooses the Feynman contour over the retarded or the advanced one.

## The Mass Can Be a Field

The scalar mass can be promoted to a biquaternion field. In the Einstein–Mayer promotion of the Lanczos system the mass $m$ becomes $\tilde E_{\Psi}$, and the second-order equations for the two field components $\tilde Q_1,\tilde Q_2$ become eigenvalue equations,

$$
\Box\tilde Q_1=\tilde Q_1\bigl(\tilde E_{\Psi}\tilde E_{\Psi}^{*}\bigr),
\qquad
\Box\tilde Q_2=\tilde Q_2\bigl(\tilde E_{\Psi}^{*}\tilde E_{\Psi}\bigr).
$$

The two mass-squareds of the doublet are the eigenvalues of $\tilde E_{\Psi}\tilde E_{\Psi}^{*}$, equivalently of $\tilde E_{\Psi}^{*}\tilde E_{\Psi}$ for the nonzero spectrum. The mass is no longer a number multiplying the field; it is the element $\tilde E_{\Psi}\tilde E_{\Psi}^{*}$ multiplying the field from the right.

Three cases show the range the corpus uses. A real scalar $\tilde E_{\Psi}=s e_t$ gives $\tilde E_{\Psi}\tilde E_{\Psi}^{*}=s^2e_t$, a degenerate doublet of mass $|s|$. A Hermitian mass field of unit square, $\tilde E_{\Psi}=\tfrac12(e_t+i\vec u_n)$ with $\vec u_n$ a real vector of unit square, gives $\tilde E_{\Psi}\tilde E_{\Psi}^{*}=\tilde E_{\Psi}$, with eigenvalues $1$ and $0$: one massive mode and one massless mode. A singular mass field leaves a massless mode whatever else it does: the two mass-squareds multiply to $\bigl|\langle\tilde E_{\Psi}\rangle_{\natural}\bigr|^2$, because the biquaternion norm is multiplicative and $\langle\tilde E_{\Psi}^{*}\rangle_{\natural}=\overline{\langle\tilde E_{\Psi}\rangle_{\natural}}$, and for the Hermitian element $\tilde E_{\Psi}\tilde E_{\Psi}^{*}$ that product is the product of its two eigenvalues; a vanishing norm therefore forces a zero eigenvalue. The example $\tilde E_{\Psi}=\vec e_x+i\vec e_y$ is singular, with mass-squareds $4$ and $0$. A general mass field gives two distinct nonzero mass-squareds; the three cases above do not.

**The boundary.** The framework derives no numerical mass. The algebra is scale-free, and the mass field is put in by hand in the Einstein–Mayer system; the Petiau system closes it by giving the mass field its own equation, at the cost of nonlinearity. The construction shows that the algebra can carry a mass that is a field with its own equation, and that the mass-squareds of a doublet are the eigenvalues of the element $\tilde E_{\Psi}\tilde E_{\Psi}^{*}$. It does not produce the electron mass or any other.

## The Mass Can Be Generated by a Condensate

The corpus carries two first-order writings, and the condensate is a bilinear of the second. The **gradient equation** is the one-sided chiral pair

$$
\tilde\nabla\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}=k_m\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L},\qquad
\tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}=k_m\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R},
$$

with $\tilde\nabla$ the biquaternionic gradient. Every derivative acts by left multiplication, so no right multiplication is among its terms and no chirality operator can appear. On a plane wave $\tilde\nabla$ acts as left multiplication by $\frac{i\,\tilde{P}_{\Phi}\,p}{\hbar}$, so the mass shell above is the level set of $\langle\tilde P_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural}$. Here $\frac{\hbar}{p}$ is the momentum length. Here $\hbar/(mc)$ is the mass length.

The **pair equation** is the same dynamics written as two first-order lines on the pair of fields,

$$
\tilde\nabla\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}=k_m\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L},
\qquad
\tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}=k_m\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R},
$$

with $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}=(\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L},\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R})$ and each line composed with its partner equal to $\Box I$. Both members are left actions, and no third object stands between them. The pair is not a rewording: $\tilde\nabla^2$ is not scalar, because the mixed spatial derivatives survive as units, while $\tilde\nabla\tilde\nabla^{\natural}=\Box$ is; no one-field first-order equation carries a mass.

The chirality is not a term of either member. It is a right multiplication of the field, and a right multiplication of $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}$ is a left multiplication of the quaternion conjugate, by the exchange of *Operator and Calculus Conventions*: the same operator is the left multiplication of $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}^{\natural}$ by $i\vec e_z$. A chirality needs a right multiplication, and the one-sided gradient has none.

The axial symmetry, the axial current and the condensate are not a second theory: they are what a right multiplication carries, and what the gradient does not.

The massless equation is invariant under the axial rotation

$$
\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\longmapsto\exp(i\alpha\Gamma)\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}
=\cos\alpha\,\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}+\sin\alpha\,\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\vec e_z ,
$$

a right multiplication by an element of the algebra, so it commutes with the left action of the gradient. Read on the pair, the rotation multiplies $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}$ by $e^{-i\alpha}$ and $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}$ by $e^{+i\alpha}$, the two parts named by the subscript; the mass is the term that couples the two members, so the massless pair is invariant and the massive one is not. The axial current and its divergence are recorded there, in *The Chiral Condensate and Dynamical Symmetry Breaking in Biquaternionic Form*.

A nonzero vacuum expectation of the fermion bilinear,

$$
\langle\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}^*i\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\vec e_x\rangle\neq0,
$$

is the order parameter of the breaking. It is the bilinear a mass term multiplies, so the dynamically generated coefficient is

$$
m=-2G\langle\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}^*i\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\vec e_x\rangle,
$$

with $G$ the coupling of the four-fermion interaction. The condensate is a central scalar and is neutral under the vector phase; the vector symmetry survives and the axial one is broken. The Goldstone modes follow from the broken generator.

**The boundary.** The framework has no colour group and no strong gauge dynamics. The compact gauge algebra inside $\mathbb{B}$ is at most $\mathrm{U}(2)$ of real dimension four, and $SU(3)$ is absent, so a QCD-like condensate cannot be derived. The algebra supplies the carrier of the condensate, the broken-symmetry pattern and the sector placement of the order parameter; the coupling, the cutoff, the scale and the numerical value are empirical input. The condensate is also not the real structure $\flat$: it is the linear chiral pairing, not a Majorana-type pairing, and interpreting it through $\flat$ gives the wrong object.

## The Root Can Be a Geometric Map

The Kustaanheimo–Stiefel map carries four coordinates $(\varsigma_1,\varsigma_2,\varsigma_3,\varsigma_0)$ to three,

$$
x_1=2(\varsigma_1\varsigma_3-\varsigma_2\varsigma_0),\qquad
x_2=2(\varsigma_1\varsigma_0+\varsigma_2\varsigma_3),\qquad
x_3=\varsigma_1^2+\varsigma_2^2-\varsigma_3^2-\varsigma_0^2 .
$$

The radius is the biquaternion norm of the quaternion $\varsigma=\varsigma_1\vec e_x+\varsigma_2\vec e_y+\varsigma_3\vec e_z+\varsigma_0e_t$,

$$
\boxed{\;r=\sqrt{x_1^2+x_2^2+x_3^2}=\varsigma_1^2+\varsigma_2^2+\varsigma_3^2+\varsigma_0^2=\langle\varsigma\rangle_{\natural}\;},
$$

and the Coulomb singularity is the reciprocal of the norm,

$$
\frac1r=\frac1{\langle\varsigma\rangle_{\natural}} .
$$

The map is a circle bundle away from the origin: the fiber over a nonzero $x$ is a circle, and $\varsigma$ and $-\varsigma$ are two points of it. The sign pair is the discrete part of the ambiguity; the circle is the gauge freedom of the KS constraint, not a second physical position. The map is a square root of the position in the sense that the three position coordinates are quadratic in the four KS coordinates, while the radius is the algebra's norm of $\varsigma$.

**The boundary.** The transformation, the pseudo-time $dr'_t=r\,ds$, the reduction to the four-dimensional oscillator and the Mehler kernel are standard and imported. The algebra supplies the interpretation: the Coulomb potential is a central scalar, the radius is the norm, and the singularity is the inverse norm. It does not cure the analytic failure of the time-sliced kernel. The sign pair is not two physical positions either: it is one discrete ambiguity inside a circle of representatives, and the circle is the constraint's gauge freedom.

## The Roots Are Frames

The plain root set of a generic element has four elements, two opposite pairs. A nonzero central scalar has a continuum: two scalar roots and a four-parameter family of pure roots. The algebra does not select one element of the set. A calculation chooses a root, and the choice fixes the sign and the branch of the square. That choice is what the article calls a **frame**.

The word is not a Lorentz frame and not a gauge orbit. It names the fact that the root is a representative: the value $\tilde Q$ is fixed, the root is one of its square roots, and the passage from one root to another is a discrete sign or a continuous family, not a symmetry of the value. In the quaternionic band the interpretation is sharper. The root set of $-m^2c^2$ is the mass shell, and every point of the shell is a frame in this sense: a representative four-momentum, a choice of energy and momentum that realizes the same invariant mass. The word names the representative, not an observer's basis. The shell is the set of representatives, not a set of particles; a particle is a state, and the shell is its kinematical arena.

**The boundary.** The roots are not all related by a physical transformation. The four plain roots are two opposite pairs, not one orbit; the central family is a continuum, not a gauge orbit. The frame interpretation is an interpretation of the multiplicity, not a derivation of a gauge symmetry. The algebra gives the multiplicity; it does not give a principle for choosing one root over another.

## Limits

The article derives no mass, no coupling and no dynamics. The mass field is an input; the condensate needs a dynamics the algebra does not contain; the propagator contour is a boundary condition; the Kustaanheimo–Stiefel transformation is imported. The root set is not a particle spectrum: the mass shell is a set of four-momenta, and the mass-squareds of a doublet are eigenvalues of an operator, not roots. The frame interpretation is not a gauge symmetry.

The bands must be kept apart. The plain root set of a nonzero central value is a finite set together with a four-parameter family; the quaternionic root set is the shell; the Hermitian root set is the mass operator's family. A reader who applies the plain classification to the mass shell will look for a finite root set where there is a hyperboloid, and a reader who applies the quaternionic level set to the plain problem will lose the multiplicity. The distinction is the first result of the article and the boundary of the other four.

## Summary

The root problem $\tilde{Ζ}^2=\tilde Q$ is band-dependent. In the plain band the roots are generically four, falling to two or to none, with a four-parameter family for a central scalar; the root of $0$ is the nilpotent cone. In the quaternionic band the root set of a central value is the level set of the interval, and on the material sector the level set $-m^2c^2$ is the mass shell. In the Hermitian band the root set is the family $\tilde Q^{1/2}$ times a $*$-unitary element, and the mass-squared operator of a doublet lives in that band.

The five interpretations use these sets: the mass shell is a propagator pole; the mass can be a field, with a mass-squared operator whose eigenvalues split a doublet; the mass can be generated by a chiral condensate; the root can be the Kustaanheimo–Stiefel square root of the position; and the roots are frames, the representatives the algebra does not choose among. The mass itself is the Hamilton modulus, $m=\lvert\Rho\rvert/c$, and the shell is its level set. The isospectral family of a generic element is the complex quadric of fixed trace and fixed vector square; on the material slice it is the sphere of directions, and at a repeated eigenvalue it splits into a scalar class and a class with nilpotent vector part.

The mass shell is a statement of the gradient equation; the chirality, the axial current and the condensate are statements of the field's right multiplication, and the two are kept apart. The algebra supplies the sets and the identities; it supplies no numerical mass, no dynamics and no choice of contour.
