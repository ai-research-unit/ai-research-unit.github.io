The Trace Anomaly in Biquaternionic Form

Introduction

A theory that is classically invariant under a local rescaling of the metric has a stress tensor whose trace vanishes, and a quantised version of it generally does not. The equality $T_\mu{}_\mu = 0$ holds for the classical action of a massless field; the regulated one-loop effective action produces a non-vanishing trace, a trace anomaly (or Weyl anomaly), whose coefficient is fixed by the field content and whose form is fixed by the symmetries. The anomaly is not an artifact of a particular regulator: it is the obstruction to defining the regulated determinant in a way that preserves the classical symmetry, and the axial current's $\langle\tilde\nabla,\tilde J_5\rangle_{\natural} \neq 0$ is the same failure of the measure read on the chiral current (Adler 1969; Bell and Jackiw 1969).

This article asks what the biquaternion framework contributes to that statement. The findings are the following.

  1. The quantum trace is a property of the determinant, not of the algebra. The anomaly arises from the regularisation of $\log\det S^{(2)}$, which is the object of The Functional Determinant in Biquaternionic Form. The divergence of that determinant is controlled by the heat-kernel coefficient $a_{d/2}$, and the anomalous trace is the conformal variation of the renormalised determinant. The algebra contributes neither the regularisation nor the divergence; it contributes the operator on which the determinant is taken and the trace that reads off the answer. This is the same division of labour the functional-integral and determinant articles record, and it is stated here at the outset so that the anomaly is not misattributed to the algebra.

  2. The trace that computes an anomaly is the trace of the operator, and the framework's informational trace is not that trace. The anomaly's coefficient counts the dimensions on which the determinant is taken. A biquaternion field is an element of $\mathbb{B}$, with four complex coefficients and eight real components, four in each sector, so the count is four complex or eight real. The informational trace of Norm and Pairing Conventions, the state-and-observable pairing of the Hermitian sector $\mathbb{M}_+$, is a different object: it reads the scalar part of an element and it is not the trace of a determinant. Anomalies and Anomaly Cancellation in Biquaternionic Form keeps the two apart; this article uses its separation.

  3. The Weyl transformation is a level-1 statement, and it must not be confused with the $i c r'_t$ construction of the coordinate. The framework obtains the Lorentzian signature from the level-1 form read on the material sector with the time coordinate written $i c r'_t$: the minus sign is one use of $i^2=-1$, and the signature is an output rather than an input. A Weyl rescaling changes the level-1 form, and therefore changes the level-2 interpretation; it does not change the $i c r'_t$ assignment or the sector split. The anomaly is a statement about the level-1 form's variation under a rescaling, and it is the one place in this subcategory where a conformal rescaling of the primary convention is contemplated. The three levels are kept apart throughout.

  4. The coefficient has a fixed shape, and the algebra contributes only its multiplicity. In four dimensions the trace is $(4\pi)^{-2}(cW^2-aE_4+b\Box R)$, with $a$ and $c$ rational and additive over fields; the spin weights of a scalar, a Dirac fermion, and a vector are $1:11:62$ for $a$ and $1:6:12$ for $c$. The algebra fixes how many copies of the per-component number a biquaternion field contributes, and it fixes nothing else: not the weights, not the curvature invariants, not the scheme-dependent $\Box R$. This is the same division the partition-function, functional-integral, and determinant articles make; it is not a claim that the biquaternion scalar is a multiplicity of complex scalars, for the reason those articles give.

  5. The anomaly and the $\theta$ vacuum are the two structural quantum effects of this group. The trace anomaly is the failure of a conformal symmetry; the $\theta$ vacuum of the next article is the failure of a topological triviality. Both arise from the same place — the regularised determinant, and in particular its non-central part — and the determinant article's distinction between the central case (a determinant that factors) and the non-central case (a determinant with a phase) is what separates them. The trace anomaly is a central statement about a non-central operator; the $\theta$ vacuum is a statement about the phase that the non-central operator acquires.

The article proceeds as follows. The next section fixes what is meant by the trace and recalls the classical vanishing. A section derives the quantum trace from the conformal variation of the determinant and gives the heat-kernel origin of the coefficient, and a section evaluates the coefficient in two and four dimensions. A section separates the informational trace from the trace that a determinant uses and counts the components of the field, and a section treats the Weyl transformation at the three levels of the one metric. A section relates the anomaly to the renormalisation-group $\beta$ function of The Renormalization Group in Biquaternionic Form, and a section records the proper-time representation and the finite-dimensional check of the counting. A section separates what is established from what is interpretation, and the summary, the notation table, and the literature close the article.

The Classical Trace and Its Vanishing

The classical statement is fixed first, because the anomaly is the deviation from it.

The stress tensor and its trace. For a field theory with action $S[Ξ;g]$ on a background metric $g_{\mu\nu}$ the stress tensor is $$ T_{\mu\nu} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{|g|}}\frac{\delta S}{\delta g_{\mu\nu}} , $$ with both indices low, the variation being taken in the components $g_{\mu\nu}$ and no index raised, and the trace is the contraction $T_\mu{}_\mu = g_{\mu\nu}T_{\mu\nu}$. A Weyl transformation is a local rescaling $$ g_{\mu\nu}\;\longrightarrow\;\exp(2\sigma(x))g_{\mu\nu}, \qquad \delta_\sigma g_{\mu\nu} = 2\sigma\,g_{\mu\nu}, $$ and the trace is exactly the response of the action to it: $$ \delta_\sigma S = \int d^dx\,\sqrt{|g|}\;\sigma\,T_\mu{}_\mu . $$ So $T_\mu{}_\mu=0$ is the statement that the classical action is Weyl invariant, and the anomaly is the statement that the quantum effective action is not.

When the classical trace vanishes. For a massless scalar with the conformal coupling $\zeta=(d-2)/(4(d-1))$, for the massless Dirac field, and for the Maxwell field in $d=4$, the classical action is Weyl invariant, and $T_\mu{}_\mu=0$ on shell. A mass term, a curvature coupling with non-critical coefficient, and a dimensionful coupling all break the invariance explicitly; those breakings are not anomalous, because they are already present classically and their coefficients are fixed by the classical action rather than by the regulator. The anomaly is the part that cannot be removed.

The improved tensor. For a scalar the canonical tensor is not traceless even for the massless action at the conformal coupling; adding the improvement term $$ T_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{imp}} = T_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{can}} + \zeta\big(g_{\mu\nu}\Box - \nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu\big)\xi^2 , \qquad \zeta=\frac{d-2}{4(d-1)}, $$ produces $T_\mu{}_\mu=0$ on shell, the bracket reducing in flat coordinates to $\delta_{\mu\nu}\Box-\partial^2/\partial R_\mu\partial R_\nu$, with $\Box$ read on the flat background. The arithmetic is short. The added trace is $\zeta(d-1)\Box\xi^2$, which on shell is $2\zeta(d-1)\sum_\mu(\partial\xi/\partial R_\mu)^2$, because $\Box\xi^2=2\sum_\mu(\partial\xi/\partial R_\mu)^2+2\xi\,\Box\xi$ and the second term vanishes for a massless field, while the canonical trace is $-\tfrac{d-2}{2}\sum_\mu(\partial\xi/\partial R_\mu)^2$; the value $\zeta=(d-2)/(4(d-1))$ cancels the two, giving $\zeta=\tfrac16$ at $d=4$. This is worth recording because it fixes the normalisation against which the quantum trace is measured: the anomaly is a statement about the improved tensor, and a calculation that omits the improvement term reports a classical trace instead of an anomaly.

The $i c r'_t$ interpretation. The flat metric is $\delta_{\mu\nu}=(1,1,1,1)$ on the four $Q_\mu$, and the level-2 interpretation of the level-1 form is the material reading $Q_t=i c r'_t$, $Q_k=x_k$, as Frame and Metric Conventions fixes. The trace $T_\mu{}_\mu$ is the contraction with $\delta_{\mu\nu}$, and it is a level-2 object numerically while being a level-1 statement structurally. This is important for the Weyl transformation: a rescaling of the level-1 form is not a change of the $i c r'_t$ assignment, and the two operations are different. The section on the three levels makes this precise.

The Quantum Trace as the Conformal Variation of the Determinant

The one-loop effective action is the determinant of the second variation, and the anomaly is its conformal variation.

The one-loop trace. From The Generating Functional and the Effective Action in Biquaternionic Form the one-loop effective action is $$ \Gamma_1[\xi;g] = \tfrac12\,\mathrm{Tr}\log S^{(2)}[\xi;g] , $$ with $S^{(2)}$ the second variation of the classical action. Its response to a Weyl transformation is $$ T_\mu{}_\mu\big|_{\text{one loop}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{|g|}}g_{\mu\nu}\frac{\delta\Gamma_1}{\delta g_{\mu\nu}} , $$ and the anomaly is the statement that this is non-zero for a classically Weyl-invariant theory. In the path-integral language the same statement is that the measure $\mathcal{D}Ξ$ is not invariant under the rescaling, or equivalently that the regularised determinant is not; the two languages agree, and both are standard (Capper and Duff 1974; Deser, Duff, and Isham 1976; Duff 1994).

The heat-kernel origin. Regulating the determinant with a proper-time cutoff, the trace of the heat kernel has the small-$r'_t$ expansion $$ \mathrm{Tr}\,\exp(-r'_t\,S^{(2)})\;\sim\;\frac{1}{(4\pi r'_t)^{d/2}}\sum_{k\ge0}{r'_{t}}^{k}\int d^dx\,\sqrt{|g|}\,a_k , $$ where $a_k$ is the Seeley–DeWitt coefficient of order $k$, the first being $a_0=1$. The term that survives renormalisation is $a_{d/2}$, because ${r'_{t}}^{k-d/2}$ is logarithmically divergent exactly at $k=d/2$. The conformal variation of the regularised determinant is $$ \delta_\sigma\Gamma_1 = \frac{1}{(4\pi)^{d/2}}\int d^dx\,\sqrt{|g|}\;\sigma\,a_{d/2} , $$ so that the anomalous trace is proportional to $a_{d/2}$; the proportionality is what the boxed coefficients below encode. This is the standard heat-kernel account (DeWitt 2003; Vassilevich 2003), and it is the reason the anomaly is a one-loop, one-coefficient statement rather than a tower of terms: $a_{d/2}$ is local, and the higher coefficients are not.

The algebra's role in the formula. The heat kernel of $S^{(2)}$ is taken on the space the framework names. If $S^{(2)}$ is a differential operator acting on a biquaternion-valued field, then the trace in $\mathrm{Tr}\,\exp(-r'_t S^{(2)})$ runs over the field's four complex components, and the coefficient $a_{d/2}$ carries that multiplicity as an overall factor. The geometry of the operator supplies the curvature invariants; the algebra supplies the dimension of the space on which the trace is taken. This is the same division as in the determinant article, and it is why the anomaly's multiplicity — as opposed to its form — is the algebra's contribution.

The Coefficients in Two and Four Dimensions

The standard values are recalled, and the multiplicity is exhibited.

Two dimensions. For a massless scalar field on a two-dimensional background the anomalous trace is $$ \boxed{\;T_\mu{}_\mu = \frac{c}{24\pi}\,R\;}, $$ and the central charge is $c=1$ for a real scalar, $c=2$ for a complex scalar, and it is additive over decoupled fields. The coefficient is the Seeley–DeWitt value $a_1=R/6$ of the minimal scalar, since $\frac{1}{4\pi}a_1=R/(24\pi)$; the value $c=1$ per real scalar is the standard one (Polyakov 1981), and it is proportional to the number of real components: the anomaly's coefficient counts components.

Four dimensions. In four dimensions the trace for a free field is a linear combination of the Euler density, the Weyl-square term, and $\Box R$: $$ \boxed{\;T_\mu{}_\mu = \frac{1}{(4\pi)^2}\big(c\,W^2 - a\,E_4 + b\,\Box R\big)\;}, $$ with $E_4=R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}-4R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu}+R^2$ the Euler density and $W^2=W_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}W^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$ the Weyl-square term; the minus on the Euler density is the convention of Duff's review, and a text that writes $+aE_4$ defines $a$ with the opposite sign. In that convention the coefficients are rational functions of the field content, $$ a=\frac{1}{90}\big(n_s+11n_f+62n_v\big), \qquad c=\frac{1}{120}\big(n_s+6n_f+12n_v\big), $$ for $n_s$ real scalars, $n_f$ Dirac fermions, and $n_v$ vector fields: $a=\tfrac{1}{90},\tfrac{11}{90},\tfrac{62}{90}$ and $c=\tfrac{1}{120},\tfrac{1}{20},\tfrac{1}{10}$ for one field of each kind. Both are additive over decoupled fields, and both are independent of the renormalised coupling. The symbol $c$ here is the Weyl-square coefficient and is not the two-dimensional central charge of the previous paragraph; the two share a letter in the literature and nothing else. The $\Box R$ term is the exception to scheme independence: its coefficient is moved by a finite local counterterm, so only $a$ and $c$ are predictions of the field content.

Multiplicity. The pattern in both dimensions is that the anomaly coefficient is the sum over the field's internal components of each component's contribution, weighted by its spin. A field whose internal space is $n$-real-dimensional contributes $n$ times a single real component's coefficient, with the spin weight read off the ratios above: $1:11:62$ for $a$ and $1:6:12$ for $c$. The framework's task is therefore to count the components of a biquaternion field; that is the next section.

The Trace That the Anomaly Uses and the Counting of Components

This is where the algebra enters, and it enters twice: through the trace of the operator and through the component count.

The trace of an element is not the trace of the anomaly. The corpus fixes a trace of an element, $\operatorname{Tr}$, in Norm and Pairing Conventions. It is the state-and-observable pairing of the Hermitian sector $\mathbb{M}_+$, it reads the scalar part of its argument, and it is not the trace of an operator on the field space. A determinant is not taken with it. Calling the two objects by one word is the category error that Anomalies and Anomaly Cancellation in Biquaternionic Form identifies.

The trace an anomaly uses. The determinant of $S^{(2)}$ is taken over the field's components, and the trace that reads it is the internal trace, the trace of the operator in the algebra's four complex dimensions. The identity action has trace four, and the two-sided action has trace four times the product of the scalar parts of its two multipliers: $$ \operatorname{tr}\bigl(\tilde Q\mapsto a\,\tilde Q\,b\bigr)=4\,\mathrm{Sc}(a)\,\mathrm{Sc}(b), \qquad \operatorname{tr}\bigl(\tilde Q\mapsto\tilde Q\bigr)=4 . $$ This is a derived form of the regular trace of Matrix Representations Conventions, read from the multiplication of the units.

The count. The algebra has complex dimension four, and a biquaternion field has four complex coefficients: eight real components, four in each sector. That is the multiplicity the algebra contributes. The coefficient is additive over components, so a biquaternion field contributes four times the per-component coefficient of its spin, eight in a real counting; the spin weights themselves, $1:11:62$ and $1:6:12$, are not the algebra's. This is a component count and not a claim that the field is a multiplet of independent complex scalars, for the reason The Functional Integral in Biquaternionic Form, The Partition Function in Biquaternionic Form, and The Harmonic Oscillator in Biquaternionic Form give.

What the algebra does not fix. The algebra fixes the component count; it does not fix the spin of the field, the background's curvature invariants, or the coefficients of $E_4$ and $W^2$. Those are the standard heat-kernel data and are transcribed. The framework's contribution is the dimension of the field space, exactly as in the determinant article.

The Weyl Transformation at the Three Levels of the One Metric

The one metric is read at three levels in the series, and the Weyl transformation is where they are most likely to be mixed. The levels themselves are fixed in Algebra and Element Conventions and Frame and Metric Conventions; this section states only what the rescaling does to each.

Level 1. The level-1 form is the biquaternion norm of Frame and Metric Conventions, whose metric is $\delta_{\mu\nu}$. A Weyl rescaling is a change of its normalisation, and the anomalous trace measures the response. The rescaling is not a change of the algebra, of the basis, or of the trace: it changes the one form, and that form is the only object the anomaly's definition varies.

Level 2. The level-2 reading is the same form read on the material sector, with $Q_t=i c r'_t$ and $Q_k=x_k$, which is the substitution Frame and Metric Conventions fixes. A Weyl rescaling rescales the level-1 form, and the level-2 coefficients with it, but it does not change the $i c r'_t$ assignment or the sector split. The minus of the material $\Box$ comes from $i^2=-1$ in $\partial/\partial i c r'_t$ and nothing else, and no sign in the anomaly formula is to be "corrected" on the basis of the level-1 form.

Level 3. The third level is the older display, the arrays in which the literature prints the same objects. It is a display, not a convention of this article: Matrix Representations Conventions fixes it, and Operator and Calculus Conventions states why it is legacy. The display enters the anomaly only as the standard transcription of the four-dimensional trace. A calculation in the display and a calculation with biquaternion elements must agree on the anomaly, because the anomaly is a physical coefficient; where they appear to disagree it is the transcription that is wrong.

What the transformation is not. A Weyl rescaling is not a coordinate transformation, since it changes distances without changing coordinates; not the Wick rotation, which in this series is either the exchange of the real and imaginary sectors or the continuation $r'_t\mapsto-i\tau$ of one coordinate, and which rotates rather than rescales all of them; and not a Lorentz transformation, which preserves the level-1 form. Keeping these apart is the content of the section.

The Anomaly and the Renormalisation Group

The anomaly's coefficient and the running coupling's $\beta$ function are two interpretations of the same loop computation, and the corpus's renormalisation conventions fix how they are related.

The relation. For a coupling $g$ that multiplies a marginal operator, the anomalous trace is proportional to the $\beta$ function of the coupling: $$ T_\mu{}_\mu \supset \frac{\beta(g)}{2g}\,O , $$ where $O$ is the operator multiplying the coupling. The relation is standard (the trace anomaly and the $\beta$ function are the same coefficient read in two ways), and it is the reason the anomaly is not removable by a counterterm: removing it would contradict the running that The Renormalization Group in Biquaternionic Form computes.

Scheme dependence. The $a$ and $c$ coefficients are scheme-independent in four dimensions; the coefficient of $\Box R$ is not, and it is adjusted by a finite local counterterm. The anomaly's scheme-independent part is therefore a genuine prediction of the field content, which is exactly what makes it a useful check on the framework's counting. A reader who finds a $\Box R$ coefficient differing between articles is looking at a scheme choice, not at a disagreement.

The anomaly's one-loop exactness. In a conformal theory the anomaly is one-loop exact: the higher loops do not add new terms of the anomalous form. This is the statement that $a_{d/2}$ is local and that the conformal variation of the determinant is exhausted by it, and it is standard. It is also the reason an anomaly computed at one loop in the framework is final, and it is worth stating because it is the exception rather than the rule for one-loop results.

The non-central case. Where the determinant does not factor — the massive operator, whose mass term carries one chirality value into the other — the determinant acquires a phase, the eta invariant of the operator family. The trace anomaly remains the curvature statement; the phase is its topological partner, and the $\theta$ vacuum is what reads it.

The Chiral Pair and the Mass

The chiral pair. The derivatives are the gradient and its quaternion conjugate, fixed in Operator and Calculus Conventions, §The Gradient; $\Box$ is the scalar composition of the two. The massive dynamics is the chiral pair of The Dirac Equation in Biquaternionic Form, and its two letters are the two values of the chirality: $\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}_L$ is the $-1$ part and $\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}_R$ the $+1$ part. No one-field first-order massive equation stands beside the pair, because the square of the gradient is not scalar. In the coordinates $Q_\mu$ it is $$ \tilde\nabla^2=\frac{\partial^2}{\partial R_t^2}-\Delta_Q+2\sum_k\vec e_k\frac{\partial^2}{\partial R_t\,\partial R_k}, $$ where the mixed term survives because $e_t$ commutes with the spatial units, and $\Delta_Q$ is the convention's Laplacian. Only the composition with the conjugate is scalar, and that composition is the series' $\Box$. Both identities are derived forms, read from the multiplication of the units.

The mass. The mass term is the right multiplication $\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}\mapsto m\,\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}(i\vec e_x)$, the frame's timelike cell up to the scalar $m$. The chirality cell multiplies on the right as well, and the two cells anticommute, because $\vec e_x\vec e_z=-\vec e_z\vec e_x$. The mass therefore carries $\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}_L$ into a multiple of $\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}_R$ and $\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}_R$ into a multiple of $\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}_L$: it exchanges the two values. This is a derived form, and it was checked on generic elements. The gradient multiplies on the left, where it commutes with the chirality cell, so the free pair carries each value into itself. The massive operator mixes the two values: it is not block-diagonal in them, and its determinant is not the product of the two free determinants.

The phase, and not the curvature coefficient, is the subject of the next article, and the determinant article's central/non-central distinction is what separates them. The framework's statement is that the trace anomaly is the central statement (a scalar coefficient multiplying curvature invariants) while the $\theta$ vacuum is the phase statement, and that the two are read from the same determinant.

The Proper-Time Representation and the Finite-Dimensional Check

Two details of the derivation are recorded here: the proper-time form of the one-loop action and the finite-dimensional identity that isolates the component count.

The one-loop effective action. With $S^{(2)}$ the second variation of the action about the background, the one-loop contribution is $$ \Gamma_1 = \tfrac12\,\mathrm{Tr}\log S^{(2)} = -\tfrac12\int_0^\infty\frac{dr'_t}{r'_{t}}\,\mathrm{Tr}\,\exp(-r'_t S^{(2)}), $$ up to a divergent constant, the proper-time representation of the logarithm. The integrand is the heat kernel of $S^{(2)}$; its small-$r'_t$ expansion is the one given in the section on the quantum trace, and the term $a_{d/2}$ of that expansion is the anomalous coefficient. This is where the anomaly's arithmetic comes from, and the framework's contribution is visible in it as the multiplicity of the trace.

The finite-dimensional shadow. The counting that fixes the coefficient is visible in a Gaussian integral. For a complex $K$ on an $n$-dimensional space, $$ \det\big(\exp(s)K\big)=e^{ns}\det K, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial s}\log\det\big(\exp(s)K\big)\Big|_{s=0}=n, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial s}\det\big(\exp(s)K\big)\Big|_{s=0}=n\det K . $$ The first identity is exact, so the second and third are exact; a central difference with $h=10^{-6}$ returns the dimension to ten digits, and on a space of dimension four it returns $4$. The dimension of the space is the coefficient of the conformal-like rescaling, which is the finite-dimensional image of "the anomaly's coefficient counts components". The count is four on the algebra, the dimension of the field space; the value two belongs to a two-component object, which is not this field.

What Is Established and What Is Interpretation

Established (framework and algebra).

  • The Weyl transformation rescales the level-1 biquaternion norm and leaves the algebra, the basis, the $i c r'_t$ assignment, and the sector split unchanged; a rescaling is not the Wick rotation and not a Lorentz transformation.
  • The trace that the anomaly uses is the trace of an operator of the algebra in its four complex dimensions: the identity action has trace four, and the two-sided action $\tilde Q\mapsto a\tilde Q b$ has trace $4\,\mathrm{Sc}(a)\,\mathrm{Sc}(b)$. The informational trace $\operatorname{Tr}$ of Norm and Pairing Conventions is the $\mathbb{M}_+$ pairing; it reads the scalar part of an element and is not this trace.
  • A field valued on the algebra has four complex coefficients, hence eight real components and four per sector. The anomaly's multiplicity is that component count: four times the per-component coefficient of the field's spin, eight in a real counting. It is a component count and not a multiplicity of complex scalars, for the reason The Functional Integral in Biquaternionic Form, The Partition Function in Biquaternionic Form, and The Harmonic Oscillator in Biquaternionic Form give.
  • The conformal variation of a finite-dimensional Gaussian determinant is proportional to the dimension of the space, $\partial/\partial s\,\log\det(\exp(s)K)\big|_{s=0}=\dim$; the identity is exact, and a central difference returns the dimension to ten digits, with the determinant derivative equal to $\dim\cdot\det K$. This is the finite-dimensional shadow of the statement that the anomaly's coefficient counts components.

Standard, and transcribed.

  • The definition of $T_{\mu\nu}$ as the metric variation of the action, the improvement term, and the classical vanishing of the trace.
  • The one-loop trace as the conformal variation of $\log\det S^{(2)}$; the heat-kernel expansion; the identification of $a_{d/2}$ as the anomalous coefficient.
  • The values $c=1$ per real scalar in two dimensions and the four-dimensional $a$ and $c$ coefficients for scalar, fermion, and vector fields.
  • The relation $T_\mu{}_\mu\supset\frac{\beta}{2g}O$, the scheme dependence of $\Box R$, and the one-loop exactness of the anomaly (Adler–Bardeen non-renormalisation).
  • The proper-time representation $\Gamma_1=-\tfrac12\int_0^\infty\frac{dr'_t}{r'_{t}}\mathrm{Tr}\,\exp(-r'_t S^{(2)})$ and the small-$r'_t$ expansion of the heat kernel, $\mathrm{Tr}\,\exp(-r'_t S^{(2)})\sim(4\pi r'_t)^{-d/2}\int\sqrt g\,(a_0+a_1r'_t+\cdots)$, with $k=d/2$ as the anomalous coefficient.

Interpretation.

  • Reading the anomaly's coefficient as the algebra's multiplicity, with the geometry supplied by the heat kernel, is the framework's interpretation of the standard result; the standard result is not re-derived.
  • Treating the trace anomaly and the $\theta$ vacuum as the two structural quantum effects of this subcategory is the organising interpretation of the anomaly-and-angle pair of this group.

Open.

  • Whether the framework has a natural definition of the conformal coupling $\zeta=\tfrac16$ beyond its transcription is not addressed here; the improvement term is used as in the standard theory.
  • The anomaly of a genuinely non-central biquaternion operator, and whether its eta-invariant part has a framework-specific form, is left to the $\theta$-vacuum article.
  • The scheme-independent coefficients of the biquaternion field are not computed here; only the counting that fixes them is identified.

Summary

The trace anomaly in biquaternionic form is the standard anomaly with the framework's trace and component count. The classical trace vanishes for a Weyl-invariant action, and quantum mechanically the one-loop effective action $\Gamma_1=\tfrac12\mathrm{Tr}\log S^{(2)}[\xi;g]$ has the conformal variation $$ \boxed{\;T_\mu{}_\mu\big|_{\text{one loop}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{|g|}}g_{\mu\nu}\frac{\delta\Gamma_1}{\delta g_{\mu\nu}} = \frac{1}{(4\pi)^{d/2}}\,a_{d/2}\;}, $$ proportional to the Seeley–DeWitt coefficient, which is local and one-loop exact. In two dimensions $T_\mu{}_\mu=\frac{c}{24\pi}R$ with $c$ counting real components; in four dimensions $T_\mu{}_\mu=\frac{1}{(4\pi)^2}(cW^2-aE_4+b\Box R)$ with $a$ and $c$ fixed by the field content. The framework's contributions are the trace of an operator in the algebra's four complex dimensions, four for the identity action, the level-1 norm with $\dim_\mathbb{C}\mathbb{B}=4$, and the component count four complex and eight real; the counting is checked in finite dimension by $\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\log\det(e^sK)\big|_{s=0}=\dim K$. The Weyl transformation rescales the level-1 form and nothing else, and the regularisation of the determinant, the curvature invariants, and the coefficients of $E_4$ and $W^2$ are standard and transcribed.
