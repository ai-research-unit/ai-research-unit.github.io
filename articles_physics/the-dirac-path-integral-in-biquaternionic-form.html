The Dirac Path Integral in Biquaternionic Form

Introduction

The path integral is the third formulation of quantum physics, alongside the operator formulation and the wave-equation formulation. For a relativistic spin-$\tfrac12$ particle it presents a specific difficulty that the scalar case does not: the Dirac operator is first order, and a path integral whose phase is a sum over a Lagrangian is naturally a device for a second-order operator. The history of the subject is the history of two complementary resolutions of that difficulty — Feynman's original "checkerboard", in which the first-order structure is built into a discrete lattice of directed paths, and the proper-time/worldline formulation, in which the first-order operator is applied to a scalar path integral or is generated by an additional Grassmann (spin) degree of freedom on the worldline.

This article develops the biquaternionic interpretation of both. The square of the biquaternionic Dirac operator is the Klein–Gordon operator (the companion article Klein–Gordon from the Dirac Square in Biquaternionic Form derives this), so the Dirac propagator is obtained from the scalar path integral by applying the first-order operator; and the reason the spin is carried by a path integral over an odd variable is that the odd variable generates the Clifford algebra whose even part is the biquaternion algebra $\mathbb{B}$. The two statements are the two halves of one algebraic fact: the algebra is the even part of the Clifford algebra generated by the spin, and the square of the Dirac operator is the scalar operator whose path integral is $\Delta_F$.

Three boundaries are respected. The Klein–Gordon path integral and the scalar propagator belong to the spin-$0$ subcategory and are cited, not re-derived. The spin-half path integral and the fermionic field path integral are different objects: the former is the non-relativistic spinning path integral and the latter is the quantised-field (Grassmann-functional) integral belonging to Biquaternion Quantum Fields; neither is the single-particle Dirac path integral treated here. The general apparatus of the d'Alembertian and its Green's functions belongs to the generalities.

The conventions are the series conventions. The biquaternion algebra $\mathbb{B} = \mathbb{C}\otimes_\mathbb{R}\mathbb{H}$, with $e_t = 1$ and $\vec e_x^2 = \vec e_y^2 = \vec e_z^2 = -e_t$, is the even subalgebra of $\mathrm{Cl}_{1,3}$, and the scalar imaginary is central and obeys $i^2 = -1$, so that every power of $i$ reduces to $\pm1$ or $\pm i$.

The gradient and its quaternion conjugate are $\tilde{\nabla} = e_t\partial/\partial Q_t + \sum_k \vec e_k\partial/\partial Q_k$ and $\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural} = e_t\partial/\partial Q_t - \sum_k \vec e_k\partial/\partial Q_k$, so that

$$ \tilde{\nabla}\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural} = \tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{\nabla} = \Box = \frac{\partial^2}{\partial Q_t^2} + \Delta , $$

where $\Delta = \partial^2/\partial Q_x^2 + \partial^2/\partial Q_y^2 + \partial^2/\partial Q_z^2$ is the quaternionic Laplacian. The dynamics is the chiral pair

$$ \tilde{\nabla}\tilde{\Psi}_R = \mu\tilde{\Psi}_L, \qquad \tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{\Psi}_L = \mu\tilde{\Psi}_R, \qquad \mu = \frac{mc}{\hbar}, $$

with massless limit $\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{\Psi} = 0$; in natural units $\mu = m$, which is the form in which the pair is usually displayed. Its component form is the standard Dirac equation on the spinor module, with the four generator cells of the canonical frame

$$ G_0 = iR_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{axf}}:\psi\mapsto ie_t\psi\vec e_x, \qquad G_k = iL_{\vec e_k}^{\mathrm{axf}}R_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{axf}}:\psi\mapsto i\vec e_k\psi\vec e_y \ (k = x,y,z), $$

where the spatial index runs over $k = x,y,z$ and is written $k = 1,2,3$ wherever a numerical label is needed, and with the pseudoscalar cell

$$ G_5 = -iR_{\vec e_z}^{\mathrm{axf}} = -iL_{e_t}^{\mathrm{axf}}R_{\vec e_z}^{\mathrm{axf}}:\psi\mapsto -ie_t\psi\vec e_z . $$

The frame is a choice: the right factor $\vec e_y$ is common to the three spatial cells, the left factor is the direction of the coordinate, and the time cell has $\vec e_x$ on its right. The four cells carry the Clifford relations of the level-3 (Clifford) metric,

$$ \{G_\mu,G_\nu\} = 2g_{\mu\nu}I_4, \qquad g = \mathrm{diag}(+1,-1,-1,-1), $$

which is the sense in which the component form is the standard Dirac equation. The propagator below is computed on the module and is therefore matrix-valued. The worldline parameter is $\tau$ and the proper time $T$; $x(\tau)$ is the material four-position $\tilde R = ict'\,e_t + x\,\vec e_x + y\,\vec e_y + z\,\vec e_z \in \mathbb{M}_-$.

Two different equations carry the name of Dirac, and this article uses one of them. The first is the one-sided chiral pair displayed above: the gradient acts by left multiplication, the field lies on the two chiral halves of the module, and the mass is the off-diagonal pairing of the two halves. The second is the two-sided form of The Dirac Equation in Biquaternionic Form, a single equation on $\mathbb{B}$ in which the derivative acts from the left and the mass by right multiplication through the fixed direction $\vec e_z$. The two are the same field read through the dictionary of that article, and they are not the same operator; the difference is exactly the difference between a one-sided and a two-sided action. Every statement below is about the one-sided pair, and the path integral is built for it.

The Dirac Propagator and the Square

The object the path integral must compute is the Feynman propagator for the spin-$\tfrac12$ field,

$$ S_F(x-x_2) = \int\frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4}\, \frac{i\left(\slashed p+\mu\right)}{p^2-\mu^2+i\epsilon}\, \exp(-ip\cdot(x-x_2)), $$

with $\slashed p$ the momentum cell of the reference frame, the signed sum of the four generator cells contracted with the four-momentum,

$$ \slashed p = iR_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{axf}}p_0 - \sum_{k=1}^{3}iL_{\vec e_k}^{\mathrm{axf}}R_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{axf}}p_k = G_0p_0 - \sum_{k=1}^{3}G_kp_k , \qquad (\slashed p)^2 = p^2 I_4 , \qquad p^2 = p_0^2-p_1^2-p_2^2-p_3^2 , $$

and with $p\cdot(x-x_2) = p_0(x_0-x_2^0)-\sum_k p_k(x_k-x_{2k})$ the Lorentz contraction. The four $x_\mu = (x_0,x_1,x_2,x_3)=(q'_t,x,y,z)$ are the real coordinates of the material sector, the ones the phase is written in, and $p_\mu$ are real; the algebra's fourth coordinate is $Q_t=iq'_t$, and $\partial/\partial Q_t=-i\,\partial/\partial x_0$. The relative minus between the time and the space terms is a labelling convention and not a sign the square can see: distinct cells anticommute, so the cross terms vanish and $\left(G_0p_0\pm\sum_kG_kp_k\right)^2=p^2$ for either sign. The corpus writes the minus, which is the contraction of the lower cell index with the upper momentum, $\slashed p=G_\mu p^\mu=G_0p^0+\sum_kG_kp^k$.

The fraction is the matrix inverse of $\slashed p-\mu$, and the two readings agree because

$$ \left(\slashed p-\mu\right)\left(\slashed p+\mu\right) = (\slashed p)^2-\mu^2 = \left(p^2-\mu^2\right)I_4 , $$

so that $\frac{i}{\slashed p-\mu}$ is the same object as $\frac{i(\slashed p+\mu)}{p^2-\mu^2}$. The two factors of $i$ do different work and neither is redundant. The $i$ of the numerator is the normalisation of the Green's function, fixed by the right-hand side below. The $i$ inside each cell fixes the sign pattern of the four squares: without it the bare multiplications $R_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{axf}}$ and $L_{\vec e_k}^{\mathrm{axf}}R_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{axf}}$ square to $-I_4$ and $+I_4$ respectively, the pattern $(-,+,+,+)$ of $\eta$, and the central $i^2=-1$ flips every one of them into the pattern $(+,-,-,-)$ of $g$. With that pattern the denominator is the real scalar $p^2-\mu^2$ and not its negative.

The propagator satisfies

$$ \left(i\slashed\partial-\mu\right)S_F(x-x_2) = i\,\delta^{(4)}(x-x_2), $$

where $i\slashed\partial$ is the same contraction with $p_\mu$ replaced by the derivative and the minus sign kept, so that $i\slashed\partial\exp(-ip\cdot(x-x_2))=\slashed p\exp(-ip\cdot(x-x_2))$. Its square is scalar,

$$ \left(i\slashed\partial\right)^2 = -\left(G_0^2\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_0^2}+\sum_{k=1}^{3}G_k^2\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_k^2}\right) = -\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_0^2}+\Delta = \Box , $$

the cross terms vanishing because distinct cells anticommute, and the three signs $G_0^2=I_4$, $G_k^2=-I_4$ and $i^2=-1$ combining into the d'Alembertian of the conventions article. There $\Box=\partial^2/\partial Q_t^2+\Delta$; on the material sector, where $Q_t=iq'_t$ and the derivative is taken with respect to the real $q'_t=x_0$, the same operator reads $\Delta-\partial^2/\partial x_0^2$, which is the display. The square is therefore the series' own scalar operator and not a second one, and that is what makes the first-order operator a square root.

This is standard relativistic quantum physics (see the Further Reading); the companion article The Feynman Propagator in Biquaternionic Form treats the same object in the biquaternion framework. Two structural facts are needed here.

The propagator is the scalar kernel acted on by the first-order operator. Write

$$ \Delta_F(x-x_2) = \int\frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4}\, \frac{i}{p^2 - \mu^2 + i\epsilon}\, \exp(-ip\cdot(x-x_2)), \qquad \left(\Box - \mu^2\right)\Delta_F = i\,\delta^{(4)}, $$

for the scalar (Klein–Gordon) Feynman propagator, the spin-$0$ object of the companion subcategory. It carries the same $i$ as $S_F$, so the two kernels have one and the same Green's-function normalisation and differ by the matrix factor alone. Since $\Box$ acts on the exponential as multiplication by $p^2$, the difference is exactly the matrix prefactor, and formally

$$ \boxed{\;S_F = \left(i\slashed\partial + \mu\right)\Delta_F\;} $$

and the verification is the one-line algebra of the pair,

$$ \left(i\slashed\partial - \mu\right)\left(i\slashed\partial + \mu\right) = \left(i\slashed\partial\right)^2 - \mu^2 = \Box - \mu^2, $$

which is the operator that $\Delta_F$ inverts. The Dirac propagator is therefore the Klein–Gordon propagator with the first-order operator applied; if the scalar kernel is available as a path integral, the Dirac propagator is available from it by a differential operation. This is one of the two standard resolutions of the first-order problem, and it is the one the biquaternion algebra states most directly: the inverse of the first-order operator is the conjugate first-order operator times the inverse of its square, and its square is central.

The propagator is not one path integral with one phase. The worldline proper-time representation makes the same point in a Lorentz-invariant way. Since

$$ \frac{i}{p^2 - \mu^2 + i\epsilon} = \int_0^\infty dT\,\exp\left(iT\left(p^2 - \mu^2 + i\epsilon\right)\right), $$

the scalar propagator is the integral over a proper time $T$ of a kernel $\langle x_2|\exp(iT(\Box - \mu^2))|x\rangle$, and that kernel is a sum over worldlines,

$$ \Delta_F(x,x_2) = \int_0^\infty dT\int_{x(0)=x}^{x(T)=x_2}\mathcal{D}x(\tau)\; \exp(iS_0[x]), \qquad S_0[x] = \int_0^T d\tau\left(\frac{1}{4}\dot x_\mu\dot x_\mu - \mu^2\right), $$

up to the standard normalisation of the measure and the einbein gauge choice. The tangent is contracted with the level-2 form $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(-1,+1,+1,+1)$ — the $ict'$-coordinate metric of the conventions article, read here on the real-time components $(x_0, x\,\vec e_x + y\,\vec e_y + z\,\vec e_z)$ — so that for a straight worldline

$$ \dot x_\mu\dot x_\mu = -\frac{(x-x_2)^2}{T^2}, \qquad (x-x_2)^2 = (x_0-x_2^0)^2 - \left|\vec x-\vec x_2\right|^2 , $$

writing $\vec x = x\,\vec e_x+y\,\vec e_y+z\,\vec e_z$ for the vector part of the position, and the phase reduces to $\exp\left(-i\left[\frac{(x-x_2)^2}{4T} + \mu^2 T\right]\right)$; this is the sign that the explicit free kernel of the next section displays. The Dirac propagator is then $\left(i\slashed\partial + \mu\right)$ applied to $\Delta_F$. The worldline is a curve in the material sector $\mathbb{M}_-$: its tangent $d\tilde{R}/d\tau$ is a material four-velocity, and the phase $S_0$ is central. This is the biquaternion content of the scalar worldline: a central phase over a material curve.

The deficiency of this representation is that the spin is nowhere to be seen until the differential operator is applied at the end. The second resolution supplies the spin directly.

The Free Kernel, Explicitly

It is worth evaluating the scalar worldline kernel in the free case, because the calculation is the whole content of the proper-time method and fixes the sense in which a first-order object is a square root. The proper-time kernel is the Fourier transform

$$ \Delta_F(x,x_2) = \int_0^\infty dT\int\frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4}\, \exp\left(iT\left(p^2 - \mu^2 + i\epsilon\right)\right)\,\exp(-ip\cdot(x-x_2)), \qquad \mu = \frac{mc}{\hbar}, $$

where the $i\epsilon$ prescription makes the $T$-integral converge at large $T$. The $p$-integral is a Gaussian: completing the square in each of the four components gives the standard free kernel

$$ \Delta_F(x,x_2) = \int_0^\infty dT\;\mathcal{N}(T)\; \exp\left(-i\left[\frac{(x-x_2)^2}{4T} + \mu^2 T\right]\right), $$

with $\mathcal{N}(T)$ the standard Gaussian normalisation $\propto T^{-2}$, whose precise constant depends on the measure convention and is not needed below. Three features of this expression carry the physics.

  • The proper time $T$ is the Schwinger parameter, and the exponent is the classical action of a relativistic free particle, $\frac{(x-x_2)^2}{4T}$ being the kinetic term and $\mu^2T$ the mass term; the path integral over $x(\tau)$ is the statement that the exponent is extremised by a straight line, with the fluctuations around it producing the prefactor.
  • The mass is a constant in the exponent. There is no spin and no matrix structure: $\Delta_F$ is a scalar, and it obeys $(\Box - \mu^2)\Delta_F = i\delta^{(4)}$ up to the measure convention.
  • The first-order structure enters only through the differential prefactor. The Dirac propagator is obtained by applying $\left(i\slashed\partial + \mu\right)$ to $\Delta_F$, which is why the spin is invisible until that operation is performed.

The same proper-time integral is the bridge to the worldline of the next section: replacing the constant mass by a general worldline interaction, and adding the odd variables, converts this scalar kernel into the matrix kernel of the Dirac propagator.

The Spinning Worldline and the Spin Factor

The worldline that generates the Dirac propagator itself, rather than the Klein–Gordon kernel it acts on, carries an additional odd (Grassmann) degree of freedom $\psi_\mu(\tau)$ at each point. The standard supersymmetric worldline action is

$$ S[x,\psi] = \int_0^T d\tau\left[ \frac{1}{4}\dot x_\mu\dot x_\mu + \frac{i}{2}\psi_\mu\dot\psi_\mu - \mu^2 \right], $$

with both quadratic terms contracted with the same $\eta$: the tangent and the odd pair are the two halves of one supersymmetric pair, so they share the metric, and that is why the same $\eta$ appears twice. Each half contributes its own kinetic term, the bosonic $\frac{1}{4}\dot x_\mu\dot x_\mu$ and the fermionic $\frac{i}{2}\psi_\mu\dot\psi_\mu$. Quantization of the odd variables gives the Clifford algebra

$$ \{\psi_\mu,\psi_\nu\} = \frac{1}{2}\eta_{\mu\nu}\,I_4 , $$

in the normalisation of the worldline literature — the one in which the spin factor below takes the quoted form — so that the Hilbert space of the odd sector is the spinor representation, of dimension $2^{[4/2]} = 4$ in four dimensions. The metric here is $\eta$ and not $g$, and the difference between the two is one factor of the central $i$. Write $C_0=R_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{axf}}$ and $C_k=L_{\vec e_k}^{\mathrm{axf}}R_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{axf}}$ for the bare multiplications, the cells without their factor of $i$: they square to $(-1,+1,+1,+1)$ and satisfy $\{C_\mu,C_\nu\}=2\eta_{\mu\nu}I_4$, while the cells of the introduction are $G_\mu=iC_\mu$ and satisfy

$$ \{G_\mu,G_\nu\} = i^2\{C_\mu,C_\nu\} = -2\eta_{\mu\nu}I_4 = 2g_{\mu\nu}I_4 . $$

The central $i$ is thus exactly the change of metric that separates the worldline algebra from the Dirac operator, and the odd variable is that cell up to it and up to the normalisation of the kinetic term,

$$ \psi_\mu = \tfrac12 iG_\mu = -\tfrac12 C_\mu , \qquad \{\psi_\mu,\psi_\nu\} = \tfrac14\{iG_\mu,iG_\nu\} = \tfrac12\eta_{\mu\nu}I_4 , $$

the factor $\tfrac12$ being the one that the coefficient $\tfrac{i}{2}$ of $\psi_\mu\dot\psi_\mu$ fixes. Multiplying by $i$ does not change the parity, so the even products of the odd variables span the same even subalgebra $\mathrm{Cl}_{1,3}^{+} = \mathbb{B}$, and nothing in the identification depends on which of $\eta$ and $g$ carries the minus sign.

The path integral over the odd variables, with the boundary condition appropriate to the object computed, is a spin factor $\Phi[x]$ multiplying the scalar measure:

$$ \boxed{\;S_F(x,x_2) = \int_0^\infty dT\int\mathcal{D}x\;\Phi[x]\,\exp(iS_0[x])\;} $$

with the spin factor

$$ \Phi[x] = \int_{\text{b.c.}}\mathcal{D}\psi\; \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_0^T d\tau\;\psi_\mu\dot\psi_\mu\right). $$

The exponent of $\Phi$ carries no $i$, and the reason is the product of two of them: the action contributes $\frac{i}{2}\psi_\mu\dot\psi_\mu$, the weight is the exponential of $i$ times the action, and $i\cdot i = -1$. Written with the two $i$'s kept apart, the spin factor is $\exp\left(i\int_0^T d\tau\,\frac{i}{2}\psi_\mu\dot\psi_\mu\right)$; multiplied out, it is $\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_0^T d\tau\,\psi_\mu\dot\psi_\mu\right)$, which is real. It is bilinear in the odd variables, so its expansion is a sum of products of pairs $\psi_\mu\psi_\nu$ — the scalars $\psi_\mu\psi_\mu$ and the bivectors $\psi_\mu\psi_\nu$ with $\mu\ne\nu$ — which is why the path integral over $\psi$ produces a Clifford-algebra-valued weight.

The complete worldline path integral, with the correct measure, reproduces the Dirac propagator $S_F$; this is the Berezin–Marinov / Brink–Di Vecchia–Howe construction, and it is standard. The same construction in a background electromagnetic field replaces the free kinetic term by the minimally coupled one and produces the spin factor $\Phi[x] = \mathcal{P}\exp\left(-\frac{i}{2}\int \sigma_{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}\,d\tau\right)$ quoted in the literature, with the sign conventions of the cited works; the trace of the same object is the closed-worldline weight and belongs to the next section.

The Biquaternion Interpretation of the Spin Factor

The spin factor is where the algebra sits, and its structure is exactly the algebra's defining relation to the Clifford algebra.

The odd variable generates the Clifford algebra of the cells, and the biquaternion algebra is its even part. The quantized $\psi_\mu$ generate a Clifford algebra; read through the identification $\psi_\mu=-\tfrac12C_\mu$, that is $iG_\mu=2\psi_\mu$, it is $\mathrm{Cl}_{1,3}$. Products of an even number of the $\psi_\mu$ close under multiplication and form the even subalgebra, which is $\mathrm{Cl}_{1,3}^{+}\cong\mathbb{B}$; this is the comparison of Matrix Representations Conventions, §The Clifford Structure and the Frame. A single $\psi_\mu$ is Clifford-odd — it exchanges the two chiral halves and is the generator of the reflection — and it is exactly the kind of object the biquaternion algebra does not contain. The worldline spin variable is therefore the odd object whose even products are the biquaternions.

The spin factor is the Clifford-algebra path-ordered product. The weight $\Phi[x]$ is, order by order, a sum of Clifford elements: the identity, then bivectors, then quadrilinears. Its even part lies in the even subalgebra, its odd part is the Clifford-odd remainder. In the free case the odd part is absorbed by the boundary spinors and the even part is what multiplies the biquaternion-valued field; in a background field the bivector term is the spin–field coupling $\sigma_{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}$ of the standard spin factor. The biquaternion framework thus reads the spin factor as the algebraic object that converts a material curve into a spinor transport: the curve is $\mathbb{M}_-$-valued, and the transport is even-subalgebra-valued with an odd dressing.

The square of the operator is the scalar path integral. The worldline action has a bosonic kinetic term $S_0$ and a fermionic term; integrating out the odd variables returns a factor that is the square root's worth of spin content. The statement that the Dirac propagator is the Dirac operator applied to the scalar kernel, and the statement that the worldline spin variable generates the Clifford algebra, are two faces of the same relation: the odd variable is the spin, and the even products are the algebra; squaring the first-order operator removes the odd variable and returns the scalar operator whose path integral is $\Delta_F$.

The Spin Factor in a Background Field

The spin factor earns its name in a background electromagnetic field, and the calculation shows how the biquaternion algebra's even/odd split organises the result. Minimal coupling adds the gauge term $qA_\mu(x)\dot x_\mu$ to the worldline action,

$$ S[x] = \int_0^T d\tau\left[\frac{1}{4}\dot x_\mu\dot x_\mu - \mu^2 + qA_\mu(x)\dot x_\mu\right], $$

and the odd kinetic term is unchanged. The coupling is through the bivector of the cells,

$$ \sigma_{\mu\nu} = \frac{i}{2}\left[G_\mu,G_\nu\right] = i\,G_\mu G_\nu \quad (\mu\ne\nu), \qquad \mathrm{tr}\,\sigma_{\mu\nu} = 0 , $$

which is traceless and vanishes for $\mu=\nu$, where the commutator does. The path integral over the odd variables produces the path-ordered exponential of that coupling; for the open worldline that computes the propagator it is a matrix on the two boundary spinors,

$$ \boxed{\;\Phi[x] = \mathcal{P}\exp\left(-\frac{i}{2}\int_0^T d\tau\;\sigma_{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}(x(\tau))\right)\;}, $$

and for the worldline closed into a loop it is the traced weight

$$ \Phi_{\mathrm{loop}}[x] = \mathrm{Tr}\,\mathcal{P}\exp\left(-\frac{i}{2}\int_0^T d\tau\;\sigma_{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}(x(\tau))\right). $$

Closing the worldline into a loop turns the matrix element into a trace, and that is the antiperiodic case of the odd integral above: the fermionic variables are antiperiodic around the circle, as every fermionic trace requires.

This is the standard Berezin–Marinov spin factor, and the path ordering is required in both cases because the bivectors at different times do not commute. The same structure is what produces the anomalous magnetic moment in the worldline computation of the electron's form factors, and it is the worldline form of the spin–field coupling that the companion articles The Electron in Biquaternionic Form and The Minimal Coupling of the Biquaternion Dirac Field to Electromagnetism treat from the operator side.

In the biquaternion framework the bivector decomposes into the two pieces of the algebra. The product $G_\mu G_\nu$ is a Clifford bivector; the bivectors split into the timelike ones, which lie in the image of $\mathbb{M}_+$ and are the boost generators, and the spacelike ones, which lie in the image of the real-quaternion sector and are the rotation generators (the decomposition of The Reflection and the Rotation in Biquaternionic Form). The spin factor therefore exponentiates a boost generator and a rotation generator at once. On the closed worldline the trace collapses the Clifford-valued weight to a number: a single bivector is traceless, the pseudoscalar $G_5$ is traceless, and the trace of a product of two bivectors is purely metric, $\mathrm{tr}(\sigma_{\mu\nu}\sigma_{\rho\sigma})=4\left(g_{\mu\rho}g_{\nu\sigma}-g_{\mu\sigma}g_{\nu\rho}\right)$, with no $\epsilon$ term. The loop weight is therefore built from the two invariants of the field strength. The scalar $F_{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}$ appears at second order. The pseudoscalar $\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}F_{\mu\nu}F_{\rho\sigma}$ — the $\vec E\cdot\vec B$ invariant — cannot appear there, because the trace of two bivectors has no room for an $\epsilon$; it enters from the fourth order, and only as its square, the trace being built from the metric alone and so even under parity, while the pseudoscalar is odd. The open weight keeps the two matrix indices and is what dresses the boundary spinors. The path-ordered exponential is the worldline image of the algebra's rotor construction: at each instant the spin is transported by a bivector, and the product of all the transports is the spin factor.

Feynman's Checkerboard

Before the continuum worldline there was a discrete model, and in 1+1 dimensions it exhibits the biquaternion structure with unusual clarity. Feynman's checkerboard (1949) represents the Dirac particle as moving at the speed of light along the two null directions of two-dimensional spacetime, with the amplitude for a path built from two rules: a directed step carries a phase, and each corner (reversal of direction) carries a factor $i\epsilon m/\hbar$, with $\epsilon$ the step length and $m$ the mass; the sign of the corner weight is conjugate to the naming of the two directions, and the recurrence below fixes the one used here. The propagator is the sum over all such paths, and in the continuum limit the sum satisfies the two-dimensional Dirac equation.

The reason is the one on which this whole article turns. In 1+1 dimensions the Dirac spinor has two components, and the two components are exactly the amplitudes for the two directions of motion. Writing the propagator as the four amplitudes $K_{RR}$, $K_{RL}$, $K_{LR}$, $K_{LL}$, where the indices record the initial and final directions, the corner weight builds the mass mixing: the off-diagonal amplitudes require an odd number of corners, so they are the ones proportional to $m$, and the diagonal ones require an even number.

amplitude corners parity in $m$ continuum role
$K_{RR}$, $K_{LL}$ even even free propagation of one chirality
$K_{RL}$, $K_{LR}$ odd odd, $\propto m$ mass insertion between the chiralities

The continuum limit is the two-component Dirac propagator in 1+1 dimensions, a matrix of Bessel functions whose small-$\epsilon$ expansion satisfies the two-dimensional Dirac equation with the corner weight as its mass term; the standard derivations of the checkerboard (see the Further Reading) give the explicit form. The two directions are the two chiral components, and the mass is the corner operator that flips them.

The biquaternion interpretation is then immediate. In 1+1 dimensions the two retained directions generate the even Clifford algebra

$$ \mathrm{Cl}_{1,1}^{+} \cong \mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}, \qquad \mathrm{Cl}_{1,1}^{+} = \operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\left\{e_t,\; L_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{axf}}R_{\vec e_z}^{\mathrm{axf}}\right\}, \qquad \left(L_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{axf}}R_{\vec e_z}^{\mathrm{axf}}\right)^2 = e_t , $$

and the cell $L_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{axf}}R_{\vec e_z}^{\mathrm{axf}} = G_0G_1$ is the two-dimensional chirality. Its two minimal ideals are the two chiral halves; the two directions of the checkerboard are those two halves, and each corner is an insertion of the off-diagonal mass of the chiral pair $\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{\Psi}_R = \mu\tilde{\Psi}_L$, $\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{\Psi}_L = \mu\tilde{\Psi}_R$. The checkerboard is thus the discrete, real-time picture of the mass pair: the free propagation is diagonal in the chirality — each half propagates on its own — and the mass is the reversal that connects them, since $G_0$ and $G_1$ anticommute with $L_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{axf}}R_{\vec e_z}^{\mathrm{axf}}$ and therefore exchange its two eigenspaces. The continuum limit is the statement that the off-diagonal mass pair, iterated on the lattice, reproduces the flat-space Dirac propagator.

The Recurrence and Its Limit

The discrete structure can be written as a pair of coupled recurrences and taken to the continuum, and doing so exhibits the mass pair explicitly. Let $R(x,t')$ and $L(x,t')$ be the amplitudes to arrive at the lattice point $(x,t')$ having last moved in the $+x$ and $-x$ directions respectively, with the lattice spacing and time step both equal to $\epsilon$ (units $c = 1$). A step carries the particle one cell in its direction of motion, and a corner contributes the weight $i\epsilon m/\hbar$; the recurrences are therefore

$$ R(x,t'+\epsilon) = R(x-\epsilon,t') + \frac{i\epsilon m}{\hbar}\,L(x-\epsilon,t'), $$

$$ L(x,t'+\epsilon) = L(x+\epsilon,t') + \frac{i\epsilon m}{\hbar}\,R(x+\epsilon,t'), $$

where the first term on each right-hand side is the straight continuation and the second is the corner. Two features are visible by inspection. The free propagation is diagonal: the term that carries $R$ to $R$ and $L$ to $L$ involves no mixing, and each direction propagates independently. The mass is off-diagonal: the corner terms couple $R$ to $L$ and $L$ to $R$ with the weight $i\epsilon m/\hbar$, i.e. with an explicit factor of the mass and an explicit factor of $i$.

Taylor-expanding and taking $\epsilon \to 0$ with the lightlike constraint $|dx/dt'| = 1$ — the particle moves at the speed of light along the null directions, one lattice cell per time step — turns the pair into

$$ \partial R/\partial t' = -\partial R/\partial x + \frac{im}{\hbar}L, \qquad \partial L/\partial t' = +\partial L/\partial x + \frac{im}{\hbar}R, $$

the two-component Dirac equation in 1+1 dimensions, whose two components are $R$ and $L$. The corner weight $i\epsilon m/\hbar$ becomes the mass term $i\mu$, with $\mu=m/\hbar$ at $c=1$, and the two directions become the two chiral components. Squaring either member with the other substituted gives $\left(\partial^2/\partial t'^2-\partial^2/\partial x^2\right)R = -m^2R/\hbar^2$: the cross terms cancel and the two corner factors meet as $i\cdot i=-1$, which is why the mass enters the squared equation with a minus sign. The $i$ of the corner is a relative phase of the two halves, since the rotation $R\mapsto e^{i\pi/4}R$, $L\mapsto e^{-i\pi/4}L$ turns the mass term $i\mu$ into the real $\mu$; that is why the biquaternion pair can carry the real $\mu$, and why the corner weight is a convention rather than a sign. This is the Klein–Gordon equation of the series' d'Alembertian, so the discrete model carries the Dirac square as well as the Dirac equation. This is the discrete origin of the off-diagonal mass pair that the biquaternion algebra writes as $\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{\Psi}_R = \mu\tilde{\Psi}_L$, $\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{\Psi}_L = \mu\tilde{\Psi}_R$: the checkerboard is that pair before the continuum limit has identified the directions with the chirality eigenspaces. The order of the two factors in a corner is the path ordering that survives as the ordering of the operators $\tilde{\nabla}$ and $\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}$ in the continuum.

Composition, the Propagator Equation, and the Scalar Reduction

The path integral must carry the composition law of a kernel and the Green's-function equation, and both are transparent in the biquaternion framework.

Composition belongs to the proper-time kernel, not to $S_F$. Write $K_T(x,x_2)$ for the kernel of $\exp\left(iT\left(\Box-\mu^2\right)\right)$. Then

$$ \int d^4y\;K_{T_1}(x,y)\,K_{T_2}(y,x_2) = K_{T_1+T_2}(x,x_2), $$

a sum over paths being a sum over intermediate points, and $S_F$ is the transform $\int_0^\infty dT$ of the family. The law stops there: $\int d^4y\,S_F(x,y)S_F(y,x_2)$ is the convolution of two propagators — in momentum space the square of $\frac{i(\slashed p+\mu)}{p^2-\mu^2+i\epsilon}$, not the propagator itself — so $S_F$ is not idempotent and the composition law is not one of its properties. That is why the worldline keeps the proper time explicit instead of integrating it away at the start. In the biquaternion algebra the composition is the multiplication of biquaternion-valued amplitudes in the module, with the central phase composing additively; the algebra's non-commutativity means that the order of the intermediate points matters, and the path-ordered spin factor is the price of that non-commutativity.

The propagator equation. The kernel satisfies $\left(i\slashed\partial - \mu\right)S_F = i\delta^{(4)}$. This is the statement that the propagator is the Green's function of the first-order pair. Applying $i\slashed\partial + \mu$ and using $\left(i\slashed\partial\right)^2 = \Box$ gives

$$ \left(\Box - \mu^2\right)S_F = \left(i\slashed\partial + \mu\right)i\,\delta^{(4)} , $$

so the first-order propagator, acted on by its conjugate, obeys the Klein–Gordon equation, with the spin structure carried entirely by the source. This is the path-integral form of the Dirac square: the first-order propagator, acted on by its conjugate, obeys the second-order equation. The path integral for the first-order object is therefore not an independent construction; applying the conjugate operator to it returns the scalar path integral of the companion subcategory, and the spin factor is what distinguishes them.

It is worth stating what the square does not do in this setting. It does not remove the spin: the squared propagator is matrix-valued and carries the Clifford structure in its free indices, even though its differential operator is central. The spin-0 theory is recovered only after the spin indices are contracted or traced. In the algebra this is the statement that the even part of the spin factor is what survives squaring, while the odd part has been absorbed into the boundary spinors.

The Measure and the Einbein

The worldline integrals above are written with a proper-time parameter $T$ that has already been gauge-fixed, and the fixing is worth stating because it is the reason the four-dimensional propagator carries a proper-time integral at all. A relativistic worldline is reparametrisation invariant: the action

$$ S[x,e] = \int_0^1 d\tau\left[\frac{\dot x_\mu\dot x_\mu}{4e(\tau)} - e(\tau)\,\mu^2\right] $$

depends on the worldline only through its image, the auxiliary field $e(\tau)$ (the einbein) transforming as a density; the parameter $\tau$ has no physical meaning. In the path integral one does not fix $e$ but integrates over it, and the integral over the constant modes of $e$ is precisely the proper-time integral: writing $e(\tau) = T$ for the constant mode, the other modes can be gauged to zero and divide out the volume of the reparametrisation group, and the action becomes

$$ S = \frac{1}{4T}\int_0^1 \dot x_\mu\dot x_\mu\,d\tau - \mu^2 T , $$

which is the proper-time exponent of the previous sections, with $T$ identified as the total proper time. The fluctuations of the material trajectory $x(\tau)$ about the straight line — equivalently, in the Fourier form used above, the four-dimensional Gaussian integral over $p$ — produce the $T^{-2}$ prefactor $\mathcal{N}(T)$ of the free kernel.

The algebraic interpretation is a statement about the two sectors. The einbein is a central scalar: it commutes with the odd variables and carries no spinor index, so its integral only reorganises the material part of the worldline. It is the same for every spin, and it is the reason the spin-0 and spin-$\tfrac12$ theories share the proper-time structure. The spin enters entirely through the odd variables and the spin factor, not through the measure. In the biquaternion framework the einbein is a central element of the worldline's phase, and the spin factor is the Clifford-valued remainder; the split of the worldline integral into a central measure and an algebra-valued weight is the path-integral image of the algebra's centre-versus-module distinction.

The Chiral Pair on the Worldline

The two constructions of the Dirac path integral can be expressed in one biquaternionic statement, and it is worth writing it explicitly because it is the point at which this article meets the rest of the subcategory.

A worldline carries two structures at once: a material trajectory $x_\mu(\tau)$, whose tangent is a four-velocity in $\mathbb{M}_-$, and a spin transport, whose generator is the odd variable $\psi_\mu$. The material trajectory is the object the scalar path integral sums over; the spin transport is the object the spin factor sums over. Squaring the first-order operator pairs them: the square of the Dirac operator is central and acts only on the material trajectory, while the spin indices are carried along by the Clifford structure. The chiral pair

$$ \tilde{\nabla}\tilde{\Psi}_R = \mu\tilde{\Psi}_L, \qquad \tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{\Psi}_L = \mu\tilde{\Psi}_R $$

is the operator statement of the same split: the two gradients act on the two chiral halves, the material trajectory is common to both, and the mass is the off-diagonal corner that turns one into the other. In the worldline language the two chiral halves are the two ways the transport can hand a boundary spinor to the mass term, and the mass term is the reversal; in the checkerboard the same reversal is the lattice corner. The three descriptions — the operator pair, the discretised checkerboard, and the continuous spinning worldline — are the same off-diagonal mass structure in three registers.

There is a further consistency check that ties this article to Klein–Gordon from the Dirac Square in Biquaternionic Form. The scalar kernel $\Delta_F$ is the path integral of the squared theory; the spin factor is what the first-order theory adds. If the spin factor is expanded in the coupling, its leading term is the identity, with no bivector insertion, and the worldline path integral reduces to the scalar $\Delta_F$; this is why the square of the Dirac propagator's equation is the Klein–Gordon equation with a delta source, as computed above. The higher terms of the spin factor are the spin–field couplings, and they have no spin-0 counterpart. The spin-0 path integral of the companion subcategory is therefore not a separate construction with an analogous appearance; it is the trivial-spin-factor case of the worldline integral, and the full Dirac path integral is what the spin degrees of freedom add to it.

What Is Standard and What the Algebra Adds

The Feynman propagator, the proper-time representation, the worldline supersymmetric action, the spin factor and the checkerboard limit are all standard results, transcribed here and cited. The biquaternion interpretation contributes three placements:

  • The spin variable is the odd Clifford element $\psi_\mu$, whose even products close to form $\mathbb{B}$. The biquaternion algebra is not an auxiliary description of spin; it is the even part of the algebra that the spin variable generates.
  • The Dirac propagator is the Dirac operator applied to the scalar propagator, and the reason is the Dirac square: the first-order operator's square is the central Klein–Gordon operator, whose path integral is the spin-0 object of the companion subcategory. The first-order path integral is thus a "square root" of the scalar one, in the same sense as the Dirac equation itself.
  • The checkerboard is the chiral pair on a lattice. Its two directions are the two minimal ideals of the two-dimensional even algebra $\mathrm{Cl}_{1,1}^{+}$, and its corners are the off-diagonal mass insertions; the continuum limit is the biquaternion mass pair.

The worldline and the checkerboard are complementary descriptions of the same object, and the algebra shows why both work: a first-order operator can be realised as a sum over directed paths, the checkerboard, which builds in the chirality, or as a second-order path integral dressed by the Clifford-valued spin factor, the worldline, which builds in the square. The two agree because the odd variable and the mass term are the two faces of the same off-diagonal structure.

The Gamma Formulation

The gammas are a frame of $\mathrm{Cl}_{1,3}(\mathbb{C}) = M_4(\mathbb{C})$, not a different algebra; here $L_aR_b$ is the two-sided multiplication $\psi\mapsto a\psi b$. The five are the generator cells under the gammas' own letters, $\gamma_\mu = G_\mu$ and $\gamma_5 = G_5$, so that the dictionary between the two writings is the identity on the operators:

$$ \gamma_0 = iL_{e_t}^{\mathrm{pau}}R_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{pau}},\quad \gamma_1 = iL_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{pau}}R_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{pau}},\quad \gamma_2 = iL_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{pau}}R_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{pau}},\quad \gamma_3 = iL_{\vec e_z}^{\mathrm{pau}}R_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{pau}},\quad \gamma_5 = -iL_{e_t}^{\mathrm{pau}}R_{\vec e_z}^{\mathrm{pau}} . $$

The same in the axis-free display:

$$ \gamma_0 = iL_{e_t}^{\mathrm{axf}}R_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{axf}},\quad \gamma_1 = iL_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{axf}}R_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{axf}},\quad \gamma_2 = iL_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{axf}}R_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{axf}},\quad \gamma_3 = iL_{\vec e_z}^{\mathrm{axf}}R_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{axf}},\quad \gamma_5 = -iL_{e_t}^{\mathrm{axf}}R_{\vec e_z}^{\mathrm{axf}} . $$

The right factor $\vec e_y$ of the three spatial gammas is the frame's common direction and the left factor is the direction of the coordinate, so the four vectors $L_{e_t}^{\mathrm{pau}}R_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{pau}}$, $L_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{pau}}R_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{pau}}$, $L_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{pau}}R_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{pau}}$, $L_{\vec e_z}^{\mathrm{pau}}R_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{pau}}$ climb a staircase through the two-sided index pairs — a row in neither factor, and not a diagonal. That staircase is the choice of frame, not a feature of the gammas: the repetition of $\vec e_y$ is in the frame. A single product is a pure tensor; the products form the seven-dimensional cone $\operatorname{rank}c(M)\le 1$, and a general element is a sum of at most four, with $\ell(M) = \operatorname{rank}c(M)$.

$\gamma_5$ is the reference-frame cell of the chirality $\Gamma$; the central grading $\chi = i\tilde\Xi_{\mathrm{ad}}$ — the difference of the two central idempotents of the complexified algebra, so $\chi^2 = e_t$ — is a different object, represented by $\gamma_5$ on the Dirac module.

The dictionary is complex-linear; over $\mathbb{R}$ the products span $\mathrm{Cl}_{1,3}(\mathbb{R})\cong M_2(\mathbb{H})$ and the cells span $\mathrm{Cl}_{3,1}(\mathbb{R})\cong M_4(\mathbb{R})$.

The identities the bodies cite. In the $\gamma$ reading, with $\epsilon_{0123} = +1$,

$$ \{\gamma_\mu,\gamma_\nu\} = 2g_{\mu\nu}I_4,\qquad (\gamma_k)^2 = -I_4,\qquad \gamma_5^2 = I_4,\qquad \{\gamma_\mu,\gamma_5\} = 0, $$

$$ [\gamma_\mu,\gamma_\nu] = 2\gamma_\mu\gamma_\nu\quad(\mu\ne\nu),\qquad [\gamma_a,S_{cd}] = g_{ac}\gamma_d - g_{ad}\gamma_c,\qquad S_{cd} = \tfrac14[\gamma_c,\gamma_d], $$

$$ \gamma_0\gamma_1\gamma_2\gamma_3 = -i\,\gamma_5 = -R_{\vec e_z}^{\mathrm{pau}},\qquad \mathrm{tr}(\gamma_\mu\gamma_\nu) = 4g_{\mu\nu},\qquad \mathrm{tr}(\gamma_5\gamma_\mu\gamma_\nu\gamma_\rho\gamma_\sigma) = -4i\,\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}, $$

In the axis-free display:

$$ \gamma_0\gamma_1\gamma_2\gamma_3 = -i\,\gamma_5 = -R_{\vec e_z}^{\mathrm{axf}},\qquad \mathrm{tr}(\gamma_\mu\gamma_\nu) = 4g_{\mu\nu},\qquad \mathrm{tr}(\gamma_5\gamma_\mu\gamma_\nu\gamma_\rho\gamma_\sigma) = -4i\,\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}, $$

$$ \mathrm{tr}(\gamma_\mu\gamma_\nu\gamma_\rho\gamma_\sigma) = 4\left(g_{\mu\nu}g_{\rho\sigma} - g_{\mu\rho}g_{\nu\sigma} + g_{\mu\sigma}g_{\nu\rho}\right). $$

The four contraction sums are the sums in which one gamma is contracted against a fixed string of $n$ gammas,

$$ \sum_\mu\gamma^\mu\gamma_\mu,\qquad \sum_\mu\gamma^\mu\gamma_\alpha\gamma_\mu,\qquad \sum_\mu\gamma^\mu\gamma_\alpha\gamma_\beta\gamma_\mu,\qquad \sum_\mu\gamma^\mu\gamma_\alpha\gamma_\beta\gamma_\gamma\gamma_\mu , $$

and one rule produces all four: for a string of distinct indices,

$$ \sum_\mu\gamma^\mu\gamma_{\alpha_1}\cdots\gamma_{\alpha_n}\gamma_\mu = (-1)^n(4-2n)\,\gamma_{\alpha_1}\cdots\gamma_{\alpha_n}, $$

so that the four values are $4I_4$, $-2\gamma_\alpha$, $0$ and $+2\gamma_\alpha\gamma_\beta\gamma_\gamma$. The two-gamma entry is the one that vanishes, and it is where the dimension enters: the two contracted gammas are moved past the $n$ fixed ones, which gives the $(-1)^n$, and what is left is the contraction of the string with itself, which counts $4-2n$. In the Euclidean frame, where every cell squares to $+I_4$ and no index is raised, the four are $4I_4$, $-2\gamma_\alpha$, $4\delta_{\alpha\beta}I_4$ and $-2\gamma_\gamma\gamma_\beta\gamma_\alpha$: the two-gamma entry counts the degenerate string $\gamma_\alpha\gamma_\alpha=+I_4$, which is a square rather than a bivector, and so is $4I_4$ on the diagonal and $0$ off it, while the last is the rule with the three indices reversed. In the $\mathbb{M}_-$ frame the rule changes when the contracted index is lowered, because then the square of a generator carries the metric: $\sum_\mu\gamma_\mu\gamma_\mu=-2I_4$ and $\sum_\mu\gamma_\mu\gamma_\alpha\gamma_\mu=2(1+g_{\alpha\alpha})\gamma_\alpha$, which is $4\gamma_0$ for the time direction and vanishes for the three spatial ones. The one-gamma sum, unlike the two-gamma sum, is therefore not a statement about four dimensions alone but about the signature as well.

The display. Every array printed in this section is given twice: in the Peirce display first, labelled $\mathrm{pau}$, whose arrays are the ones the literature prints, and in the axis-free display after it, labelled $\mathrm{axf}$, which is the display of the body and of the corpus. The two are carried into one another by $L_{e_\mu}^{\mathrm{axf}}=C_{\Pi}L_{e_\mu}^{\mathrm{pau}}C_{\Pi}^{-1}$, and a conjugation by $C_{\Pi}$ changes no product, no anticommutator, no trace and no determinant, so every identity of the section holds in both. An array is a statement about one display; a cell named in the running text keeps the Peirce label, and its axis-free array is the conjugate. The label names the array and not the operator.

Summary

The Dirac path integral is the sum over worldlines that computes the propagator $S_F$, the matrix-valued Green's function satisfying $\left(i\slashed\partial - \mu\right)S_F = i\delta^{(4)}$ with $\mu = mc/\hbar$. Because the Dirac operator is first order, the path integral is organised in one of two standard ways. In the proper-time way, the scalar Klein–Gordon kernel is written as a worldline sum over material curves in $\mathbb{M}_-$ with central phase $S_0$, and the Dirac propagator is obtained by applying the conjugate first-order operator: $S_F = \left(i\slashed\partial + \mu\right)\Delta_F$, the identity following from $\left(i\slashed\partial\right)^2 = \Box$. In the spinning-worldline way, an odd Grassmann variable $\psi_\mu$ is added at each point with kinetic term $\frac{i}{2}\psi_\mu\dot\psi_\mu$; quantisation gives $\{\psi_\mu,\psi_\nu\} = \frac12\eta_{\mu\nu}I_4$, whose products generate the Clifford algebra; the path integral over $\psi$ is the Clifford-valued spin factor $\Phi[x]$; and the full worldline integral reproduces the Dirac propagator (Berezin–Marinov, Brink–Di Vecchia–Howe).

In the biquaternion framework, the odd variables generate the Clifford algebra and the biquaternion algebra is their even part; the spin factor is the path-ordered Clifford product, even part in the even subalgebra, odd part a dressing of the boundary spinors; and multiplying the first-order operator by its conjugate returns the scalar Klein–Gordon operator — in momentum space

$$ \left(\slashed p - \mu\right)\left(\slashed p + \mu\right) = (\slashed p)^2 - \mu^2 = \left(p^2 - \mu^2\right)I_4 , $$

the identity of the notation table below; the scalar path integral of the companion subcategory is the kernel that the first-order factor acts on, a consequence of this identity rather than an independent construction. Feynman's checkerboard is the discrete realisation of the same structure in 1+1 dimensions: the two directions are the two chiral components, the corners carry the weight $i\epsilon m/\hbar$ and are the off-diagonal mass insertions, and the continuum limit is the two-dimensional Dirac propagator.
