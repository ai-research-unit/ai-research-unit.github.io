The Field-Strength Biquaternion and Its Invariants

Introduction

The companion article Maxwell's Equations in the Biquaternionic Formulation introduced the field-strength biquaternion

$$ \tilde{F} = \vec F = i\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\vec E - \sqrt{\mu}\,\vec H $$

as the single biquaternionic object that carries the electromagnetic field, and showed that, in a medium with permittivity $\epsilon$ and permeability $\mu$, the four Maxwell equations collapse into a single equation $\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{F} = -\sqrt{\mu}\,\tilde{J}$. In that article $\tilde{F}$ appears as a means to an end: the compact rewriting of the field equations. This article studies $\tilde{F}$ for its own sake.

Two features make the field strength worth treating separately. First, $\tilde{F}$ is not a four-vector. Unlike the four-potential $\tilde{A}$, whose scalar part is imaginary and whose vector part is real, the field-strength biquaternion has vanishing scalar part and a mixed real/imaginary vector part: its imaginary half carries the electric field and its real half the magnetic field. The field strength is therefore a different kind of object from the kinematic four-vectors that live in the material subspace $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$, and its Lorentz transformation law is correspondingly different. Second, the biquaternion norm (the quaternionic norm of the conventions) evaluated on $\tilde{F}$ is a complex scalar whose real and imaginary parts are the two classical Lorentz invariants, $\vec E^2 - c^2\vec B^2$ and $\vec E\cdot\vec B$, up to the medium factors $-\epsilon$ and $-2\epsilon c$. The algebraic apparatus built to describe the Minkowski metric thus delivers the electromagnetic invariants as well.

This article is the declared foundation for three later articles on electromagnetism in media, on the Lorentz force, and on radiation from accelerated charges. It therefore fixes the field-strength notation once and for all. The canonical objects are: the field-strength biquaternion $\tilde{F} = i\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\vec E - \sqrt{\mu}\,\vec H$; the electric and magnetic fields $\vec E$ and $\vec H$, together with the magnetic induction $\vec B = \mu\vec H$; the medium speed of light $c = 1/\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}$; the Riemann–Silberstein vector $\vec N = \vec E + ic\vec B$; the two invariants $I_1 = \vec E^2 - c^2\vec B^2$ and $I_2 = \vec E\cdot\vec B$; and the energy density $W$ and Poynting vector $\vec S$. Nothing in this list is new notation; the first three come unchanged from the Maxwell article, and the rest are assembled from them.

The conventions are those of the read-list articles throughout: the algebra, the units, the conjugations and the product form are those of Algebra and Element Conventions, Physics Units and Common Elements Conventions and Product and Form Conventions; the gradient, the d'Alembertian and the derivative marks are those of Operator and Calculus Conventions; and none of them is restated here. The three-dimensional complex vector part of $\mathbb{B}$ is denoted by its components $\vec F=\sum_k F_k \vec e_k$. The symbol $c$ always denotes the speed of light in the medium, $c=1/\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}$; in vacuum it reduces to $c_0=1/\sqrt{\epsilon_0\mu_0}$.

The Field-Strength Biquaternion

The definition is the one of the Maxwell article, repeated here because everything that follows depends on it:

$$ \tilde{F} = \vec F = i\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\vec E - \sqrt{\mu}\,\vec H = \sum_{k=1}^{3} F_k\, \vec e_k, \qquad F_k = i\sqrt{\epsilon}\,E_k - \sqrt{\mu}\,H_k . $$

Its scalar part vanishes identically,

$$ \mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{F}) = 0, $$

so $\tilde{F}$ is a pure-vector biquaternion. Its three complex components $F_k$ each combine an electric and a magnetic piece: the electric contribution $i\sqrt{\epsilon}\,E_k$ is purely imaginary and the magnetic contribution $-\sqrt{\mu}\,H_k$ is real. The factor of $i$ on the electric part is the same factor that appears in the complex time coordinate $i c r'_t$. In the $i c r'_t$ convention the time direction is the imaginary direction, and the electric field carries the time index of the field tensor, so it is the electric part that acquires the factor $i$ while the magnetic part remains real. This is the algebraic reason for the asymmetric appearance of $\tilde{F}$.

The normalization factors $\sqrt{\epsilon}$ and $\sqrt{\mu}$ are the natural ones for a medium. Each term has the dimension of the square root of an energy density, since $\epsilon E^2$ and $\mu H^2$ are both energy densities; consequently $\tilde{F}$ has dimension $\sqrt{\text{energy density}}$, and the biquaternion norm calculated below has the dimension of an energy density. With the constitutive relation $\vec B = \mu\vec H$ the magnetic term may also be written $\sqrt{\mu}\,\vec H = \vec B/\sqrt{\mu}$, so the field strength can equally be regarded as the pair $(\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\vec E, \vec B/\sqrt{\mu})$.

The field strength is not an independent object: it is obtained from the potential biquaternion $\tilde{A} = i\,a'_t + \vec A$ by differentiation with the gradient $\tilde{\nabla}$ of Operator and Calculus Conventions, §The Gradient, whose conjugate is $\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}$ and whose d'Alembertian is $\Box=\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}=\partial^2/\partial R_t^2+\Delta_Q$. The construction is

$$ \tilde{F} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\Bigl(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{A} - \mathrm{Sc}\!\left(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{A}\right)\Bigr). $$

This is the vector part of $\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{A}$ divided by $\sqrt\mu$. The gradient stands to the left of the potential. The product is the algebra product, and no operator beyond the gradient and its conjugate enters. In Heaviside–Lorentz units, where the two medium parameters are $1$, this is the familiar $\tilde{F}=\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{A}-\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{A})$ of the companion articles. In a medium with $\sqrt\mu\ne1$ the division is not optional, because $\tilde{F}$ and $\mathrm{Vect}(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{A})$ then differ in dimension.

In tensor language the same object is the antisymmetric rank-two tensor

$$ F_{\mu\nu} = \frac{\partial A_\nu}{\partial R_\mu} - \frac{\partial A_\mu}{\partial R_\nu}, \qquad F_{0k} = \frac{iE_k}{c}, \qquad F_{jk} = \epsilon_{jkl}B_l . $$

It has six independent components — three electric and three magnetic — which is exactly the information carried by the three complex components $F_k$ of the pure-vector biquaternion. The six components are the six planes of the next section, each a product of two units: the three $F_{0k}$ sit on the planes that contain the time direction and the three $F_{jk}$ on the purely spatial planes. The identification is exact, with one constant: $\mathrm{Vect}(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{A})=\sqrt{\mu}\,\tilde{F}$ identically. Indeed, $\partial/\partial R_t=-(i/c)\partial/\partial r'_t$ and $A_0=+i\,a'_t$ give $\mathrm{Vect}(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{A})=(i/c)\vec E-\vec B$, and $\sqrt{\mu}/c=\mu\sqrt{\epsilon}$. The component formulas above and the combination $F_k = i\sqrt{\epsilon}E_k - \sqrt{\mu}H_k$ are then the same statement read component by component.

The Place of the Field Strength in the Algebra

The biquaternion algebra decomposes in several ways, and it is worth locating $\tilde{F}$ precisely in these decompositions, because its peculiar properties are consequences of its position.

The vector part. The pure-vector biquaternions form the complex three-dimensional subspace

$$ \mathbb{B}_{V} = \mathbb{C}\vec e_x \oplus \mathbb{C}\vec e_y \oplus \mathbb{C}\vec e_z, $$

of real dimension six. It is not one of the fixed-point subspaces of the conjugations, $\mathbb{B}_{C}$, $\mathbb{B}_{H}$, $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ and $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$: each of those contains the scalar line, whereas the vector subspace contains no scalar at all. The field strength lies entirely in this subspace, and the classical statement that the electromagnetic field has six independent components is exactly the statement that $\tilde{F}$ is a general element of $\mathbb{B}_{V}$.

The Hermitian decomposition. The vector part intersects the two complementary subspaces $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ and $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ in the three-dimensional real subspaces

$$ \mathbb{B}_{V} \cap \mathbb{B}_{\Psi} = \mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{i\vec e_x, i\vec e_y, i\vec e_z\}, \qquad \mathbb{B}_{V} \cap \mathbb{B}_{\Phi} = \mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{\vec e_x, \vec e_y, \vec e_z\}. $$

The first consists of the pure imaginary vectors and the second of the pure real vectors. The two pieces of $\tilde{F}$ fall into these two intersections:

$$ \tilde{F} = \underbrace{i\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\vec E}_{\in\,\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}} \;+\; \underbrace{\left(-\sqrt{\mu}\,\vec H\right)}_{\in\,\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}}. $$

The electric part is therefore Hermitian and the magnetic part anti-Hermitian, and $\tilde{F}$ is neither. Explicitly,

$$ \tilde{F}^{*} = -\overline{\vec F} = i\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\vec E + \sqrt{\mu}\,\vec H, $$

so that the Hermitian and anti-Hermitian parts of the field strength are

$$ \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\tilde{F} + \tilde{F}^{*}\right) = i\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\vec E, \qquad \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\tilde{F} - \tilde{F}^{*}\right) = -\sqrt{\mu}\,\vec H. $$

The split holds in the frame in which the decomposition is taken and is not Lorentz invariant: a boost mixes the two sectors. The same display shows why $\tilde{F}$ cannot lie in $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$: the material subspace contains the real vectors but not the imaginary ones, and the electric half of the field strength uses the imaginary vector directions.

The real/complex decomposition. Equivalently, with respect to the complex conjugation $\bar{\cdot}$ that fixes $\mathbb{B}_{H}$,

$$ \tilde{F} = \underbrace{\left(-\sqrt{\mu}\,\vec H\right)}_{\in\,\mathbb{B}_{H}} \;+\; \underbrace{i\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\vec E}_{\in\,\mathbb{B}_{iH}}. $$

The scalar subspace $\mathbb{B}_{C}$ receives no contribution, which is again $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{F}) = 0$. The decomposition used is $\mathbb{B} = \mathbb{B}_{H} \oplus \mathbb{B}_{iH}$. Thus the field strength uses the vector parts of $\mathbb{B}_{H}$ and $\mathbb{B}_{iH}$, and the electric and magnetic halves of $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ and $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$, and it touches $\mathbb{B}_{C}$ not at all.

The biparavector interpretation: the field as two families of planes. The two halves just separated are not merely subspaces; each is a family of planes. The separation of the field into an electric and a magnetic part is the separation of the biparavector into a timelike and a spacelike part. In the paravector presentation of the algebra (Paravectors and the Geometry of Spacetime), a biparavector is the product of two orthogonal paravectors and represents a plane in spacetime. With the paravector directions $i\vec e_k$ there are six independent planes,

$$ \{e_t\,i\vec e_k\}_{k=x,y,z}=\{i\vec e_x,i\vec e_y,i\vec e_z\}, \qquad \{(i\vec e_j)(i\vec e_k)\}_{(j,k)=(r_y,r_x),(r_z,r_y),(r_x,r_z)}=\{\vec e_z,\vec e_x,\vec e_y\}, $$

using $(i\vec e_j)(i\vec e_k)=-\vec e_j\vec e_k$ and $i^2=-1$. The first three, the planes containing the time direction, have the form $e_t\,i\vec e_k = i\vec e_k$ and are the pure imaginary vectors, that is the real span of $\{i\vec e_k\} = \mathbb{B}_{V}\cap\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$. The last three, the purely spatial planes, have the form $(i\vec e_j)(i\vec e_k)$ and are the pure real vectors, that is the real span of $\{\vec e_k\} = \mathbb{B}_{V}\cap\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$.

Comparing with the decomposition above, the electric and magnetic halves of the field are exactly the two families of planes:

$$ \tilde{F} \;=\; \underbrace{i\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\vec E}_{\text{timelike planes}\;\in\,\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}} \;-\; \underbrace{\sqrt{\mu}\,\vec H}_{\text{spacelike planes}\;\in\,\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}}. $$

The electric part is a real-linear combination of the three planes that contain the time direction, and the magnetic part a real-linear combination of the three that do not. This is the plane-geometric form of the algebra asymmetry: the electric part lies in the timelike-plane family and the magnetic part in the spacelike-plane family, the two families that generate the Lorentz boosts and the rotations respectively (Symmetric and Antisymmetric Decomposition of the Four General Products). The six components of the field are the six planes, and a general field is a real-linear combination of all six.

Two familiar facts are read off from the plane type. The quaternion conjugate of the field reverses the electric planes and fixes the magnetic ones,

$$ \tilde{F}^{\natural} = -i\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\vec E + \sqrt{\mu}\,\vec H, $$

because the conjugate negates the vector part of each coefficient and the electric part is the imaginary vector while the magnetic part is the real vector. Read on the fields this is $\vec E\to-\vec E$ and $\vec H\to\vec H$: the conjugate reverses the electric field and keeps the magnetic one. The duality rotation of the later section, $\tilde{F}\mapsto \exp(-i\theta_{\mathrm{pha}})\tilde{F}$, turns the electric planes into the magnetic ones and back. At $\theta_{\mathrm{pha}} = \pi/2$ the factor is $-i$, which carries $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ to $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ and back, so the two families are exchanged: with unit impedance the exchange is the exact swap $\vec E\mapsto\vec H$, $\vec H\mapsto-\vec E$, and in a general medium the impedance factor $Z=\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$ enters. This is why the duality rotation is a symmetry of the source-free vacuum equations and not of the equations with electric sources: it moves the field along a circle in the $(I_1,\,2cI_2)$-plane, at constant $I_1^2+4c^2I_2^2$, without preserving the planes separately.

The Biquaternion Norm and the Two Invariants

The biquaternion norm on $\mathbb{B}$ is the quaternionic norm of Product and Form Conventions, §The Form Convention,

$$ \langle\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural} = \langle\tilde{Q},\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural} = \mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{Q}\,\tilde{Q}^{\natural}\bigr) = Q_t^2+Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2 . $$

The central element $\tilde{Q}\tilde{Q}^{\natural}$ equals $\langle\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural} e_t$. For a pure-vector biquaternion the quaternion conjugate is $\tilde{F}^{\natural} = -\vec F$, and the quaternion product of two pure vectors is $\vec F\vec G = -\vec F\cdot\vec G + \vec F\times\vec G$. Hence

$$ \tilde{F}\,\tilde{F}^{\natural} = \langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural} e_t, \qquad \langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural} = \vec F\cdot\vec F = \sum_{k=1}^{3} F_k^2, $$

because $\vec F\times\vec F = 0$, which removes the cross term. One is left with the quaternionic norm, a complex scalar. Substituting $F_k = i\sqrt{\epsilon}E_k - \sqrt{\mu}H_k$ and expanding, with $(i\sqrt{\epsilon})^2=-\epsilon$ from $i^2=-1$,

$$ \boxed{\;\langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural} = \sum_{k=1}^{3}\left(i\sqrt{\epsilon}E_k - \sqrt{\mu}H_k\right)^2 = -\epsilon\,\vec E^2 + \mu\,\vec H^2 - 2i\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}\,\vec E\cdot\vec H\;}. $$

This is the central computation of the article. Written with the magnetic induction $\vec B = \mu\vec H$ and the medium speed of light $c = 1/\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}$, the real and imaginary parts become

$$ \mathrm{Re}\,\langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural} = -\epsilon\left(\vec E^2 - c^2\vec B^2\right), \qquad \mathrm{Im}\,\langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural} = -2\epsilon c\,\vec E\cdot\vec B. $$

The two real quantities appearing here are the two classical Lorentz invariants of the electromagnetic field, up to the factors $-\epsilon$ and $-2\epsilon c$:

$$ \boxed{\;I_1 = \vec E^2 - c^2\vec B^2\;}, \qquad \boxed{\;I_2 = \vec E\cdot\vec B\;} $$

so that

$$ \langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural} = -\epsilon\left[\,I_1 + 2ic\,I_2\,\right], \qquad I_1 = -\frac{1}{\epsilon}\,\mathrm{Re}\,\langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural}, \qquad I_2 = -\frac{1}{2\epsilon c}\,\mathrm{Im}\,\langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural}. $$

The biquaternion norm is a single complex number, and it carries exactly two real invariants. It is the fully contracted object built from the field strength with no derivatives and no extra vectors, so these are the only two independent invariants of the field; any other algebraic invariant is a function of $I_1$ and $I_2$.

The conjugate biquaternion norm. The quaternion norm is not the only natural quadratic object here. On a pure vector $\tilde{F}^{\natural} = -\vec F$, and the Hermitian conjugate also conjugates the coefficients, $\tilde{F}^* = -\overline{\vec F}$; that global minus is the one every real vector carries under ${}^{*}$. For the biquaternion norm of the complex-conjugate field,

$$ \langle\tilde{F}^*\rangle_{\natural} = \langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural}^*, $$

so the biquaternion norm and its conjugate carry the same two real invariants. Equivalently, for a pure vector the two products coincide, $\tilde{F}^{\natural}\tilde{F} = \tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{\natural} = \langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural} e_t$. The two invariants are therefore the two real components of the complex biquaternion norm.

Vanishing of the biquaternion norm. Because $\tilde{F}$ is a pure vector,

$$ \tilde{F}^2 = -\vec F\cdot\vec F = -\langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural} e_t, $$

so the biquaternion norm vanishes if and only if $\tilde{F}$ squares to zero. A nonzero element of $\mathbb{B}$ whose biquaternion norm vanishes is a zero divisor, and $\tilde{F}$ is nilpotent in that case. Thus

$$ \langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural} = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad I_1 = 0 \ \text{ and }\ I_2 = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \tilde{F} \text{ is a zero divisor}. $$

The vanishing of the biquaternion norm is therefore the algebraic statement that the field is a null (radiative) field: $\vec E\perp\vec B$ and $|\vec E| = c|\vec B|$ pointwise. This is the same zero-divisor cone that underlies the light cone of $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$, now realized inside the field-strength space. It is the algebraic seed of the radiation theory and will reappear in the later article on radiation from accelerated charges.

The Polar Reading of the Field

The electric half of the field strength lies in $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ and its magnetic half in $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$, and that mixed case is exactly the one in which the Cartan pairing carries information. The pairing gives a reading of the field that the invariants alone do not.

Write the general polar decomposition of the field as

$$ \tilde F=\tilde F_H\tilde F_0,\qquad \tilde F_H=\sqrt{\tilde F\tilde F^{*}}\ge0,\qquad \tilde F_0=\tilde F_H^{+}\tilde F , $$

where ${}^{+}$ is the Moore–Penrose inverse, which is $\tilde F_H^{-1}$ where $\tilde F$ is invertible. The amplitude is Hermitian positive semidefinite and the phase is a partial isometry. Off the null cone the amplitude is positive definite and the phase is $*$-unitary, and the pair is the Cartan pairing; on the cone the same formulas give the generalized pair.

The amplitude carries the intensity and the invariant modulus. The trace of its square is four times the energy density, $\operatorname{Tr}(\tilde F_H^{2})=2\langle\tilde F,\tilde F\rangle_{*}=4W$ (with $\operatorname{Tr}=2\operatorname{Sc}$), and its biquaternion norm is the modulus of the complex invariant,

$$ \langle\tilde F_H\rangle_{\natural}=\lvert\langle\tilde F\rangle_{\natural}\rvert =\epsilon\sqrt{I_1^{2}+4c^{2}I_2^{2}}\ \ge 0 . $$

The norm of the amplitude is real and non-negative even though the norm of the field is complex. $\tilde F_H$ is a Hermitian element and carries two real weights; they are fixed by $W$ and by $\lvert\langle\tilde F\rangle_{\natural}\rvert$: the energy density gives their sum of squares and the invariant modulus gives their product. The amplitude is therefore the part of the field that the invariants determine, and the phase is the part they leave free.

The phase is the duality angle and the polarization. The $*$-unitary factor is $\tilde F_0=\exp(i\alpha)\tilde u_{\mathrm r}$, whose central phase is the duality angle, $\alpha=\tfrac12\arg\langle\tilde F\rangle_{\natural}$ up to the branch, and whose unit factor $\tilde u_{\mathrm r}$ carries the polarization state of the field. A duality rotation $\tilde F\mapsto\exp(-i\theta)\tilde F$ leaves $\tilde F_H$ unchanged and multiplies $\tilde F_0$ by $\exp(-i\theta)$, so it shifts $\alpha$ by $-\theta$; it is a motion along the phase fibre, not a change of amplitude. This is the polar form of the statement that $I_1$ and $I_2$ rotate into one another while $I_1^{2}+4c^{2}I_2^{2}$ is fixed.

The null field is a pure state. On the cone, $I_1=I_2=0$, the amplitude is a scaled idempotent and the phase is a partial isometry, so the state $\tilde F\tilde F^{*}/\bigl(2\langle\tilde F,\tilde F\rangle_{*}\bigr)$ is pure. The radiative field is the zero divisor of the field space, and the operator-to-state map sends it to the boundary of the Bloch ball. This is the same cone that the massless spectrum article reads as the lightlike limit.

The boundary. The split is canonical in the algebra, and it is not a Lorentz-covariant factorisation: the field is a bivector in the tensor reading, its Lorentz action is not the four-vector congruence, and it does not act on $\tilde F_H$ and $\tilde F_0$ separately by one and the same operation. The two invariants, and the modulus $\lvert\langle\tilde F\rangle_{\natural}\rvert$ that is their combination, are the only invariant data of the split. The polar split is also not the self-dual/anti-self-dual split: the latter uses the Hodge dual and separates the two helicities, while the Cartan phase is the compact $*$-unitary quotient. The two decompositions answer different questions and must not be identified.

Lorentz Invariance of the Invariants

The claim that $I_1$ and $I_2$ are Lorentz invariants is standard and can be checked directly. Under a boost with velocity $\vec v$, speed $v=\lvert\vec v\rvert$, and Lorentz factor

$$ \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}, $$

the fields transform as

$$ \begin{aligned} \vec E_2 &= \gamma\left(\vec E + \vec v\times\vec B\right) - \frac{\gamma-1}{v^2}\left(\vec v\cdot\vec E\right)\vec v,\\ \vec B_2 &= \gamma\left(\vec B - \frac{1}{c^2}\,\vec v\times\vec E\right) - \frac{\gamma-1}{v^2}\left(\vec v\cdot\vec B\right)\vec v. \end{aligned} $$

The factor $\gamma$ here is the Lorentz factor, a number and not an element of the algebra. The components parallel to the boost are unchanged, while the transverse components acquire a contribution from the other field. Substituting these expressions into $I_1$ and $I_2$ and using $1-v^2/c^2=1/\gamma^2$, the cross terms cancel and the factors combine, giving

$$ \vec E_2^2 - c^2\vec B_2^2 = \vec E^2 - c^2\vec B^2, \qquad \vec E_2\cdot\vec B_2 = \vec E\cdot\vec B. $$

Both invariants are therefore unchanged. This is consistent with the rotor-conjugation transformation of the four-potential established in the Maxwell article. The field strength is built from $\tilde{A}$ by differentiation. Differentiating a quantity that transforms by rotor conjugation produces a field that transforms in the antisymmetric-tensor representation, whose two independent scalar contractions are exactly $I_1$ and $I_2$.

Scope of the invariance. A material medium selects a rest frame, and a boost changes the medium. The transformation above is the formal Lorentz transformation of the field tensor with $c$ the medium speed. In vacuum it is the physical Lorentz group and the invariance is exact. In a homogeneous medium it is the covariance of the field tensor at fixed $\epsilon$ and $\mu$, and the phrase Lorentz invariant is used in that formal sense. The classification below has the same scope.

There is a discrete subtlety worth recording. Under the proper orthochronous Lorentz group both $I_1$ and $I_2$ are invariant. Under the spatial reflection $\vec q\mapsto-\vec q$, however, the electric field reverses and the magnetic induction does not, so $\vec E\to-\vec E$ and $\vec B\to\vec B$, and

$$ I_1 \to I_1, \qquad I_2 \to -I_2 . $$

The quantity $I_1$ is an ordinary scalar and $I_2$ is a pseudoscalar. Both are invariants of the proper orthochronous group; of the two, only $I_1$ is also unchanged by the reflection.

The Riemann–Silberstein Vector

The two real fields can be combined into one complex three-vector, and this combination is the form in which the invariants look simplest. Define the Riemann–Silberstein vector

$$ \vec N = \vec E + ic\,\vec B. $$

The field-strength biquaternion is an overall constant multiple of it. Indeed, using $\vec B = \mu\vec H$ and $c = 1/\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}$,

$$ i\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\vec N = i\sqrt{\epsilon}\left(\vec E + ic\vec B\right) = i\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\vec E - \sqrt{\epsilon}\,c\,\vec B = i\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\vec E - \sqrt{\mu}\,\vec H = \tilde{F}, $$

because $\sqrt{\epsilon}\,c\,\mu = \sqrt{\mu}$. Thus

$$ \tilde{F} = i\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\vec N. $$

This is the sense in which the Riemann–Silberstein vector and the field-strength biquaternion are the same object: they differ only by the constant $i\sqrt{\epsilon}$, so they carry identical information and have proportional invariants. The complex vector was introduced by Ludwik Silberstein in 1907, and the biquaternion formulation is its natural algebraic home: the complex vector is the vector part of a biquaternion with vanishing scalar part.

The biquaternion norm is now immediate. Since $\vec N$ is a complex three-vector,

$$ \vec N\cdot\vec N = \left(\vec E + ic\vec B\right)\cdot\left(\vec E + ic\vec B\right) = \vec E^2 - c^2\vec B^2 + 2ic\,\vec E\cdot\vec B = I_1 + 2ic\,I_2, $$

and therefore

$$ \langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural} = \left(i\sqrt{\epsilon}\right)^2 \vec N\cdot\vec N = -\epsilon\left(I_1 + 2ic\,I_2\right), $$

where $(i\sqrt{\epsilon})^2=-\epsilon$ by $i^2=-1$, in agreement with the direct computation. The two invariants are the real and imaginary parts of the complex number $\vec N\cdot\vec N$, up to constant factors.

The Riemann–Silberstein vector also linearizes the source-free field equations. In vacuum or in a homogeneous medium with no free sources, Maxwell's equations give $\nabla\times\vec E = -\partial\vec B/\partial r'_t$ and $\nabla\times\vec B = c^{-2}\partial\vec E/\partial r'_t$, and combining these,

$$ i\,\frac{\partial\vec N}{\partial r'_t} = c\,\nabla\times\vec N, \qquad \nabla\cdot\vec N = 0 . $$

The first equation has the form of a Schrödinger equation whose Hamiltonian is $c\,\nabla\times$; the second is the transverse condition that $\nabla\cdot\vec D = 0$ and $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ give. This is the form in which the radiative content of the field is most transparent, and the null condition $\vec N\cdot\vec N = 0$ is precisely the condition that the complex vector be a null vector of the complexified spatial metric. A field whose Riemann–Silberstein vector is null, i.e. for which $\langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural} = 0$, is a radiation field.

Finally, the antisymmetric field tensor decomposes into its self-dual and anti-self-dual parts. Define the Hodge dual by its action on the fields:

$$ \star:\ (\vec E,\vec B) \mapsto (c\,\vec B, -\vec E/c), \qquad \star^2 = -1. $$

This is the $\theta = \pi/2$ case of the duality rotation below. On the Riemann–Silberstein vector the dual is multiplication by $-i$: the dual field carries $\vec N\mapsto-i\vec N$ and $\vec N^*\mapsto+i\vec N^*$. The two combinations $F \pm i\star F$ are therefore the two parts,

$$ F + i\,\star F \ \longleftrightarrow\ 2\vec N = 2(\vec E + ic\vec B), \qquad F - i\,\star F \ \longleftrightarrow\ 2\vec N^* = 2(\vec E - ic\vec B), $$

with $\star(F \pm i\star F) = \mp i\,(F \pm i\star F)$. The $\vec N$ part ($\star F = -iF$) is the self-dual half and the $\vec N^*$ part ($\star F = +iF$) the anti-self-dual half; the two halves are the two helicities of the radiation field. For a real field the two parts are complex conjugates of one another, so $\vec N^*$ adds no new data: $\vec N$ alone already carries the six real field components of $(\vec E,\vec B)$. On the complexified field space they are instead independent, and each carries one of the two three-dimensional complex representations of the Lorentz group, one at each value of the subscript of an element, $L$ and $R$. The biquaternion proof uses the antisymmetric-tensor action of the Lorentz group, not the four-vector rotor conjugation.

Convention. The dual is defined here by its action on $(\vec E,\vec B)$, with $\star^2=-1$. This is the real-time convention, and it is the $\theta=\pi/2$ case of the duality rotation below, $\star\vec N=-i\vec N$. With this definition the self-dual half is $F+i\star F$ ($\star$-eigenvalue $-i$) and the anti-self-dual half is $F-i\star F$ ($\star$-eigenvalue $+i$). Reversing the orientation density, $\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\mapsto-\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$, exchanges the two names and changes nothing else. The component-wise dual read off from the $i c r'_t$ tensor, $(\star_{i c r'_t}F)_{0k}=B_k$, is $-i\star$ and has $\star_{i c r'_t}^2=+1$; it is the same decomposition, with the two parts appearing as the eigenfields $F\pm\star_{i c r'_t}F$ of eigenvalue $\pm1$ instead of the eigenfields $F\pm i\star F$ of eigenvalue $\mp i$.

Duality Rotation

Maxwell's equations in a homogeneous medium possess a continuous symmetry that rotates the electric and magnetic parts into one another. For a real angle $\theta$, define

$$ \vec E \mapsto \vec E\cos\theta + c\,\vec B\sin\theta, \qquad \vec B \mapsto \vec B\cos\theta - \frac{1}{c}\,\vec E\sin\theta . $$

In terms of the Riemann–Silberstein vector this is a phase rotation,

$$ \vec N \mapsto \exp(-i\theta)\,\vec N, \qquad \vec N^* \mapsto \exp(+i\theta)\,\vec N^*, $$

as one checks by expanding $\exp(-i\theta)(\vec E + ic\vec B)$. The object that transforms with $\exp(+i\theta)$ is the complex conjugate $\vec N^* = \vec E - ic\vec B$, not the quaternion conjugate $\vec N^{\natural}$, which for a pure vector is $\vec N^{\natural} = -\vec N$ and therefore transforms with $\exp(-i\theta)$; the two symbols are distinct, ${}^{*}$ conjugating the coefficients of the complex vector and ${}^{\natural}$ negating it. The special case $\theta = \pi/2$,

$$ \vec E \mapsto c\,\vec B, \qquad \vec B \mapsto -\frac{1}{c}\,\vec E, $$

is the classical electric–magnetic duality transformation.

The transformation is a symmetry of the source-free Maxwell equations. Writing $\vec E_\theta,\vec B_\theta$ for the rotated fields, the two equations are preserved, $\nabla\times\vec E_\theta = -\partial\vec B_\theta/\partial r'_t$ and $\nabla\times\vec B_\theta = c^{-2}\partial\vec E_\theta/\partial r'_t$, the two terms of each matching after use of $c^2 = 1/(\epsilon\mu)$. With free electric charges and currents present, the transformation is not a symmetry: it would map an electric charge into a magnetic charge. Duality relates solutions of the source-free equations, and it relates the electric and magnetic parts of a given solution.

The effect of the duality rotation on the invariants is clean. Writing $\vec N\cdot\vec N = I_1 + 2icI_2$, the phase rotation gives

$$ \vec N\cdot\vec N \mapsto \exp(-2i\theta)\,\vec N\cdot\vec N, $$

so that

$$ I_1 \mapsto I_1\cos 2\theta + 2c\,I_2\sin 2\theta, \qquad 2c\,I_2 \mapsto 2c\,I_2\cos 2\theta - I_1\sin 2\theta . $$

In other words, the pair $(I_1,\,2cI_2)$ rotates by the angle $-2\theta$; the quantity

$$ I_1^2 + 4c^2 I_2^2 = \left|\vec N\cdot\vec N\right|^2 = \frac{1}{\epsilon^2}\left|\langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural}\right|^2 $$

is invariant under duality as well as under Lorentz transformations. Each of $I_1$ and $I_2$ is separately Lorentz invariant, but duality mixes them; only the combination above is invariant under both. Equivalently, in biquaternion language,

$$ \langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural} \mapsto \exp(-2i\theta)\,\langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural}, $$

since the scalar $\exp(-i\theta)$ is central and therefore commutes with quaternion conjugation. Duality is not a Lorentz transformation: it is an independent $\mathrm{U}(1)$ symmetry of the source-free equations that rotates the complex biquaternion norm.

How the Invariants Constrain the Field

The two invariants are the complete set of local, derivative-free invariants of the electromagnetic field, and they classify the field into a small number of types. The classification is invariant under the Lorentz action just described, because $I_1$ and $I_2$ are.

Null fields. If $I_1 = 0$ and $I_2 = 0$, then $\vec E\perp\vec B$ and $|\vec E| = c|\vec B|$ at every event. Both invariants vanish, the biquaternion norm vanishes, and the field-strength biquaternion is a zero divisor. No Lorentz transformation can remove either field, because killing one would force the other to vanish as well by the invariant relation; the field is a pure radiation field in every frame. This is the case of greatest interest for the later article on radiation.

Electric and magnetic types. Suppose $I_2 = 0$ but $I_1 \neq 0$. Then $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ are perpendicular. If $I_1 > 0$, the electric magnitude dominates, and there is a Lorentz frame in which $\vec B = 0$: the field is purely electric, with $\vec E^2 = I_1$ in that frame. If instead $I_1 < 0$, there is a frame in which $\vec E = 0$: the field is purely magnetic, with $c^2\vec B^2 = -I_1$. In either case the invariant fixes the magnitude of the surviving field in its rest frame.

Generic fields. If $I_2 \neq 0$, no frame can make either field vanish, because $I_2$ would then vanish too. Instead there is a frame in which $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ are parallel, and there $I_2 = E_0 B_0$ and $I_1 = E_0^2 - c^2B_0^2$ with $E_0,B_0\ge0$. Eliminating $B_0$ gives a quadratic equation for $E_0^2$,

$$ E_0^4 - I_1 E_0^2 - c^2 I_2^2 = 0, $$

whose positive root is

$$ E_0^2 = \frac{I_1 + \sqrt{I_1^2 + 4c^2 I_2^2}}{2}, \qquad B_0 = \frac{I_2}{E_0}. $$

The magnitudes are therefore completely determined by the two invariants. This is the precise sense in which the invariants constrain the field: they do not determine the field, but they determine the type of its Lorentz orbit and the field magnitudes in the frame in which that type is displayed.

What the invariants do not constrain. The invariants are two functions of the six real field components, so many distinct fields share the same pair $(I_1, I_2)$: they determine the local Lorentz type but not the field. They are pointwise kinematical quantities, not dynamical ones, and they are not in general conserved by the free-field evolution — a field that is null at one instant need not be null at the next.

Contrast with the energy density. The biquaternion norm is indefinite and complex: it can vanish, and it gives the Lorentz invariants. The Hermitian form is a different quadratic object, and it gives the positive energy. For the pure vector $\tilde{F}$,

$$ \tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{*} = 2W\,e_t + \frac{2i}{c}\,\vec S, \qquad \tilde{F}^{*}\tilde{F} = 2W\,e_t - \frac{2i}{c}\,\vec S, $$

with the energy density and Poynting vector

$$ W = \frac{1}{2}\left(\epsilon\,\vec E^2 + \mu\,\vec H^2\right) = \frac{1}{2}\left\|\tilde{F}\right\|_E^2, \qquad \vec S = \vec E\times\vec H. $$

The scalar part of $\tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{*}$ is $2W$, twice the (non-negative) energy density, and its vector part is $\frac{2i}{c}\vec S$, $\frac{2}{c}$ times the imaginary unit times the Poynting vector; the result is an element of $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$, as every Hermitian form must be. The contrast is instructive: the biquaternion norm is a Lorentz-invariant complex scalar that can vanish, while the Hermitian form is an $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$-valued object whose scalar part is non-negative — and positive unless the field vanishes — and whose transformation law is not that of a scalar. The first classifies the field; the second measures it. This is the biquaternion expression of the familiar fact that the electromagnetic energy density is positive-definite, whereas the invariant $I_1$ is indefinite.

The energy–momentum biquaternion, and the second route to the invariants. Half the Hermitian form is itself an element of the algebra, and it is the object the electro-gravimagnetic programme calls the energy–momentum biquaternion:

$$ \tilde{\Sigma} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{*} = W\,e_t + \frac{i}{c}\,\vec S . $$

It is Hermitian, $\tilde{\Sigma}^{*} = \tilde{\Sigma}$, with real scalar part $W$ and imaginary vector part $(i/c)\vec S$. For an element of that shape the biquaternion norm is real — and indefinite — and

$$ \langle\tilde{\Sigma}\rangle_{\natural} = \mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{\Sigma}\tilde{\Sigma}^{\natural}\bigr) = W^2 - \frac{\|\vec S\|^2}{c^2} = \frac{\epsilon^2}{4}\,I_1^2 + \frac{\epsilon}{\mu}\,I_2^2 . $$

The last equality is the point of the paragraph: the Hermitian form is not a scalar under Lorentz transformations, but its contraction is, and the contraction returns the two invariants. The energy density and the Poynting vector are therefore tied to the same pair $(I_1, I_2)$ that classifies the field. The programme states the same relation with its pseudonorm; on a Hermitian element that pseudonorm and the corpus's norm $\langle\cdot\rangle_{\natural}$ agree, so the two forms are one statement, and in vacuum ($\epsilon = \mu = 1$) it reads $\langle\tilde{\Sigma}\rangle_{\natural} = \tfrac14 I_1^2 + I_2^2$.

The Flow the Field Generates: The Four Types

The field is a generator, not only a value. The real span of $\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z,i\vec e_x,i\vec e_y,i\vec e_z$ is the bivector part of $\mathrm{Cl}^{+}_{1,3}$, which is the Lie algebra of the Lorentz group. The field therefore defines a one-parameter subgroup of the group acting at the event where it is evaluated, and the subgroup is the conjugation by the rotor

$$ U(t)=\exp\bigl(\tfrac12\,t\,\tilde F\bigr), \qquad \tilde X\ \longmapsto\ U(t)\,\tilde X\,U(t)^{*} . $$

The half sits in the exponent because the angle of a rotor is half the rapidity it generates. The four-dimensional display of the same congruence, $\Lambda(t)$ of the defining representation, carries the full parameter, its generator $G$ being the one with an index raised; the two spellings differ by that factor of two and by the $1/c$ that converts the field to the generator.

The spectrum is the spectrum of the field itself. Two biquaternion identities carry everything the flow does. The first is the square of a pure vector, $\tilde F^2=-\vec F\cdot\vec F\,e_t=-\langle\tilde F\rangle_{\natural}e_t$, already computed. The second is its reading through the principal square root of the medium-free invariant: with $\sigma+i\omega$ the square root of $\vec N\cdot\vec N=-\langle\tilde F\rangle_{\natural}/\epsilon$ and $\sigma\ge0$,

$$ \vec N=(\sigma+i\omega)\,\tilde u, \qquad \tilde u\cdot\tilde u=e_t, \qquad \tilde F=i\sqrt{\epsilon}\,(\sigma+i\omega)\,\tilde u, $$

$$ \sigma^2-\omega^2=I_1,\qquad \sigma\omega=c\,I_2,\qquad \sigma^2+\omega^2=\sqrt{I_1^2+4c^2I_2^2}, $$

so that $\tilde u$ is a unit pure element, $\tilde u^2=-e_t$, the axis of the flow, and $\tilde F^2=\epsilon(\sigma+i\omega)^2e_t=-\langle\tilde F\rangle_{\natural}e_t$, with $\omega$ positive when $I_2$ is positive and negative when $I_2$ is negative.

The two rates. The rotor is the cosine and the sine of one complex angle,

$$ U(r_t)=\cos\theta\,e_t+\tilde u\,\sin\theta, \qquad \theta=\tfrac12\,r_t\,i\sqrt{\epsilon}\,(\sigma+i\omega), $$

one formula for both regimes, since $\tilde u^2=-e_t$ makes the exponential the cosine and sine of a scalar angle: at $\omega=0$ the angle is imaginary and the flow is a boost at the rate $\sigma/c$, at $\sigma=0$ it is real and the flow is a rotation at the rate $\omega/c$, and in general the two are carried together. Read on the display of the congruence, the same statement is

$$ \operatorname{Tr}\Lambda(t)=2\cosh\frac{t\sigma}{c}+2\cos\frac{t\omega}{c}, \qquad \det\Lambda(t)=1 , $$

which is the quickest check of a computed flow. Its generator has the four eigenvalues $\pm\sigma/c$ and $\pm i\omega/c$, so its characteristic polynomial factors as $(\lambda^2-\sigma^2/c^2)(\lambda^2+\omega^2/c^2)$, the form that The Lorentz Force in Biquaternion Form writes as $\lambda^4-(I_1/c^2)\lambda^2-I_2^2/c^2=0$. Eigenvalues are convention-independent, so the two articles carry the same two numbers, and the biquaternion identity behind both is $\tilde F^2=-\langle\tilde F\rangle_{\natural}e_t=\epsilon(\sigma+i\omega)^2e_t$. Those numbers are rates of the flow, a rapidity per unit $t$ and an angle per unit $t$, and the rotor stores half of each.

The type of the field is the type of its own flow.

invariants $(\sigma,\omega)$ the flow field type
$I_2=0$, $I_1>0$ $\omega=0$ boost along $\vec E$, rapidity rate $\sigma/c$ electric
$I_2=0$, $I_1<0$ $\sigma=0$ rotation about $\vec B$, rate $\omega/c$ magnetic
$I_1=I_2=0$ $0,\,0$ null rotation null
otherwise $\sigma,\omega\neq0$ boost composed with rotation generic

These are the four classical types of a one-parameter Lorentz transformation — hyperbolic, elliptic, parabolic and loxodromic in Herglotz's naming, the classification that A Brief History of Biquaternions in Physics records from Herglotz as the four types of a Möbius transformation. The table is therefore not new data on the field. It is the classification of the paragraphs above followed through the exponential rather than left in the algebra, and the two descriptions coincide row by row. Three contributions are separable here and are kept apart: the classification of the one-parameter transformations is Herglotz's, the closed form of the exponential in terms of the two invariants, with its two regimes and its null limit, is Jadczyk and Szulga's, and the reading of the field-strength biquaternion itself as the element of the algebra is this corpus's. The axis of each flow is a line, not an oriented vector: the boost axis is the electric direction and the rotation axis the magnetic one, while the sense of each is that of the exponential itself and is not fixed by the invariants.

The null row is where the two spellings of one rotation are told apart. There $\tilde F^2=0$ with $\tilde F\neq0$: the field is a nilpotent of the algebra, and the rotor truncates at the linear term, $U(r_t)=e_t+\tfrac12t\tilde F$, so the congruence is unipotent. In the defining representation the same flow is unipotent with one three-dimensional block, the degenerate case classified in The Sandwich Exponential of an N-Regular, Spectrally Degenerate Biquaternion. Both statements describe one null rotation; what differs is the carrier, and the difference is the same one that makes $\tilde F$ a zero divisor while the display of its flow is not.

The flow fixes its own generator, and that separates the flow from the field. Because $U(t)$ is a power series in $\tilde F$ it commutes with it, so the subgroup it generates leaves the tensor at the event unchanged: the flow moves frames and not the field. What the flow is not is a worldline. The worldline of a charge is the flow of the generator multiplied by the scalar $ϙ/m$ acting on the four-momentum, in The Lorentz Force in Biquaternion Form; the two generators differ by that scalar, and they agree only in a uniform field, since otherwise the field varies along the motion and the exponent is an ordered product rather than a single exponential.

The invariants and the flow determine each other, with one exception. From $(I_1,I_2)$ the pair $(\sigma^2,\omega^2)$ is recovered, and from the conjugacy class of the flow the pair is recovered as $I_1=\sigma^2-\omega^2$ and $cI_2=\sigma\omega$; the pair therefore determines the flow up to conjugacy and is determined by it. The exception is the null row, where $(0,0)$ is shared by the vanishing field, whose flow is the identity, and by a null field, whose flow is a nontrivial unipotent subgroup; there the pair does not separate the two, and $\tilde F$ itself does, being zero in the one case and a nonzero nilpotent in the other.

Checks. Over $100$ random fields with $c$ drawn from $[0.6,1.8]$ and $t=0.4,0.9$, the trace of $\Lambda(t)$ agreed with $2\cosh(t\sigma/c)+2\cos(t\omega/c)$ to $3\times10^{-15}$ and $\det\Lambda(t)=1$ held to $8\times10^{-15}$; the coefficient of $\lambda^2$ in the characteristic polynomial agreed with $-I_1/c^2$ to $2\times10^{-15}$; and $\tilde F^2=\epsilon(\sigma+i\omega)^2e_t$ held in the algebra in every case, so that $\omega$ was positive exactly when $I_2$ was positive. A pure electric field of unit magnitude along $(1,2,2)$ gave a boost along that axis with $\operatorname{Tr}\Lambda=2\cosh(0.5)+2$, and a pure magnetic field of magnitude two along $(2,2,1)$ a rotation fixing that axis with $\operatorname{Tr}\Lambda=2+2\cos(0.8)$, each to machine precision. A null field gave $\tilde F^2=0$ with $\tilde F\neq0$ and a rotor $e_t+\tfrac12t\tilde F$ truncating after the linear term, while the deviation of its display from the identity was nilpotent and nonzero; a generic field gave four distinct eigenvalues. For the rotor the congruence was checked directly: the display of $\tilde X\mapsto U(s)\tilde XU(s)^{*}$ with $U(s)=\exp(s\tilde F)$ has trace $2\cosh(2s\sigma)+2\cos(2s\omega)$ at $c=1$ to $5\times10^{-15}$, which is the factor of two displayed.

Boundary. The section adds an interpretation and no dynamics. It does not refine the classification of the paragraphs above, and it computes no flow that the field equations ask for. What it records is that the pair which already fixed the frame displaying the field also fixes what the field generates: the invariants are the spectral data of the flow. The standard forms of the four types, their normalisations and their classification up to conjugacy are not developed here; they are Minguzzi's, and Further Reading cites them.

Summary

The field-strength biquaternion $\tilde{F} = i\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\vec E - \sqrt{\mu}\,\vec H$ is a pure-vector biquaternion with vanishing scalar part. It lies in the six-dimensional real vector part of $\mathbb{B}$, decomposed into an imaginary electric piece in the Hermitian subspace $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ and a real magnetic piece in the anti-Hermitian subspace $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$. This is why the field strength is neither a four-vector nor an element of $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$: it is an antisymmetric rank-two tensor, whose electric and magnetic pieces occupy the two complementary sectors of the algebra.

The biquaternion norm of the field strength is the complex scalar

$$ \boxed{\;\langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural} = \langle\tilde{F},\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural} = \mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{\natural}\bigr) = \sum_{k=1}^{3} F_k^2 = -\epsilon\left(I_1 + 2ic\,I_2\right)\;}, \qquad I_1 = \vec E^2 - c^2\vec B^2, \qquad I_2 = \vec E\cdot\vec B. $$

Its real and imaginary parts are the two invariants of the field; the biquaternion norm and its complex conjugate carry the same pair. Both are invariant under the proper orthochronous Lorentz group — exactly in vacuum, formally in a medium; see §Lorentz Invariance of the Invariants — and $I_1$ is unchanged by the spatial reflection while $I_2$ changes its value there. The biquaternion norm vanishes exactly when the field is null, in which case the field-strength biquaternion is a zero divisor.

The same object is, up to the constant $i\sqrt{\epsilon}$, the Riemann–Silberstein vector $\vec N = \vec E + ic\vec B$, whose self-product $\vec N\cdot\vec N = I_1 + 2icI_2$ carries the two invariants, and which obeys the source-free equation $i\partial\vec N/\partial r'_t = c\,\nabla\times\vec N$ with $\nabla\cdot\vec N = 0$.

Duality is the rotation $\vec N\mapsto \exp(-i\theta)\vec N$; at $\theta = \pi/2$ it is the classical electric–magnetic duality. It is a symmetry of the source-free Maxwell equations, and it rotates the pair $(I_1, 2cI_2)$ by the angle $-2\theta$, leaving $I_1^2 + 4c^2I_2^2$ invariant.

The field also has a polar reading, $\tilde F=\tilde F_H\tilde F_0$: the amplitude carries the intensity and the invariant modulus, and the phase carries the duality angle and the polarization.

Finally, the invariants classify the field into null, electric, magnetic, and generic types, and fix the field magnitudes in the frame that displays each type; they are the complete set of local, derivative-free invariants, but they are not a complete description of the field. The same pair fixes what the field generates: since the field is an element of the Lorentz Lie algebra, its exponential is a one-parameter subgroup whose type — hyperbolic, elliptic, parabolic or loxodromic — is the type of the field, so the classification followed through the exponential agrees row by row with the classification in the algebra.
