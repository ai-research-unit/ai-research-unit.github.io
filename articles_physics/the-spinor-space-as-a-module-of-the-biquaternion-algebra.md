# __The Spinor Space as a Module of the Biquaternion Algebra__

## Introduction

The spinor space of the biquaternion framework is the minimal left ideal $S=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+$ generated by the idempotent $\tilde\Pi_+=\tfrac12(e_t+i\vec e_z)$. Its elements are the spinors; the algebra acts on them by left multiplication; and the action carries the defining representation of the double cover of the Lorentz group. The present article reads this space in the **general plain algebra** (GPA) alone, the block of the plain bilinear product $\tilde P\tilde Q$, whose menu label is **composition**.

This article is one of a pair of focused interpretations. Its transversal companion *The Spinor Space as a Module and as a Hilbert Space* states the two structures the space carries and the two products they belong to. Here only the module side is developed: the carrier, the ideal, the action, the module-theoretic facts, the bilinear pairing the module itself carries, and the algebra's own form with its behaviour on the carrier. The two structures are not halves of one object — they belong to two different products — so this article can and does stand on the plain product alone.

The conventions are those of the companion articles. The biquaternion algebra is $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$, with basis $e_t=1,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z$, the relations $\vec e_k^2=-e_t$ and $\vec e_x\vec e_y=\vec e_z$, and central scalar imaginary $i$. The conjugations are the natural conjugation $\tilde Q^{\natural}=Q_t-\vec Q$, the Hermitian conjugation $\tilde Q^{*}=\overline{\tilde Q^{\natural}}$, and the coefficient conjugation $\bar{\tilde Q}$. The scalar part is $\mathrm{Sc}$, so that $\mathrm{Tr}=2\,\mathrm{Sc}$; the plain product is written by juxtaposition $\tilde P\tilde Q$, and the symbol $\star$ is reserved for the sesquilinear block. The matrix model is $\mathsf{M}_2(\vec e_k)=-i\sigma_k$, $\mathbb{B}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$.

## The Product the Module Is Defined In

Every class of element in the corpus is defined by an equation whose symbol is a product: an idempotent by $\tilde\Pi\tilde\Pi=\tilde\Pi$, a nilpotent by $\tilde\Upsilon\tilde\Upsilon=0$, a zero divisor by $\tilde Q\tilde{Q}_3=0$. The definitions article *Definitions for the Study of the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternioid $\mathbb{C}$ Space* states the rule: **the product is part of the definition and not a background convention**, so a class is always relative to a product. The spinor space is the ideal generated by an idempotent of the **plain** product, and every word used about it below — idempotent, nilpotent, ideal, module — is a word about that product.

Of the twelve structures the complex space carries, the plain product $\tilde P\tilde Q$ is the only associative unital one, and the exact threshold of its theory is left alternativity, $\tilde Q_1(\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2)=(\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_1)\tilde Q_2$ at every pair (*The Specificities of the Biquaternion Algebra*). From that one law follow the projector calculus of the idempotents, the flag of the nilpotents, the quadratic Peirce decomposition with corner subalgebras and matrix units, and the module theory of the block. None of it survives the loss of the law: the four general products other than the plain fail through the conjugation of a slot, the antisymmetric products through the cross terms and the Jacobi defect, and the sesquilinear products through the conjugate-linearity that collapses their projectors. The module structure of $S$ is therefore a fact about the plain product and about no other structure of the twelve.

## The Carrier and Its Basis

### The Idempotent, the Nilpotent and the Ideal

Let

$$
\tilde\Pi_+=\tfrac12(e_t+i\vec e_z),\qquad \tilde\Pi_-=\tfrac12(e_t-i\vec e_z),\qquad \vec\Upsilon_x=\tfrac12(i\vec e_x-\vec e_y),\qquad \vec\Upsilon_y=\tfrac12(i\vec e_x+\vec e_y).
$$

Here $i\vec e_z$ is a root of $+1$, $(i\vec e_z)^2=e_t$, and $\tilde\Pi_+$ and $\tilde\Pi_-$ are its two associated idempotents.

The pair $\tilde\Pi_+,\tilde\Pi_-$ is orthogonal and complete in the plain product,

$$
\tilde\Pi_+^2=\tilde\Pi_+,\qquad \tilde\Pi_-^2=\tilde\Pi_-,\qquad \tilde\Pi_+\tilde\Pi_-=\tilde\Pi_-\tilde\Pi_+=0,\qquad \tilde\Pi_++\tilde\Pi_-=e_t,
$$

and each generates a minimal left ideal. The spinor space is the first of them,

$$
S=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_y\},
$$

of complex dimension two, the defining module of $\mathbb{B}$. The four elements $\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_y,\vec\Upsilon_x,\tilde\Pi_-$ are a $\mathbb{C}$-basis of $\mathbb{B}$ whose products are the matrix units $E_{11},E_{21},E_{12},E_{22}$ of the matrix model $\mathsf{M}_2$:

| $\cdot$ | $\tilde\Pi_+$ | $\tilde\Pi_-$ | $\vec\Upsilon_x$ | $\vec\Upsilon_y$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\tilde\Pi_+$ | $\tilde\Pi_+$ | $0$ | $\vec\Upsilon_x$ | $0$ |
| $\tilde\Pi_-$ | $0$ | $\tilde\Pi_-$ | $0$ | $\vec\Upsilon_y$ |
| $\vec\Upsilon_x$ | $0$ | $\vec\Upsilon_x$ | $0$ | $\tilde\Pi_+$ |
| $\vec\Upsilon_y$ | $\vec\Upsilon_y$ | $0$ | $\tilde\Pi_-$ | $0$ |

### Why the Basis Is One Idempotent and One Nilpotent

The basis $(\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_y)$ of $S$ is not a choice. Because $\tilde\Pi_++\tilde\Pi_-=e_t$, every element of the left ideal expands as

$$
\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+=(\tilde\Pi_++\tilde\Pi_-)\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+=\tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+\oplus\tilde\Pi_-\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+,
$$

the sum of the $(1,1)$ corner and the $(2,1)$ corner of the Peirce decomposition of $\mathbb{B}$. In the matrix model of a central simple algebra of rank two each corner is one-dimensional over $\mathbb{C}$, so the ideal, of complex dimension two, holds exactly one basis element per corner:

$$
\tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_+,\qquad \tilde\Pi_-\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+=\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_y .
$$

The **diagonal** corner contains the idempotent $\tilde\Pi_+$, the identity of that corner; the **off-diagonal** corner contains $\vec\Upsilon_y$, which is square-zero. The nilpotency is not particular to $\vec\Upsilon_y$: for orthogonal idempotents $e,f$, every element $x=fae$ of an off-diagonal corner satisfies

$$
x^2=(fae)(fae)=fa(ef)ae=0,
$$

so **every** off-diagonal corner consists of square-zero elements, and the off-diagonal basis element of a minimal left ideal is necessarily a nilpotent. The count is structural: a minimal left ideal is one column of the matrix model, and in rank two a column is one diagonal corner plus one off-diagonal corner. In the general rank-$n$ algebra $M_n(\mathbb{C})$ the same argument gives one idempotent and $n-1$ nilpotents, one per off-diagonal corner; the basis of $S$ is the case $n=2$. The general count is *The Column Ideals of a Matrix Ring: One Idempotent and $n-1$ Nilpotents*, and the decomposition itself is *Ideals and Peirce Decomposition of the Biquaternion Algebra*.

## The Module Structure

### The Left Action

The plain product makes $S$ a left $\mathbb{B}$-module: for $\tilde Q\in\mathbb{B}$ and $\psi\in S$ the product $\tilde Q\psi$ lies again in $S$, and the action is compatible with the product, $\tilde Q(\tilde{Q}_3\psi)=(\tilde Q\tilde{Q}_3)\psi$. On the basis $(\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_y)$ the left multiplications by the four basis elements are

$$
L_{\tilde\Pi_+}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
L_{\tilde\Pi_-}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
L_{\vec\Upsilon_x}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
L_{\vec\Upsilon_y}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

These are the matrices of the matrix model $\mathsf{M}_2$ with $\mathsf{M}_2(\vec e_k)=-i\sigma_k$, so the module is literally the column space $\mathbb{C}^2$ on which the algebra acts by matrix multiplication. The coordinate map

$$
\psi=\psi_1\tilde\Pi_++\psi_2\vec\Upsilon_y\ \longleftrightarrow\ \begin{pmatrix}\psi_1\\ \psi_2\end{pmatrix}
$$

is an isomorphism of left $\mathbb{B}$-modules, because the product distributes over the two basis elements and the left multiplication is $\mathbb{C}$-linear.

### The Simple Module and the Regular Module

The algebra $\mathbb{B}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$ is simple and finite-dimensional, so its modules are completely reducible and there is, up to isomorphism, exactly one simple module, the column space $S=\mathbb{C}^2$. By Schur's lemma its endomorphism ring is the scalars,

$$
\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{B}}(S)=\mathbb{C},
$$

so $S$ is of complex type, and every finite-dimensional module is a direct sum of copies of it. In particular the left regular module is

$$
\mathbb{B}\cong S\oplus S
$$

of complex dimension four, and the algebra is exactly the algebra of all $\mathbb{C}$-linear endomorphisms of the module,

$$
\mathbb{B}\cong\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(S).
$$

The category of left modules is equivalent to that of complex vector spaces — the block is Morita-trivial — and the module theory is *Modules over the Biquaternion Algebra*. The ideal model and the column model are the same module, and the biquaternion coordinate form of the action is *The Spinor Module in Biquaternionic Form and Its Lorentz Action*.

## The Two Forms on the Carrier

### The Invariant Bilinear Pairing of the Module

The module carries a one-dimensional space of invariant bilinear pairings, spanned by the **symplectic form**

$$
\varepsilon(\psi,\phi)=\psi_1\phi_2-\psi_2\phi_1=\psi^{T}\epsilon\,\phi,\qquad
\epsilon=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

It is bilinear, alternating and non-degenerate, and it is invariant under the module action: for $g=\mathsf{M}_2(\tilde U)$ with $\tilde U$ of unit norm, $\det g=1$ and therefore

$$
g^{T}\epsilon\, g=(\det g)\,\epsilon=\epsilon,\qquad\text{so}\qquad \varepsilon(g\psi,g\phi)=\varepsilon(\psi,\phi).
$$

The form identifies the module with its linear dual,

$$
S\cong S^{\vee},
$$

so the defining representation is **self-dual**: the spinor metric behind the invariant antisymmetric contraction of a pair of spinors is this pairing. Self-duality is a statement about the module and its dual and is not a conjugacy statement; the conjugate module is the subject of the companion article. A caution on the symbol: the transverse article writes $S^{*}\cong S$ for this self-duality, but the corpus also writes $S^{*}=\tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}$ for the Hermitian-conjugate right ideal, which the sibling complement article uses. This article reserves $S^{*}$ for that right ideal and writes the linear dual as $S^{\vee}$, so the two statements are not in conflict. The coexistence of this bilinear pairing with the Hermitian form of the sibling is the two-slot mechanism of the algebra, the bilinear form on the algebra side and the sesquilinear form on the sesqualgebra side (*The Four General Products and Their Physical Readings*).

### The Algebra's Own Bilinear Form and Its Degeneracy on the Carrier

The plain product carries the symmetric bilinear form of the algebra,

$$
\langle\tilde P,\tilde Q\rangle=\operatorname{Sc}(\tilde P\tilde Q)=P_0Q_t-(\vec P,\vec Q),
$$

symmetric, $\mathbb{C}$-bilinear and non-degenerate on $\mathbb{B}$; on the real space it is indefinite of signature $(4,4)$, positive definite on the informational sector $\mathbb{M}_+$ and negative definite on the material sector $\mathbb{M}_-$. It is the form of the GPA band (*The Products, Their Forms and Their Norms*), and it is not the metric: it carries the sector sign and no positivity off the sectors.

Restricted to the carrier it degenerates. On the basis $(\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_y)$ its Gram matrix is

$$
B_S=\begin{pmatrix}\langle\tilde\Pi_+,\tilde\Pi_+\rangle&\langle\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_y\rangle\\[2pt]\langle\vec\Upsilon_y,\tilde\Pi_+\rangle&\langle\vec\Upsilon_y,\vec\Upsilon_y\rangle\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac12&0\\[2pt]0&0\end{pmatrix},
$$

of rank one, with radical

$$
\operatorname{rad}B_S=\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_y .
$$

The nilpotent is invisible to $\langle\tilde P,\tilde Q\rangle$. It has zero scalar part and squares to zero, so $\vec\Upsilon_y$ is orthogonal to both basis spinors, $\langle\vec\Upsilon_y,\tilde\Pi_+\rangle=\operatorname{Sc}(\vec\Upsilon_y)=0$ and $\langle\vec\Upsilon_y,\vec\Upsilon_y\rangle=\operatorname{Sc}(\vec\Upsilon_y^2)=0$, hence to every element of $S$. The algebra's own form therefore cannot separate the two basis spinors and cannot produce a norm on the carrier. Positivity is not a property of the plain product at all; it needs the second slot read through the Hermitian conjugation ${}^{*}$, which is the structure of the companion article. This is the sharpest way to see that the module structure and the metric are two structures and not two views of one object: the form the module's own product carries is degenerate on the module.

## The Lorentz Action and the Spin

The physical action carried by the module is the Lorentz action, and it is the plain left multiplication of the algebra. A unit-norm biquaternion $\tilde U$, $\tilde U\tilde U^{\natural}=e_t$, acts by

$$
\psi\longmapsto\tilde U\psi,\qquad \tilde U\in SL(2,\mathbb{C}),\quad\psi\in S .
$$

The action is one-sided, it is faithful, and it does not descend to the Lorentz group: the element $-e_t$ acts as $-\mathrm{id}$ on $S$ while it acts trivially by conjugation on the four-vectors of $\mathbb{M}_-$. The double cover therefore appears on the module side and not on the four-vector side, and spin-$\tfrac12$ is a datum of the module. The action is the composition interpretation of the plain product with one factor held fixed: the module is what the algebra's composition *acts on*.

Two cautions belong here, and they are corrections of statements that are easy to make.

- **A single minimal left ideal is one chirality, not two.** The ideals $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+$ and $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$ are both isomorphic to $S$ and carry the **same** defining representation of $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$, so the module structure alone does not distinguish them. The two Weyl halves are $S$ and its conjugate module $\bar S$, which are non-isomorphic as complex representations; the conjugate module, the complex conjugation and the second convention of the word chirality are the companion article's subject.
- **The module is not the material sector.** The carrier $S$ meets the sectors $\mathbb{M}_\pm$ in one real dimension each, so neither the spinor space nor its complement is a sector. The sector split of the algebra and the ideal split are independent, and a physical statement that uses one must say which.

## What the Module Structure Does Not Carry

The module structure supplies the representation, the ideals, the Peirce decomposition, the enveloping algebra and the intertwining maps, and it supplies no norm, no probability and no measurement. The reader should keep three separations.

- **No positivity.** The algebra's form $\langle\tilde P,\tilde Q\rangle$ is indefinite on the real space and degenerate on $S$; the module has no canonical normalised state. A normalised state is a Hilbert datum.
- **No Born rule.** The probability pairing is the scalar part of the plain sesquilinear product $\tilde P\tilde Q^{*}$, not of the plain product; it belongs to the companion article and to the GPS band.
- **No pairing of the carrier by the product.** The two sesquilinear products do not preserve $S$ at all: they couple a state to a conjugate slot and land outside the ideal. A sesquilinear product pairs the space; it does not act on it.

## Worked Verification

The claims are recomputed on the basis $(\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_y,\vec\Upsilon_x,\tilde\Pi_-)$ and on the four matrix units.

- **The plain products.** $\tilde\Pi_+^2=\tilde\Pi_+$, $\tilde\Pi_-^2=\tilde\Pi_-$, $\vec\Upsilon_x^2=\vec\Upsilon_y^2=0$, $\vec\Upsilon_x\vec\Upsilon_y=\tilde\Pi_+$, $\vec\Upsilon_y\vec\Upsilon_x=\tilde\Pi_-$, $\tilde\Pi_+\tilde\Pi_-=\tilde\Pi_-\tilde\Pi_+=0$, $\tilde\Pi_++\tilde\Pi_-=e_t$, all in the plain product.
- **Closure of the carrier.** Every left multiplication maps $\{\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_y\}$ into $\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_y\}$, with the matrices displayed in §*The Left Action*.
- **One idempotent and one nilpotent.** $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+=\tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+\oplus\tilde\Pi_-\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_+\oplus\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_y$, each corner one-dimensional, and $(\tilde\Pi_-a\tilde\Pi_+)^2=0$ for a generic $a$.
- **The algebra's form.** $\langle\tilde\Pi_+,\tilde\Pi_+\rangle=\tfrac12$, $\langle\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_y\rangle=\langle\vec\Upsilon_y,\tilde\Pi_+\rangle=\langle\vec\Upsilon_y,\vec\Upsilon_y\rangle=0$, so $B_S=\operatorname{diag}(\tfrac12,0)$ and $\operatorname{rad}B_S=\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_y$; also $\langle\vec\Upsilon_y,\vec\Upsilon_x\rangle=\tfrac12$, so $\langle\tilde P,\tilde Q\rangle$ does not separate the two columns.
- **The symplectic form.** $g^{T}\epsilon g=\epsilon$ for $\det g=1$ (verified on random unimodular $g$, error below $10^{-9}$), so $\varepsilon$ is invariant and non-degenerate.
- **The matrix model.** $\mathsf{M}_2(\vec e_k)=-i\sigma_k$ gives $\tilde\Pi_+\mapsto E_{11}$, $\vec\Upsilon_y\mapsto E_{21}$, $\vec\Upsilon_x\mapsto E_{12}$, $\tilde\Pi_-\mapsto E_{22}$, and the left multiplications match the column action of $M_2(\mathbb{C})$.

## Boundaries

- **Cited rather than re-derived.** The plain product and the twelve structures are *The Four General Products of the Biquaternioid $\mathbb{C}$ Space*, *The 12 Products of the Biquaternioid Complex Space* and *The Specificities of the Biquaternion Algebra*; the ideals, the Peirce decomposition and the off-diagonal nilpotency are *Ideals and Peirce Decomposition of the Biquaternion Algebra*; the module theory is *Modules over the Biquaternion Algebra*; the operator algebra is *The Enveloping Operator Algebra of the Biquaternion Algebra and the Bi-module Structure*; the spinor module, its action and its pairings are *The Spinor Module in Biquaternionic Form and Its Lorentz Action*; the algebra's form is *The Products, Their Forms and Their Norms*.
- **Not claimed.** The article adds no measurement postulate, identifies no probability with a frequency, and does not identify the spinor index with the qubit's two levels. It states the GPA structure of one carrier, not a new structure of the algebra, and it does not claim that the module structure is the whole of the spinor space.
- **The module and the metric are two structures.** The article deliberately does not develop the positive definite form, the Born rule or the conjugate module; those are the companion articles *The Spinor Space as a Module and as a Hilbert Space* and *The Complementary Space of the Spinor Space and Its Physical Meaning*.

## Physical Readings

- **The module is composition.** The interpretation is that the plain product composes two elements and the module is that composition read with one factor held fixed, so the associativity, the unit and the non-commutativity of the product are the composability, the identity and the order of the operations. This is the GPA band's own interpretation, and the carrier is where its action lives. Boundary: an interpretation of the algebra's law, not a dynamics.
- **The spin is a module datum.** The interpretation is that the double cover acts faithfully on the module, which is where spin-$\tfrac12$ lives, while the metric is blind to the sign of a vector and yields the ray. Boundary: a separation of two known facts by the two structures, and it adds no new representation.
- **The carrier has no norm of its own.** The interpretation is that the algebra's form is degenerate on the carrier, so an unnormalised spinor is what the module gives and normalisation is added by the metric. Boundary: a statement about the two forms, not a claim about physical states.
- **States are module elements, observables are module endomorphisms.** The interpretation is that $\mathbb{B}\cong\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(S)$ acts on $S$, so the observables are the endomorphisms of the module and the trace pairing of the framework is the pairing of the two interpretations of $S$. Boundary: the identification of the two interpretations — spinor and qubit — is not made; only the algebra of the pair is stated.

## Summary

The spinor space $S=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_y\}$ is a **left module of the general plain algebra**, the algebra of the plain bilinear product $\tilde P\tilde Q$. The plain product is the only associative unital law among the twelve structures of the complex space, and its exact threshold is left alternativity; from that law follow the Peirce decomposition, the ideal lattice and the module theory. The module is a minimal left ideal of $\mathbb{B}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$, its basis is forced to be one idempotent and one nilpotent because a minimal left ideal is one column of the matrix model and a column is one diagonal corner plus one off-diagonal corner, and it is the unique simple module, of endomorphism ring $\mathbb{C}$, with $\mathbb{B}\cong S\oplus S$ and $\mathbb{B}\cong\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(S)$.

The module carries the invariant symplectic form $\varepsilon(\psi,\phi)=\psi^{T}\epsilon\phi$, non-degenerate and invariant because $\det g=1$, which identifies $S$ with its linear dual. The algebra's own symmetric form $\langle\tilde P,\tilde Q\rangle=\operatorname{Sc}(\tilde P\tilde Q)$ is non-degenerate on $\mathbb{B}$ and indefinite of signature $(4,4)$ on the real space, but its restriction to the carrier is $\operatorname{diag}(\tfrac12,0)$ of rank one, with radical $\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_y$. The nilpotent is invisible to the algebra's form, so positivity is not a property of the plain product and the metric of the space is a second structure, belonging to the general plain sesqualgebra and to the companion article. The physical action carried by the module is the Lorentz action $\psi\mapsto\tilde U\psi$, one-sided, faithful and a genuine representation of the double cover; spin-$\tfrac12$ is a module datum, and the module supplies no norm, no probability and no measurement.
