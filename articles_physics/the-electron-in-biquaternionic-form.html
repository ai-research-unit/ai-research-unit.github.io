The Electron in Biquaternionic Form

Introduction

The electron is the object on which the relativistic quantum theory of this series is calibrated. It is the particle of Dirac's 1928 equation, the spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ field whose magnetic moment is measured to twelve significant figures, and the fermion whose two values of the chirality the Standard Model treats differently. In the biquaternion framework of these articles the electron is the biquaternion Dirac field $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\in\mathbb{B}$: an element of the algebra whose two chirality parts satisfy the pair

$$ \tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R} = k_{m_e}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}, \qquad (\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L} = k_{m_e}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}, $$

with $m = m_e$ the electron mass. At $m=0$ the pair splits into the two one-sided equations

$$ \tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R} = 0, \qquad (\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L} = 0, $$

one equation per value of the chirality. Each part satisfies the Klein–Gordon equation $(\Box - k_{m_e}^{2})\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L,R} = 0$, with $\hbar$ and $c$ restored. The algebra's real structure is the involution $\flat=-{}^{*}$ (Algebra and Element Conventions); it is a map of the algebra and not of a carrier, and it is not the mass term. Here $1/k_{m_e}=\hbar/(m_ec)$ is the mass length.

A framework that reformulates known physics can be read in two very different ways. Read generously, every correct statement about the electron becomes a statement the framework "contains"; read carefully, most of those statements are properties of the Dirac equation that the framework merely transcribes, and the specific numbers attached to the electron must be inserted by hand. This article takes the second interpretation and asks a single question: which properties of the electron are consequences of the biquaternion structure, which are consequences of the Dirac equation the framework contains, which are parameters inserted from outside, and which lie outside the framework altogether?

The answer, stated plainly at the outset, is the following. The framework forces the electron's spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ representation content and its tree-level gyromagnetic factor $g=2$; it contains the decomposition by chirality, the mass term as the off-diagonal coefficient that relates the two chirality parts, and the Zitterbewegung with its scale; it inserts the mass $m_e$, the charge $e$, and the unit $\hbar$; and it does not contain the anomalous magnetic moment, the weak chiral coupling, or a derivation of the mass or charge values. The electron's data are not contained in either subspace, $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ or $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$: its spin state, its current, and its Coulomb field are distributed across both, the operation that exchanges the two subspaces is the central $i$ (the rest-energy phase), and the algebra's real structure $\flat = -{}^{*}$ acts on the two sectors with opposite signs, while the mass term relates the two parts of the chirality.

A second, less comfortable point belongs in the introduction. Almost every statement the framework makes about "the electron" is a statement about a general structureless spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ field. The algebra does not single the electron out. Its mass, its charge, and the identity of its antiparticle are the inputs that make the general field into this field.

The form of the writing. Every equation below is an equation between elements of the algebra, and every operator is written as the act it performs. A one-sided action is written by position, $a\tilde Q$ and $\tilde Qb$; a two-sided action is written out, $\tilde Q\mapsto a\tilde Qb$; and an operator is named only where the operator itself is the subject of the sentence. The one display of the older formulation is kept in a closing section, for the reader who wants the standard notation.

Mass: A Term the Framework Needs but Cannot Supply

The electron mass appears in the free equation as the coefficient of the chirality-off-diagonal pair

$$ \tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R} = k_{m_e}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}, \qquad (\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L} = k_{m_e}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R} . $$

Three statements are worth separating — how the mass relates the two parts of the chirality, how the algebra's real structure acts on the two sectors, and what phase the mass puts between the sectors — and a fourth, negative statement closes the section.

It relates the two parts of the chirality. The two parts of the field are the part of subscript $L$ and the part of subscript $R$, and the mass term joins them: without it, the electron would be two independent massless fields. The electron's mass is therefore represented, in the framework, as a term that relates the two parts, and this is the structural origin, in the biquaternion reading, of the fact that a field of one value alone cannot carry a mass — the scalar density pairs one value with the other and vanishes on a definite value. Left multiplication keeps the value of its operand, and a product takes the value of its right factor, so a left multiplication by one element of $\mathbb{B}$ never carries one value onto the other. The mass is such a left multiplication: it is a central coefficient and it keeps the value as well, and what it joins is the two parts. The pair is complex-linear in the field but not left-$\mathbb{B}$-linear, which is the precise sense in which the mass sits outside the action the algebra supplies of itself. The value is exchanged by a right multiplication perpendicular to the declared axis and by the coefficient conjugation, and those two moves are the ones the two-sided form of the equation uses.

The real structure acts on the two sectors with opposite signs. This is a derived form of the star's two sector signs. For an element $\tilde{Q} = \tilde{Q}_+ + \tilde{Q}_-$ with $\tilde{Q}_\pm\in\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}\cup\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ one has $\tilde{Q}_\pm^{*} = \pm\tilde{Q}_\pm$, hence

$$ \tilde{Q}^\flat = -\tilde{Q}^{*} = -\tilde{Q}_+ + \tilde{Q}_-, $$

so $\flat$ acts as $-1$ on the $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ part and $+1$ on the $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ part. It preserves each sector and marks it with a sign: it is diagonal on the sector decomposition, not an exchange of the two sectors. This is a property of the algebra's real structure, not of the mass term.

It is not the mass, though it has the shape of one. An algebra-level coupling built on $\flat$ — the single-field equation $\tilde\nabla_\PhiF = mF^\flat$ on $F\in\mathbb{B}$, the retired form recorded in Algebra and Element Conventions — is Majorana-shaped: because $\flat$ is antilinear, such a term pairs the field with its own conjugate and is not invariant under the continuous central phase, which is exactly how a Majorana pairing differs from an ordinary Dirac mass. But it is the wrong equation for the electron, not merely a differently written one: its central-phase plane waves sit on the spacelike locus rather than on the mass shell. The electron's mass must therefore break the central phase rather than being built from $\flat$, and in this article it does — it is the off-diagonal chirality pair, and $\flat$ is used only for conjugation. The operation that exchanges the two sectors is multiplication by the central $i$, $i\,\mathbb{B}_{\Psi} = \mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$.

It sets the rate at which the two sectors rotate into one another. Factoring the rest energy out of a free plane wave gives

$$ \exp\!\Big(-c\,k_m\,i\,r'_{t}\Big) = \cos\!\Big(c\,k_m\,r'_{t}\Big)e_t - \sin\!\Big(c\,k_m\,r'_{t}\Big)(ie_t), $$

where the right-hand side uses only $i^2=-1$ and $e_t=1$: expanding the exponential, $\exp(-i\theta)=e_t-i\theta e_t-\tfrac12\theta^2e_t+\cdots$ and collecting the real and imaginary parts gives the cosine and the sine. Here $e_t\in\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ and $ie_t\in\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ (an imaginary scalar is anti-Hermitian). The rest-energy phase is central, and because $i\,\mathbb{B}_{\Psi} = \mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ it is a rotation between the sectors at angular frequency $ck_{m_e}$: it takes the $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ direction $e_t$ toward the $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ direction $ie_t$ and back. The mass sets this scale — the frequency is $ck_{m_e}$ — through the mass shell. This is the sense in which the electron's mass, in the framework, is the rate of the rotation between the material and informational subspaces.

What is not derived. The value $m_e = 9.1093837015\times10^{-31}$ kg, or equivalently $m_ec^2 = 0.510998950$ MeV, is not a consequence of the algebra or of the equation; it is a parameter. The framework is scale-free until $m_e$ is supplied, and every electron scale below — the Compton length, the Zitterbewegung frequency — is set by the supplied value, not produced by the structure.

Charge: A Coupling Constant in the Gradient

The free biquaternion Dirac equation contains no charge. Charge enters only through the coupling of the field to the four-potential $\tilde{A}_{\Phi} = i\,a'_t\,e_t + \vec A\in\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$. In the intrinsic form this is the minimal substitution

$$ \tilde P_{\Phi} \to \tilde P_{\Phi} - ϙ\tilde A_{\Phi}, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad p_\mu \to p_\mu - ϙA_\mu, $$

with $ϙ$ the particle's charge; for the electron $ϙ = -e$. The precise biquaternion form of this coupling is set out in the companion article The Minimal Coupling of the Biquaternion Dirac Field to Electromagnetism, which replaces $\tilde\nabla_\Phi$ and $(\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}$ by the gauge-covariant derivative $\tilde\nabla_{\mathrm{gco}}$ of Operator and Calculus Conventions and its conjugate $\tilde\nabla_{\mathrm{gco}}^{\natural}$, both carrying $+\frac{i\,ϙ}{\hbar}\tilde{A}_{\Phi}$, and derives the coupled equation

$$ \tilde\nabla_{\mathrm{gco}}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R} = k_{m_e}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}, \qquad \tilde\nabla_{\mathrm{gco}}^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L} = k_{m_e}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}, $$

and shows that it is exactly gauge covariant in the massive case as well as the massless one, the central phase passing through the linear mass pair; the charge is therefore represented in the framework, not derived by it.

Four statements are worth separating.

The conservation law is derived. Given the equation, a four-current is conserved. The current is the material-sector element $\tilde{J}_{\Phi} = ic\,j_0 e_t + \vec j$, with $j_0\ge0$, and its conservation is the vanishing of the scalar part of $(\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde{J}_{\Phi}$, that is $\partial j_0/\partial r'_t + \nabla\cdot\vec j = 0$. That the electron's conserved current is a material-sector object is a structural consequence of the equation, not an added assumption. What is not derived is the bilinear itself: it is not a product of the field with its own conjugate inside the algebra, because it carries an adjoint read through a form, and a pairing of two elements is not an element. It is displayed, once and in the older formulation, in the closing section of the companion article on minimal coupling, where it is also shown that no bilinear of the field alone takes its place. No step of this article uses it: the conservation law just stated is all that the sections below need.

The value and sign of the charge are inserted. Nothing in the algebra fixes $e$, explains why charge is quantised, or explains why the electron and the proton have equal and opposite charge. The equation with $ϙ = -e$ and the equation with $ϙ = +e$ have the same form, related by $\tilde{A}_{\Phi}\to-\tilde{A}_{\Phi}$; the labelling of which branch is the electron is a convention. Charge conjugation makes this explicit: the equation admits a symmetry that exchanges the two frequency branches and reverses the sign of the current, and the operator that realises it is displayed in the closing section. The framework represents both signs of charge on the same footing.

The electron's Coulomb field is an $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ object. For a point charge $ϙ$ at rest the field-strength biquaternion is, from the companion article on Maxwell's equations,

$$ \tilde{F}(r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z) = \frac{i\,ϙ\,\vec u_x}{4\pi\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\|(r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z)\|^2}, $$

a purely imaginary vector, hence an element of the Hermitian subspace $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$, with $\vec u_x = (r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z)/\|(r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z)\|$ the radial unit vector. The electron's charge shows itself, in the framework, through a field in the informational sector, while its current lies in the material sector. The two faces of the same charge sit in opposite halves of the algebra.

What is derived once the coupling is inserted. The minimal substitution yields the magnetic coupling with the coefficient $1/2m_e$, and hence the sign relation $\vec\mu_e = -(e/m_e)\vec S$: the electron's magnetic moment is anti-parallel to its spin because its charge is negative. The magnitude, however, is dimensional and carries $e$ and $\hbar$.

Spin: Derived as a Representation, Normalised by Hand

The electron's spin is where the framework is strongest and where the distinction between derived and inserted is sharpest.

Derived: the representation. The algebra $\mathbb{B}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$ has a unique irreducible left action, on a complex space of dimension two, and the group of unit elements of the quaternionic norm is $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$, the double cover of the proper orthochronous Lorentz group (Norm and Pairing Conventions). A field in that space carries the representation $(\tfrac12,0)$; the field of this article splits by the chirality into two such parts and carries $(\tfrac12,0)\oplus(0,\tfrac12)$. In either case the spin is $\tfrac12$: a rotation by $2\pi$ acts as $-e_t$ on the two-dimensional space and as $+e_t$ on the four-vectors of $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$. The twofold, double-valued character of electron spin is therefore a consequence of where the field lives, not an independent postulate. The spin rotations are the real unit quaternions, forming $SU(2)$.

Derived: the state and observable structure. The electron's spin state along a declared unit direction $\vec u$ is the state $\tilde{Ϛ}_{\Psi}(\vec u)\in\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ of Algebra and Element Conventions, and its spin observables are the Hermitian elements $i \sum_\eta h_\eta\vec e_\eta\in\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$. With the Born pairing of Norm and Pairing Conventions the state along $\vec u$ returns the weight $n_\nu h_\nu$ on $\tilde H_\Psi = i\sum_\eta h_\eta\vec e_\eta$; in particular the state along $\vec e_z$ returns $1$ on $i\vec e_z$ and $0$ on $i\vec e_x$, the values the spin along $z$ requires.

Inserted: the magnitude. The spin element is $\tilde S_{\Psi,\eta} =\frac{\hbar}{2}(i\vec e_\eta)$. The element $i\vec e_\eta$ is Hermitian and squares to $+e_t$, so it is the algebra's spin component and has the two eigenvalues $\pm1$. The factor $\frac{\hbar}{2}$ is a normalisation, not a prediction: the algebra fixes that spin is two-valued and that its observables close under $SU(2)$, but it does not fix the unit in which spin is measured. The number $\tfrac{1}{2}$ in "spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$" is algebra; the number $\hbar$ is physics inserted from outside.

The Dirac Description of the Electron

The electron's relativistic dynamics is the chirality pair $\tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}=k_{m_e}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}$, $(\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}=k_{m_e}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}$. The plane-wave solutions are computed in the companion solutions article, in the algebra's own objects. They come in two branches at each momentum, the positive-frequency $w^{(r)}(\vec p)\exp\!\big(-\frac{i\,(E\,r'_t/c-\vec p\cdot\vec r)}{\hbar\,c}\big)$ and the negative-frequency $v^{(r)}(\vec p)\exp\!\big(+\frac{i\,(E\,r'_t/c-\vec p\cdot\vec r)}{\hbar\,c}\big)$, with $\vec r = r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z$, $E = +\sqrt{\vec p^2c^2 + m_e^2c^4}$ and $r$ the spin label. Together they span a complex space of dimension four, one pair for each value of the chirality; the dimension two of each pair is the dimension of the irreducible action, and the count is the count of the two values. The spin sums, normalisations, and bilinears are constructed in the companion solutions article and are not repeated here.

A note on the wave biquaternion. The mass-shell condition is written in the corpus convention, in which the four-wavevector has the imaginary time component of the $i r'_t$ convention,

$$ \tilde K_{\Phi} = i\,\frac{\omega}{c}\,e_t + \vec k_\Phi, \qquad \langle\tilde K_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural} = \tilde K_{\Phi}\tilde K_{\Phi}^{\natural} = -\frac{\omega^2}{c^2} + \vec k_\Phi^2 = -k_{m_e}^{2}, $$

with $\vec k_\Phi^2$ the Euclidean square $k_x^2+k_y^2+k_z^2$, which is what the norm of a vector part produces. This is $E^2 = \vec p^2c^2 + m_e^2c^4$ under $E =\hbar\,\omega$, $\vec p =\hbar\,\vec k_\Phi$, and it is the form used by the companion solutions article and by the parent Dirac article. Here $k_{\Phi}=\frac{p}{\hbar}$ is the momentum wave number.

Antiparticle. The two frequency branches are exchanged by charge conjugation, and in the quantized interpretation the negative-frequency branch describes the positron. The framework represents the electron and the positron on the same algebraic footing; which branch is called the electron is fixed by the charge convention of the previous section, not by the algebra.

The One-Sided and the Two-Sided Forms of the Equation

The article carries two Dirac equations, and they must not be read as one. They use the same mass coefficient; they differ in which side of the field the derivative acts and in where the mass sits.

The one-sided equation is the corpus's definition: the chirality pair above. The gradient acts by left multiplication, the field is the algebra element $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}} = \tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}+\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}$, and the mass is the off-diagonal pairing of the two chirality parts,

$$ \tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R} = k_{m_e}\,\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}, \qquad (\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L} = k_{m_e}\,\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R} . $$

This is the form in which the chirality is visible, and it is the form the sections on mass and on the two chirality values use.

The two-sided equation is the dictionary form of V. V. Kravchenko: a single equation on the algebra-valued field $F\in\mathbb{B}$,

$$ \mathcal{N}F = \Big(i\frac{\partial}{\partial r'_t}+\vec\nabla\Big)F - m\,i\,\overline{F}\,\vec e_z = 0, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad -(\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}F - k_m\,i\,\overline{F}\,\vec e_z = 0 $$

with $\vec\nabla = \sum_\eta\vec e_\eta\,\partial/\partial R_\eta$ and the bar the coefficientwise complex conjugation. The time derivative and the spatial gradient act by left multiplication; the mass term acts by right multiplication through $\vec e_z$ and carries the antilinear conjugation. This is the form in which the quaternionic analysis acts — the shifted operator, the Cauchy kernel, and the integral representation of the harmonic field.

Gyromagnetic Ratio: The One Quantitative Number the Equation Predicts

The Dirac description yields one celebrated number. Carrying the biquaternion Dirac equation to the non-relativistic regime — the computation is performed in the companion solutions article and its exercise — eliminates the small part and leaves the Pauli equation with the spin term. That term is the Hermitian element

$$ -\frac{ϙ c1/k_{m_e}}{2}\,i\,\vec B \in \mathbb{B}_{\Psi} . $$

Writing the coupling as $-\vec\mu\cdot\vec B$ identifies $\vec\mu = (ϙ/m_e)\vec S$, with the spin components $\tilde S_{\Psi,\eta} =\frac{\hbar}{2}i\vec e_\eta$, which is $\vec\mu = g\,(ϙ/2m_e)\vec S$ with

$$ g = 2 . $$

The coefficient $1/2m_e$ is produced by the elimination, not put in by hand: this is Dirac's tree-level prediction, here a property of the biquaternionic mass term. For the electron, $ϙ = -e$, so

$$ \boxed{\;\vec\mu_e = -\frac{e}{m_e}\vec S = -g\,\frac{e}{2m_e}\vec S, \qquad g = 2\;}, $$

of magnitude one Bohr magneton $\mu_B = ec\dfrac{1}{2\,k_{m_e}} = 9.274010\times10^{-24}$ J T$^{-1}$ when $\vec S$ is aligned with $\vec B$.

Two qualifications belong with the result. First, the derivation is the standard Dirac one, transcribed; the biquaternion form supplies the compact notation and the sector interpretation of the spin coupling as an $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ observable, not an independent derivation. Second, $g=2$ is not an electron-specific number: it holds for any structureless spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ field, so it distinguishes the electron from a spin-1 particle but not from a muon.

Outside the framework: the anomaly. The measured value is $g/2 = 1.00115965218$, i.e. $a = (g-2)/2 = 1.159652\times10^{-3}$, close to the one-loop Schwinger value $a \approx \alpha/2\pi = 1.161410\times10^{-3}$. This is a radiative correction of quantum electrodynamics; it lies outside the classical equation treated here, and the framework does not contain it. The tree-level $g=2$ is the whole of what the equation supplies.

Zitterbewegung: The Scale of the Mass, Not a Signature of a Sector

The electron is the particle on which the Zitterbewegung was found. In the framework's own terms, the trembling is the interference of the two frequency branches, with angular frequency $2c\,k_{t}^{\prime}$ and amplitude of order $\dfrac{1}{2\,k_{m_e}}$. For the electron at rest these are

$$ \omega_Z = 2c\,k_{m_e} = 1.55269\times10^{21}\ \text{rad s}^{-1}, \qquad \frac{1/k_{m_e}}{2} = 193.1\ \text{fm}, $$

half the reduced Compton wavelength $386.2$ fm. These are the standard values, reconfirmed numerically in the companion article on the Zitterbewegung. Here $k_{t}^{\prime}=E/(\hbar\,c)$ is the energy length.

The framework's temptation is to read the trembling as the observable face of the complexification — positive- and negative-energy branches being the two sectors. The companion article tests that identification and refutes it. The energy sign is the sign of the eigenvalue of a Hamiltonian whose terms multiply the field on the right, through the action $\tilde Q\mapsto i\tilde Q\vec e_x$, and no element of $\mathbb{B}$ does that by left multiplication, while the sectors are the two signs of the star. The two are also splits of different objects: a family of solutions and the values it takes. What survives is a relation of scale and phase: at rest the trembling frequency is exactly twice the rest-energy sector-rotation frequency $ck_{m_e}$, because the trembling phase is the square of the free-evolution phase. For the electron this means that its rest energy sets both the rate at which the sectors rotate into one another and the frequency of the trembling, without the trembling being an informational-sector effect.

The Two Values of the Chirality

The chirality of the electron field is the subscript of the element, and it takes two values. The field is written

$$ \tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}} = \tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L} + \tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}, $$

where $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}$ is the part of value $L$ and $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}$ the part of value $R$; only the zero element carries both. Each part is carried by a complex space of dimension two, and the pair is what the Standard Model calls the left-handed and the right-handed halves of the field.

Two facts about products carry the whole structure, and they are stated in words because that is all they are. Left multiplication keeps the value: for every element $a$, the product $a\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}$ has value $L$ and $a\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}$ has value $R$. A product takes the value of its right factor: when the factor on the right carries a value, so does the product, and it is that value. Two consequences follow at once. The massless pair splits into one equation per value, because each of the two one-sided equations above carries a single value through. And the mass term, which is a left multiplication on the field and an off-diagonal coupling of the two values, is the only term that relates them — the same statement as the chirality-mixing of the mass section, read from the other side.

Derived. The two-value structure is a consequence of the representation: the field is the direct sum of the two inequivalent two-dimensional representations of $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$, and the mass term is the unique Lorentz-invariant coupling between them. For a massless electron the value of the chirality coincides with helicity, so the two branches become the two definite chiralities. This is a genuine structural consequence of the algebra and of the equation.

Represented, not derived: the weak chiral coupling. In the Standard Model the electron couples to the weak interaction through its left-handed part only. The framework contains the decomposition by chirality, so it has the vocabulary for this, but it does not select the coupling: nothing in $\mathbb{B}$ or in the Dirac equation says that one value couples and the other does not. The $V-A$ structure is an input.

Caution: the values of the chirality are not the two sectors. The decomposition by chirality is a decomposition of the element by a subscript; the sector decomposition is a decomposition of the algebra by the star. The Zitterbewegung article reaches the same distinction from the other side when it rejects the identification of the energy branches with $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ and $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ and names the chirality as the candidate for the interference structure. The two decompositions share nothing: a statement about one is not a statement about the other.

The Gaps, Named

Two gaps are structural and cannot be closed by better notation: the mass value and the charge value are inputs, and the anomaly is outside the classical equation. Three further gaps belong to the programme rather than to this article. Quantization has been carried out at the level of the algebra, but the $\mathbb{B}$-intrinsic form — the operator-valued biquaternion field, its Lagrangian, its Fock space, and the embedding of the fermionic $\mathbb{Z}/2$ grading in $\mathbb{B}$ — remains open, so the electron as a quantum field is only partly inside the framework. Empirical contact is absent: nothing here distinguishes the framework from standard relativistic quantum physics for the electron. And the framework is not electron-specific: it represents the electron, the muon, and any other structureless spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ field by the same objects, with no reason in the algebra to prefer one mass or charge over another. These are labelled gaps, not a closed account.

The Legacy Pauli Gamma Formulation

The Pauli and gamma formulation is legacy: the reasons are explained in the article Why AXF Matrices instead of Pauli and Gamma Matrices. The generators carry no frame label: $\gamma_\mu$ is one set of matrices, and the frame is named on the coordinate.

The sections above are written in basis operators; this section writes the same basis operators as gamma matrices, for readers who want the standard notation. The gammas are a frame of $\mathrm{Cl}_{1,3}(\mathbb{C})=M_4(\mathbb{C})$, not a different algebra.

Each gamma is a single basis operator $T_{a,b}=L_aR_b$, a pure tensor, and a general element of $M_4(\mathbb{C})$ is a sum of at most four such basis operators.

$$ \gamma_0=iL_{\mathrm{pau},e_t}R_{\mathrm{pau},\vec e_x},\quad \gamma_1=iL_{\mathrm{pau},\vec e_x}R_{\mathrm{pau},\vec e_y},\quad \gamma_2=iL_{\mathrm{pau},\vec e_y}R_{\mathrm{pau},\vec e_y},\quad \gamma_3=iL_{\mathrm{pau},\vec e_z}R_{\mathrm{pau},\vec e_y},\quad \gamma_5=-iL_{\mathrm{pau},e_t}R_{\mathrm{pau},\vec e_z} . $$

The same in the axis-free display:

$$ \gamma_0=iL_{\mathrm{axf},e_t}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x},\quad \gamma_1=iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y},\quad \gamma_2=iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y},\quad \gamma_3=iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_z}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y},\quad \gamma_5=-iL_{\mathrm{axf},e_t}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_z} . $$

$\gamma_5$ is the basis operator of the chirality operator $\Gamma:\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\mapsto -i\,e_t\,\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\,\vec e_z = -iR_{\mathrm{pau},\vec e_z}$, which acts on the Dirac module. The central grading $\chi=i\tilde{Ζ}_{\mathrm{ad}}$, where $\tilde{Ζ}_{\mathrm{ad}}$ is the central root of $-1$ that complexifies the real subalgebra $\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{B}_{H}$ ($\tilde{Ζ}_{\mathrm{ad}}^2=-1$, $[\tilde{Ζ}_{\mathrm{ad}},i]=0$), is the difference of the two central idempotents of the complexified algebra, so $\xi^2=i^2\tilde{Ζ}_{\mathrm{ad}}^2=(-1)(-1)=e_t$. The grading $\xi$ is central and belongs to the algebra, $\Gamma$ is its representative on the module, and $\gamma_5$ is that representative written as a basis operator.

The dictionary is complex-linear; over $\mathbb{R}$ the products span $\mathrm{Cl}_{1,3}(\mathbb{R})\cong M_2(\mathbb{H})$ and the basis operators span $\mathrm{Cl}_{3,1}(\mathbb{R})\cong M_4(\mathbb{R})$. The four generator basis operators of the older display are these gammas, $\gamma_\mu$, and the charge-conjugation basis operator is $C = -iL_{\mathrm{pau},\vec e_y}R_{\mathrm{pau},\vec e_z} = i\gamma_2\gamma_0$, the antisymmetric matrix with $C^2=-I_4$ that reverses all four gammas under transposition.

The identities the bodies cite. In the $\gamma$ reading, with $\varepsilon=(1,-1,-1,-1)$ the sign vector of the basis operators, $$ \{\gamma_\mu,\gamma_\nu\}=2\varepsilon_\mu\delta_{\mu\nu}I_4,\qquad (\gamma_\eta)^2=-I_4,\qquad \gamma_5^2=I_4,\qquad \{\gamma_\mu,\gamma_5\}=0, $$ $$ [\gamma_\mu,\gamma_\nu]=2\gamma_\mu\gamma_\nu\quad(\mu\ne\nu),\qquad [\gamma_a,S_{cd}]=\varepsilon_a\delta_{ac}\gamma_d-\varepsilon_a\delta_{ad}\gamma_c,\qquad S_{cd}=\tfrac14[\gamma_c,\gamma_d], $$ $$ \mathrm{tr}(\gamma_\mu\gamma_\nu)=4\varepsilon_\mu\delta_{\mu\nu},\qquad \mathrm{tr}(\gamma_5\gamma_\mu\gamma_\nu\gamma_\rho\gamma_\sigma)=-4i\,\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}, $$ $$ \mathrm{tr}(\gamma_\mu\gamma_\nu\gamma_\rho\gamma_\sigma) =4\bigl(\varepsilon_\mu\delta_{\mu\nu}\varepsilon_\rho\delta_{\rho\sigma} -\varepsilon_\mu\delta_{\mu\rho}\varepsilon_\nu\delta_{\nu\sigma} +\varepsilon_\mu\delta_{\mu\sigma}\varepsilon_\nu\delta_{\nu\rho}\bigr). $$ The contraction sums follow from the squares. With one index raised, $\gamma^\mu=-\varepsilon_\mu\gamma_\mu$, so that $\sum_\mu\gamma^\mu\gamma_\mu=-4I_4$, $\sum_\mu\gamma^\mu\gamma_\alpha\gamma_\mu=2\gamma_\alpha$, $\sum_\mu\gamma^\mu\gamma_\alpha\gamma_\beta\gamma_\mu=-4\varepsilon_\alpha\delta_{\alpha\beta}I_4$ and $\sum_\mu\gamma^\mu\gamma_\gamma\gamma_\beta\gamma_\alpha\gamma_\mu=-2\gamma_\gamma\gamma_\beta\gamma_\alpha$.

Summary

The electron is the biquaternion Dirac field $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\in\mathbb{B}$: one element whose two chirality parts satisfy the pair $\tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R} = k_{m_e}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}$, $(\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L} = k_{m_e}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}$. The mass is the coefficient of the off-diagonal coupling of the two values; the algebra's real structure $\flat={}-{}^{*}$ acts on the two sectors with opposite signs; and the rest-energy phase $\exp(-i r'_t/1/k_{m_e}) = \cos(r'_t/1/k_{m_e})e_t - \sin(r'_t/1/k_{m_e})(ie_t)$ rotates the two sectors into one another at $ck_{m_e}$. The charge is inserted at the gradient by minimal coupling, which for the linear mass pair is exactly gauge covariant; the conserved current then lies in $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$, while the static Coulomb field of a point charge is a purely imaginary vector and lies in $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$.

The framework derives the electron's spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ representation content from the unique irreducible action of $\mathbb{B}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$ on a complex space of dimension two, and the spin state and observable structure from the state of $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ with the Born pairing; the magnitude $\frac{\hbar}{2}$ is a normalisation. It derives the tree-level gyromagnetic factor $g=2$ from the $1/2m_e$ coefficient produced by eliminating the small part, giving $\vec\mu_e = -(e/m_e)\vec S$ and one Bohr magneton $\mu_B = ec\dfrac{1}{2\,k_{m_e}} = 9.274010\times10^{-24}$ J T$^{-1}$; the anomalous part $a\approx\alpha/2\pi$ is outside the equation. It reproduces the Zitterbewegung at $\omega_Z = 2ck_{m_e} = 1.55269\times10^{21}$ rad s$^{-1}$ and amplitude $\frac{\hbar}{2\,m_{e}\,c}= 193.1$ fm, and the companion article's test shows the trembling is the second harmonic of the sector rotation, not a signature of either sector. It contains the two values of the chirality and the mass term as the chirality-mixing coupling, but not the weak $V-A$ coupling of the left-handed electron.

The electron's data are not confined to either sector: state-like and field-strength-like data are $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$, current is $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$, the real structure $\flat$ acts on the two sectors with opposite signs while the mass term couples the two chirality values, and the rest-energy phase rotates the sectors into one another. What the framework cannot supply is the electron's mass, its charge, its anomalous moment, its empirical signature, and any reason to single it out among spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ fields. Those are the labelled gaps, and they are not closed by the notation.
