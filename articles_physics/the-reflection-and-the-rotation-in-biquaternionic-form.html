The Reflection and the Rotation in Biquaternionic Form

Introduction

A basis operator multiplies on the left by one element and on the right by another, with the composition that Matrix Representations Conventions fixes. A basis operator is an element of the array algebra that acts on $\mathbb{B}$, so it is at once an object and an operator: an object of the arrays, an operator on the biquaternions.

This article is about a structure of the basis operator algebra which the relativistic articles use and none names: the split between the basis operators that carry an even number of generator factors and those that carry an odd number, and the identification of one basis operator, the frame basis operator $iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}$, as the hinge on which the split turns. The frame basis operators are the four legs of spacetime read as operators, and Matrix Representations Conventions lists them and records that the time one is a single right multiplication.

The claim is short. The even half is the biquaternion algebra itself. Its rotors are the elements of unit norm, the points of $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$, and they act on four-vectors by conjugation. The four generators are odd, none of them is a rotor, and each of them carries an element of one part into the other part. The split is therefore not a bookkeeping device: it is the split between the transformations that turn and those that reflect, and the letter $L$ or $R$ of an element is what an odd basis operator exchanges and an even basis operator keeps.

The article runs as follows. §One Operator, Two Readings separates the basis operator read as a point from the basis operator read as an operator. §The Two Halves by Reflection Count states the split and ties it to the parity of the number of reflections. §The Frame Operator and the Hermitian Sector shows that the sign vector of the basis operators is the biquaternion norm of the Hermitian sector, which is what makes the mostly-minus metric of the series a statement about the objects rather than a choice. §Conjugation by the Frame Operator Is the Bar shows that conjugation by the frame basis operator is the conjugation of the coefficients. §The Spaces of Rotations and of Reflections and §How Many Reflections? describe the two sets and count the reflections a transformation needs. §The Two Parts and the Reflection draws the consequence for the letter, and §Why Three Generators Cannot Carry the Letter shows that the timelike generator is what makes the letter exist.

The conventions are the series'. The algebra, the units, the sectors and the metric are those of Algebra and Element Conventions and Frame and Metric Conventions; the basis operators and their multiplication are those of Matrix Representations Conventions, and the comparison of the biquaternion algebra with the even half is the dictionary of the same article. None of them is restated here. A basis operator enters by its two multipliers, and the short names used later — the frame basis operator, the chirality basis operator, $\omega$, the three $\eta$ factors — are bound to those multipliers where they are introduced; no basis operator is named without its product being written at least once. The derivatives are the series' gradient, written once here and never as a basis operator, $$ \tilde\nabla=\sum_{\mu\in\{t,x,y,z\}}e_\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial R_\mu},\qquad \tilde\nabla^{\natural}=\sum_{\mu\in\{t,x,y,z\}}\varepsilon_\mu e_\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial R_\mu},\qquad \Box_\Phi=\tilde\nabla_\Phi(\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}=\frac{\partial^2}{\partial R_t^2}+\Delta_Q, $$ with $\varepsilon=(1,-1,-1,-1)$, and on the material sector $\partial/\partial R_t=-i\,\partial/\partial r'_t$, so that there $\Box_\Phi=\Delta_\Phi-\partial^2/\partial {r'_t}^2$. No basis operator of this article carries a derivative.

One Operator, Two Readings

A basis operator of a matrix algebra is at once a point and an operator, and the quaternions show the doubling most plainly: a unit quaternion is a point of the three-sphere and it is also the rotation that acts on vectors by conjugation. The same element, read two ways.

The rotors. A Lorentz rotor $\tilde U_{\mathrm{lor}}$ of unit norm, $\langle\tilde U_{\mathrm{lor}}\rangle_{\natural}=1$, is a point of $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$, the double cover of the restricted Lorentz group, and it is an operator on the algebra, $$ Q_2 = \tilde U_{\mathrm{lor}}\,Q\,\tilde U_{\mathrm{lor}}{}^{*}, $$ which is the Lorentz transformation of the material element $Q\in\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$. For a pure boost the rotor is Hermitian, $\tilde U_{\Psi,\mathrm b}^{*}=\tilde U_{\Psi,\mathrm b}$, and the action collapses to $Q_2=\tilde U_{\Psi,\mathrm b}Q\tilde U_{\Psi,\mathrm b}$; a general element of $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$ needs the general form. Both facts are those of The Lorentz Transformation as a Biquaternionic Rotation.

The generators. A generator basis operator is a vector and an operator at once: one of the four legs of spacetime, read as a basis operator. The four are $$ iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x},\qquad iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y},\qquad iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y},\qquad iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_z}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y} $$

For the rotors the doubling is familiar, since the same element is the transformation and the operator that applies it. For the generators it is not familiar, and the reason is the parity of the number of generator factors. An even basis operator, applied on the left, keeps the letter of the element; a generator exchanges it. So two operators of one algebra, both acting on the same elements, do qualitatively different things, and the remainder of the article is the elaboration of that sentence.

The odd action. An odd element is a reflection, and it acts on the vector generators twice-sidedly, $$ \rho(u) : x \mapsto -\,\epsilon(u)\,u\,x\,u, \qquad u\ \text{odd},\quad u^2 = \epsilon(u)\,I_4,\quad \epsilon(u)=\pm1 . $$ A unit vector is its own inverse up to sign: $u^2=\epsilon(u)I_4$ makes the inverse $\epsilon(u)u$, so the formula needs the basis operator product and the sign of the normal and nothing else. The minus sign is what makes the map a reflection: $x\mapsto u\,x\,u^{-1}$ already fixes the direction of $u$ and negates the vectors orthogonal to it, so its negative fixes the hyperplane orthogonal to $u$ and reverses the normal. The sign $\epsilon(u)$ records whether the normal is timelike, $\epsilon=+1$, or spacelike, $\epsilon=-1$.

The Two Halves by Reflection Count

The theorem of Cartan and Dieudonné states that every orthogonal transformation of a non-degenerate space is a product of reflections in hyperplanes. In dimension $n$ at most $n$ of them are needed, and the determinant of the transformation is $(-1)^k$, where $k$ is the number of reflections in any factorisation. Two remarks follow, and together they are the content of this section.

First, the parity of the count is the determinant. An even number of reflections gives a proper transformation, an odd number an orientation-reversing one. In four dimensions with the Lorentzian signature a single reflection is parity, time reversal or an axis flip, and two reflections give a proper transformation whose time orientation depends on the normals: it is orthochronous when both normals are timelike or both spacelike, and it reverses time when one is timelike and the other spacelike, as $\mathrm{diag}(-1,-1,+1,+1)$ does for the normals $e_t$ and $\vec e_x$.

Second, and this is the point, the parity of the count is the split of the basis operators. Every basis operator carries a right multiplier, and the sixteen basis operators separate by it: eight have right multiplier $e_t$ or $\vec e_z$ and eight have $\vec e_x$ or $\vec e_y$. The first eight span a closed subalgebra; the second eight span a subspace of the same dimension that is not closed. A generator has a right multiplier $\vec e_x$ or $\vec e_y$, and multiplication by either of the two exchanges the two spans, since the right multiplier of a product is the product of the two multipliers in the reverse order, and $e_t\vec e_x=\vec e_x$, $\vec e_z\vec e_x=\vec e_y$, $e_t\vec e_y=\vec e_y$, $\vec e_z\vec e_y=-\vec e_x$. So a product of an even number of generators lies in the first eight and a product of an odd number in the second.

The first eight are exactly the image of the biquaternion algebra under the comparison of Matrix Representations Conventions. That comparison is $\mathbb{R}$-linear and not $\mathbb{C}$-linear: a factor $i$ multiplying a biquaternion becomes a right multiplication, not a multiplication of the basis operator by the scalar $i$. Everything the comparison produces is even, so the rotations sit inside $\mathbb{B}$ and the reflections do not.

The correspondence between the parity and the determinant is exact, and not a counting resemblance:

element of $\mathbb{B}$ or basis operator generator factors determinant of the action on vectors in $\mathbb{B}$?
a rotor $\tilde U_{\mathrm{lor}}$ of unit norm an even number $+1$ yes
an odd element: a generator, a unit vector, a trivector an odd number $-1$ no

The Lie algebra of the rotations is generated by the bivectors, and a bivector is the product of two reflections. So the two halves of the algebra of the basis operators are the two classes of orthogonal transformations, and the correspondence is an identity.

The Frame Operator and the Hermitian Sector

The odd half is reached from the even half by multiplying by the time generator, which is the frame basis operator $iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}$: that basis operator times the image of $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ is the vector space and times the image of $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ the trivector space, as Matrix Representations Conventions records. The four generators are therefore the Hermitian basis read through the frame basis operator, and a statement about the generators is a statement about that basis.

Two derived forms are used below, both read off the frame basis operators at the place of use. A vector basis operator is the frame basis operator applied after the image of $w\in\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$, $$ w = q_t e_t + i q_x\vec e_x + i q_y\vec e_y + i q_z\vec e_z , \qquad iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}\times(\text{image of } w) = q_t\, iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x} + \sum_\eta q_\eta\, iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_\eta}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y} , $$ and a trivector is the frame basis operator applied after the image of an element of $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$, up to a sign that depends on the orientation chosen for each trivector.

The decisive consequence concerns the metric. The biquaternion norm is the sum of the four squared coefficients, and a coefficient $i$ contributes $i^2=-1$. On the Hermitian basis $(e_t,i\vec e_x,i\vec e_y,i\vec e_z)$ it therefore takes the four values $(+1,-1,-1,-1)$, which is the plain weight $\varepsilon$; on the material basis $(ie_t,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z)$ it takes $(-1,+1,+1,+1)$, which is the interval of the material sector. The minus signs of the first list are those of $i^2$ on the three spatial directions, and nothing else. The sign vector of the generators is therefore not an independent convention laid on top of the algebra: it is the biquaternion norm of the Hermitian sector, and the interval of the material sector is its negative. The material basis carries $(-1,+1,+1,+1)$ and the generators, which are Hermitian objects, carry the weight $\varepsilon$, which is its negative; the two differ by the sign that distinguishes the two sectors, and neither could consistently be anything else.

The same fact appears in the square of a vector. For $w$ as above, $$ \Bigl(q_t\, iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x} + \sum_\eta q_\eta\, iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_\eta}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}\Bigr)^2 = \bigl(q_t^2 - q_x^2 - q_y^2 - q_z^2\bigr)\,I_4 , $$ with no relative sign between the square of the basis operator and the biquaternion norm. The absence of that sign is the signature showing through.

Conjugation by the Frame Operator Is the Bar

The frame basis operator is not a bookkeeping element. It has a definite action on the algebra, and that action is what defines the split $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{B}_{H}\oplus \mathbb{B}_{iH}$.

For every element $\tilde Q$, $$ \boxed{\;iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}\times\bigl(\text{image of }\tilde{Q}\bigr)\times iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x} = \text{image of }\bar{\tilde{Q}}\;} $$ where $\bar{\tilde Q}$ is the conjugation of the coefficients, $i\mapsto -i$, with the units $e_t,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z$ fixed. That map fixes $\mathbb{B}_{H}$ and negates $\mathbb{B}_{iH}$, so the identity says that conjugation by the frame basis operator is the bar: it is the spacetime-side avatar of the split into the quaternion sector and its imaginary multiple. It preserves each of the two sectors, because it commutes with the Hermitian conjugation whose fixed spaces they are; the sector exchange is multiplication by $i$, not a conjugation.

Among the four generators, the frame basis operator is unique in being the bar itself. Conjugation by any of the four reverses the imaginary basis operator, since the image of $i$ is $L_{\mathrm{axf},e_t}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_z}$ and every generator anticommutes with it, so all four carry the scalar imaginary $i$ to $-i$ and act on the biquaternions as conjugate-linear maps rather than $\mathbb{C}$-linear ones. Only the time one leaves the quaternion units alone. Conjugation by a spatial generator is the bar composed with a sign change of two units: it fixes $\vec e_\eta$ and negates the two units $\vec e_j$ with $j\neq k$, and the bar then flips the imaginary multiples the other way round. The spatial generators reflect the quaternion units; the time one conjugates them without a reflection.

The frame basis operator completes the reading of the three conjugations of $\mathbb{B}$, and the three can be written together. Reversal, $\mathrm{rev}$, is the anti-automorphism that reverses the order of the factors in a product: on an array it is the transpose, and on the biquaternions it is quaternion conjugation. The image does not carry one of the two into the other, because it sends the scalar $i$ to a right multiplication rather than to a scalar, and the frame basis operator is exactly the difference. Of the three conjugations, the bar and the quaternion conjugation each carry one factor of the frame, and the composite carries none: $$ \text{image of }\bar{\tilde Q}=iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}\times\bigl(\text{image of }\tilde{Q}\bigr)\times iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}, $$ $$ \text{image of }\tilde{Q}^{\natural}=iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}\times\mathrm{rev}\bigl(\text{image of }\tilde{Q}\bigr)\times iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}, \qquad \boxed{\;\text{image of }\tilde{Q}^{*}=\mathrm{rev}\bigl(\text{image of }\tilde{Q}\bigr)\;} $$ The Hermitian conjugation is therefore the bar composed with the quaternion conjugation, which is the algebra's own identity and not an accident of the frame; and in the array the two frame factors cancel, because the square of the frame basis operator is $I_4$.

That cancellation is the point, and it bears on the mass. The real structure of the algebra is $\flat=-{}^{*}$, and it is the bare reversal: no frame enters it. The frame sits one level up, in the two factors of the Hermitian conjugation — the bar is a conjugation by the frame basis operator, and the quaternion conjugation is that same conjugation composed with the bare reversal — and it is because the two factors of the frame cancel that a formulation written in terms of $\flat$ alone never shows it. The linear mass term of The Dirac Equation in Biquaternionic Form displays it openly instead. This is an observation about how the frame is packaged, not a derivation; it is stated because it is the reason the frame is easy to overlook.

The Spaces of Rotations and of Reflections

With the split in hand, the two sets can be described, and they are not of the same kind.

The rotations. The rotors are the unit-norm biquaternions, $$ SL(2,\mathbb{C}) = \{\tilde U_{\mathrm{lor}}\in\mathbb{B} : \langle\tilde U_{\mathrm{lor}}\rangle_{\natural} = 1\}, $$ a group of real dimension six, the double cover of the restricted Lorentz group. The sign of the norm is the time orientation: an even unit element with $\langle\tilde U\rangle_{\natural}=+1$ is orthochronous, and one with $\langle\tilde U\rangle_{\natural}=-1$ reverses time, which is why the group defined here is the restricted one. Its Lie algebra is spanned by the six bivectors, the images of the six directions $\vec e_\eta$ and $i\vec e_\eta$, $\eta = 1, 2, 3$. This is the space in which the rotors of the relativity articles live and act: on four-vectors in Relativistic Mechanics in Biquaternionic Form and Exercise: Boosting a Four-Velocity and Rapidity Composition, on the elements in The Spinor Module in Biquaternionic Form and Its Lorentz Action, on states in Quantum Physics in Biquaternionic Form, and in the composition of rotations of Exercise: The Thomas Precession.

Two subfamilies are worth separating, because the series uses them differently:

subfamily condition biquaternion form character
spatial rotations $\tilde u_{\mathrm r}$ a real quaternion $\cos\frac{\theta}{2} + \sin\frac{\theta}{2}\vec u_{j\eta}$ the group $SU(2)$
pure boosts $\tilde U_{\Psi,\mathrm b}$ Hermitian, $\tilde U_{\Psi,\mathrm b}^{*} = \tilde U_{\Psi,\mathrm b}$ $\cosh\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}}{2} + i\sinh\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}}{2}\vec u$ a symmetric submanifold, not a group

The boosts do not close under multiplication — the product of two non-collinear boosts is a boost and a Thomas–Wigner rotation — so they form a submanifold of $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$ rather than a subgroup. The two families are the exponentials of the two sector algebras: the boosts of elements of $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$, namely the timelike bivectors $i\vec u$, and the spatial rotations of elements of $\mathbb{B}_{H}$, namely the spacelike bivectors $\vec u_{j\eta}$.

The reflections. A reflection is an odd basis operator of unit norm, and its data is its normal, a unit vector. Writing the vectors as the frame basis operator applied after the image of $w$ with $w\in\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$, the unit normals are those $w$ of norm $+1$ or $-1$, the timelike ones for $\langle w\rangle_{\natural}=+1$ and the spacelike ones for $\langle w\rangle_{\natural}=-1$. The canonical reflection is the frame basis operator itself, and its normal is the time axis: the vector basis operator $iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}$ is the frame basis operator applied to the image of $e_t$, which is the identity basis operator $L_{\mathrm{axf},e_t}R_{\mathrm{axf},e_t}$.

The reflections do not form a group, and this is the sharp difference from the rotations. The product of two reflections is even, a proper transformation, so the set is not closed. The reflections lie in a single coset of the rotation group and split into two orbits, one of timelike normals and one of spacelike normals; a product of one of each is even with norm $-1$, which is the component that reverses time. Their dimension is three, the dimension of the unit quadric, against the six of the rotations. The two sets together are $\mathrm{Pin}(1,3)$, whose even part is $\mathrm{Spin}(1,3)=SL(2,\mathbb{C})$. The relation between the two sets is generation: the rotations are the products of an even number of reflections, and the bivectors that generate the Lorentz algebra are the products of two.

The reflections account for the four components of the full Lorentz group, and the timelike and the trivector normals give the two classical ones. The action of the frame basis operator, whose normal is timelike, is time reversal, $$ \rho(iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}) : x \mapsto -\,iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}\,x\,iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x} = \mathrm{diag}(-1,+1,+1,+1). $$ Parity is not that map but the action of the trivector in the direction $ie_t$, the product of the three spatial generators, $$ u = iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}\,iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}\,iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_z}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}, \qquad u^2=I_4, \qquad \rho(u) : x \mapsto -\,u\,x\,u = \mathrm{diag}(+1,-1,-1,-1). $$ Both are reflections, with determinant $-1$; the identity component is the even half, and the discrete symmetries are the reflections.

The anti-linear case. The rule just stated — even basis operators act with determinant $+1$, odd ones with $-1$ — holds for the linear action. The discrete operations of the Dirac field are not all linear: $C$ and $T$ are anti-linear, and each carries an internal factor. The CPT Theorem in Biquaternionic Form fixes the three, $$ \eta_C=-L_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y},\qquad \eta_P=iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x},\qquad \eta_T=-L_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y} , $$ acting as $C[\tilde{Ξ}]=\eta_C\tilde{Ξ}^*$, $P[\tilde{Ξ}]=\eta_P\tilde{Ξ}(-\vec r)$ and $T[\tilde{Ξ}]=\eta_T\tilde{Ξ}^*(-r'_t)$, with $\tilde{Ξ}^*$ the Hermitian conjugate. The internal factor does not decide the determinant by itself:

operation internal factor its generator factors coordinates reversed determinant induced map
$P$ $\eta_P=iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}$, the frame basis operator odd $\vec r$ $-1$ reflection
$T$ $\eta_T=-L_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}$ even $r'_t$ $-1$ reflection
$C$ $\eta_C=-L_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}$ odd none $+1$ proper

$P$ and $T$ each reverse one coordinate and are reflections; $C$ reverses none and is proper. What the parity of the factor decides is not the determinant but the letter, since the letter is read from the chirality basis operator: the odd factors $\eta_C$ and $\eta_P$ exchange the two parts, while the even factor $\eta_T$ keeps them. The composite $CPT$ carries an even factor and preserves the letter, which is the statement of the CPT article.

A single rule reproduces the determinant column: the parity of the factor contributes $(-1)^{k}$, with $k$ its number of generator factors, and the anti-linearity another $-1$, $$ \det = (-1)^{k}\,(-1)^{\text{anti}} . $$ It is a bookkeeping statement of an inversion, not a derivation. Its content is negative and worth stating plainly: for a linear map the parity is the reflection count, and for an anti-linear one it is not — the two columns are offset by one, and the even factor $\eta_T$ is the reflection while the odd factor $\eta_C$ is not.

How Many Reflections?

The theorem gives an upper bound of four reflections in four dimensions. The sharper question — given a rotation, how many reflections does it take — has a clean answer, and it separates the simple Lorentz transformations from the generic ones.

A product of two reflections is a basis operator $uv$, with $u$ and $v$ unit vector basis operators. It is the product of two odd basis operators, hence even, hence the image of an element $\tilde U_{\mathrm{lor}}$; from here the image of an element is written as the element, and a product of a basis operator with it is the basis operator product. The criterion is algebraic. The product $u\tilde U_{\mathrm{lor}}$ has a vector part and a trivector part, and the trivector part is linear in $u$. So the condition that $u\tilde U_{\mathrm{lor}}$ be a vector — that is, that $\tilde U_{\mathrm{lor}}$ be $u$ times a vector — is a system of four linear conditions on the four components of $u$, and it has a nonzero solution exactly when their determinant vanishes: $$ \tilde U_{\mathrm{lor}} = uv \ \text{for unit vectors}\ u,v \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u\tilde U_{\mathrm{lor}}\ \text{is a vector for some}\ u\neq 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{the four conditions are dependent}. $$ Geometrically the criterion is that $\tilde U_{\mathrm{lor}}$ fixes a two-dimensional subspace pointwise, since a product of two reflections is the identity on the plane orthogonal to the span of $u$ and $v$. In the basis operators the same determinant vanishes exactly when the bivector part of $\tilde U_{\mathrm{lor}}$ is the product of two vectors, and the solutions $u$ are then the vectors of the plane they span; a plane in Lorentzian four-space always contains a non-null vector, so a nonzero solution can be rescaled to a unit normal. The three forms of the criterion agree: the determinant vanishes exactly when the fixed subspace has dimension two.

transformation the four conditions fixed subspace reflections needed
pure boost dependent dimension two two
pure spatial rotation dependent dimension two two
boost along $\vec e_x$ with a rotation in the $xy$ plane dependent dimension two two
boost along $\vec e_x$ with a rotation about $\vec e_x$ independent dimension zero four

The rule the table obeys is geometric: the transformation is simple exactly when the boost plane and the rotation plane share a direction. A pure boost or a pure rotation carries one plane, so the condition is empty; a boost with a rotation in the plane of the boost shares the boost direction; a boost with a rotation about the boost axis shares nothing.

The factorisations are explicit. The image of a boost is the frame basis operator times a unit vector, $$ \text{image of }\tilde U_{\Psi,\mathrm b} = iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}\,v , $$ where $v$ is the unit vector basis operator whose coordinates in the generator basis $(iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x},iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y},iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y},iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_z}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y})$ are $$ \Bigl(\cosh\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}}{2},\ \sinh\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}}{2}u_x,\ \sinh\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}}{2}u_y,\ \sinh\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}}{2}u_z\Bigr), $$ the time axis boosted by half the rapidity. The boost is therefore the reflection in the resting time axis composed with the reflection in the moving one, and the half is what makes the two rapidities add to $\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}$.

A spatial rotation in the plane spanned by two spatial generators is $iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_j}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}\,v$ with the vector basis operator $$ v = -\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\,iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_j}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y} + \sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_\eta}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y} , \qquad v^2 = -I_4 , $$ so that $$ iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_j}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}\,v = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\,I_4 + \sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_j}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}\,iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_\eta}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y} . $$ For the plane $(x,y)$ the right-hand side is the image of $\cos\frac{\theta}{2}e_t+\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\vec e_z$, the rotation about $\vec e_z$, and the other coordinate planes follow by relabelling. A generic Lorentz transformation fixes no plane at all, so it needs four reflections, the maximum.

The answer to the question is therefore: pure boosts and pure spatial rotations are products of two reflections, and so is a boost with a rotation in the plane of the boost; a generic Lorentz transformation is not, and needs four. The two-reflection class is a proper submanifold of $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$, cut out by the vanishing of that determinant. The product of two non-collinear boosts stays inside it — the Thomas–Wigner rotation of a composed boost lies in the plane of the two boosts — so what leaves the class is a boost combined with a rotation about the boost axis, that is, a boost and a rotation in orthogonal planes.

The Two Parts and the Reflection

The basis operator that separates the two parts of an element is one such basis operator, a right multiplier and the product of the four generators, $$ -iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_z} = i\,\omega , \qquad \omega = iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}\,iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}\,iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}\,iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_z}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y} = -L_{\mathrm{axf},e_t}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_z} , $$ where the four scalar factors of $\omega$ multiply to $i^4=1$, so that $\omega$ is a pure product of basis operators. That product is the pseudoscalar of the frame, $\omega^2=-I_4$, and the chirality basis operator is a single right multiplication whose square is $I_4$.

The letter. An element is $L$ when the chirality basis operator gives it the value $-1$, and $R$ when it gives $+1$. That is the whole definition. The two values are exactly two, and only the zero element is both. The letter belongs to the object and not to a state, so a product, a unit and a field carry it as well, and the two parts of a field are written $\tilde{Ξ}_{L}$ and $\tilde{Ξ}_{R}$.

Which basis operators keep it and which exchange it. A basis operator acts through its right multiplier: a product takes the letter of its right factor, and left multiplication keeps the letter of its operand. Every even basis operator has right multiplier $e_t$ or $\vec e_z$ and keeps the letter. Every generator has right multiplier $\vec e_x$ or $\vec e_y$ and exchanges it, so a generator carries the $R$ part into the $L$ part and the $L$ part into the $R$ part.

Combined with the split established above, the statement is: rotations keep the letter and reflections exchange it. A rotation cannot change which part an element belongs to, and a reflection always does. The letter is not an extra label attached to the element; it is the value of the basis operator that tells the two parts apart, and only the odd half moves it.

The mass is where the two parts are coupled, and the coupling is the chiral pair of The Dirac Equation in Biquaternionic Form, $$ \tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde{Ξ}_{R} = k_m\tilde{Ξ}_{L} , \qquad (\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{L} = k_m\tilde{Ξ}_{R} , $$ in which every derivative is the gradient and acts by left multiplication. The pair stays two lines and is never written as one operator on the direct sum $\mathbb{B}\oplus\mathbb{B}$. Each line is off-diagonal in the two slots: the first slot of the output is filled from the second slot of the input and the second from the first, and no slot is filled from itself. The subscripts name the two slots, not a value that the operator computes: the gradient acts by left multiplication, a left multiplier does not touch the letter, and the term that carries a field from one letter to the other is therefore the mass, which is the only such term. The mass is a multiple of the identity on the sum, central and even, and it moves nothing by itself. The composition of the two lines is scalar because each is a gradient followed by the conjugate of the other, $$ \tilde\nabla_\Phi(\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{L}=\Box_\Phi\,\tilde{Ξ}_{L}, \qquad (\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde{Ξ}_{R}=\Box_\Phi\,\tilde{Ξ}_{R}, $$ with $\Box_\Phi=\Delta_\Phi-\partial^2/\partial {r'_t}^2$ on the material sector, so that eliminating one part returns $\Box\tilde{Ξ}=k_m^{2}\tilde{Ξ}$, the Klein–Gordon equation exactly, with no overall sign left to fix. Here $k_m=mc/\hbar$ is the mass wave number, the inverse of the mass length.

The consequence is immediate. Set $\tilde{Ξ}_{R}=0$ in a massive field: the first member reads $0=k_m\tilde{Ξ}_{L}$, so $\tilde{Ξ}_{L}=0$ as well, and a massive field carries both parts. A field of one letter alone is not a solution. At $m=0$ the pair decouples into $\tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde{Ξ}_{R}=0$ and $(\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{L}=0$, one member for each part, so a field of one part alone is allowed. That decoupling is the operator-level content of the statement that the letter and the helicity agree only in the massless limit.

The representation-theoretic form of the same fact is the split of the complexified Lorentz algebra into two copies of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})$, each of them the complexification of an $\mathfrak{su}(2)$, $$ \mathfrak{so}(1,3)_{\mathbb{C}} \cong \mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})\oplus\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C}), $$ under which a finite-dimensional representation is labelled by a pair $(j_1,j_2)$. The rotations and the boosts act within each copy, and the letter is which copy:

object label letter
a scalar $(0,0)$ in neither copy; no letter exists
$\tilde{Ξ}_{L}$ $(\tfrac12,0)$ in the first copy alone
$\tilde{Ξ}_{R}$ $(0,\tfrac12)$ in the second copy alone
a vector $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ in both copies equally

This is the precise sense in which the letter is tied to spin $\tfrac12$: the two parts are the two smallest nontrivial representations of the two copies, and $L$ means $(\tfrac12,0)$. A scalar has no letter because it sits in neither copy, and a vector is blind to it because it sits in both equally. The link is not a coincidence to be explained: each part is a minimal representation of one copy, and the basis operator is what tells the two copies apart.

The exchange of the two copies is an orientation-reversing operation, which is why it takes a reflection. Parity is exactly that reflection. It fixes the time direction and reverses the three spatial ones, and it exchanges the two parts, $$ \mathsf{P} : (\tfrac12,0) \longleftrightarrow (0,\tfrac12). $$ It is odd in both readings: the trivector $iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}\,iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}\,iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_z}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}$ for the geometric reflection, the internal factor $\eta_P=iR_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}$ for the parity of the Dirac field. Every odd basis operator carries the $R$ part into the $L$ part, so the exchange of the parts is the parity.

Parity violation — the statement that the weak interaction couples to one part and not the other — is therefore not an accidental property of the Standard Model but the statement that the interaction distinguishes the two copies of the complexified Lorentz algebra. This is what Chiral Fermions in the Biquaternion Framework and The Neutrino and Majorana Fermions in Biquaternionic Form develop on the physical side.

Why Three Generators Cannot Carry the Letter

The non-relativistic limit keeps three directions and drops the time generator, and it is natural to ask whether the letter could have been defined there. It could not, and the reason is algebraic and exact.

Take the algebra generated by the three spatial generators, $iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}$, $iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}$, $iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_z}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}$. Their product is odd, it squares to $+I_4$, and it commutes with all three: $$ \bigl(iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_x}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}\;iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}\;iL_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_z}R_{\mathrm{axf},\vec e_y}\bigr)^2 = I_4 . $$ A basis operator that commutes with the three generators multiplies each of the two irreducible pieces they act on by a scalar, so it separates the elements into two halves that the three generators never mix. That is a copy index of the algebra, and not the letter: the letter must be a value that the odd basis operators exchange, and a basis operator that commutes with them cannot supply one.

The other half of the argument is the same statement read backwards. A basis operator that all three generators exchange must anticommute with all three, and the algebra of the three contains no such basis operator: its even part is spanned by the identity and the three products of two generators, a product of two anticommutes with the two it carries and commutes with the third, so a combination that anticommuted with all three would have to vanish. The odd part contains the triple product, which commutes. There is nowhere to put the letter.

The fourth generator is what supplies it. The chirality basis operator is the product of all four generators, and it is even. The timelike direction is therefore not an optional extra; it is the ingredient that makes the two parts exist. The two values are intrinsically relativistic: four generators carry them, three cannot, and the non-relativistic limit keeps only the spin.

Summary

The claims of this article, in order.

  1. One algebra, two readings. A basis operator is an object of the arrays and an operator on the algebra. The rotors are the even basis operators; the four frame basis operators are the odd ones. An even basis operator keeps the letter of the element it acts on, an odd basis operator exchanges it.

  2. The parity is the reflection count. By Cartan–Dieudonné an orthogonal transformation is a product of reflections and its determinant is $(-1)^{\text{count}}$. The split of the basis operators by their right multiplier — eight with $e_t$ or $\vec e_z$, eight with $\vec e_x$ or $\vec e_y$ — is that parity, and the first eight are the image of $\mathbb{B}$.

  3. The frame and the signs. Every vector is the frame basis operator applied after the image of $w\in\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$. The biquaternion norm on the Hermitian basis is $(+1,-1,-1,-1)$, its minus signs being those of $i^2$, and on the material basis it is $(-1,+1,+1,+1)$. The sign vector of the generators is the biquaternion norm of the objects they represent, so the mostly-minus convention is forced rather than chosen.

  4. Conjugation by the frame basis operator is the bar. The frame basis operator is the bar, and the bar composed with the reversal is the quaternion conjugation. The two frame factors cancel in the Hermitian conjugation, so the real structure $\flat=-{}^{*}$ is the bare reversal and carries no frame.

  5. Rotations and reflections have different spaces. The rotations are the unit-norm biquaternions, a group of dimension six with the bivectors as Lie algebra. The reflections are the odd elements, two orbits of dimension three organised by their normals, and not a group. Together they are $\mathrm{Pin}(1,3)$. Anti-linear maps shift the correspondence by one: an even internal factor belongs to a reflection and an odd one to a proper transformation, which is how $T$ is a reflection while $C$ is not.

  6. Two reflections, or four. A rotation is a product of two reflections exactly when it fixes a plane. Pure boosts, pure spatial rotations and boosts with a rotation in the plane of the boost qualify; a boost with a rotation about the boost axis does not, and needs four.

  7. The letter is what the reflection changes. Even basis operators keep the letter, odd basis operators exchange it, so rotations preserve the two parts and reflections reverse them. The mass is even and central and couples the two parts without moving either; each line of the pair is off-diagonal in the two slots, and the mass is the one term that carries a field from one letter to the other. In the split $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)_{\mathbb{C}}\cong\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})\oplus\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})$ the letter is which copy, and the exchange of the copies is parity.

  8. The letter is relativistic. Three generators cannot carry it: their product is central, and no basis operator of their algebra anticommutes with all three. The chirality basis operator is the product of all four generators and is even, so it exists only because one generator is timelike.
