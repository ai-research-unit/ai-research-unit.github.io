The Relativistic Particle and the Lagrangian: The Stationary-Action Principle

Introduction

The equations of the relativistic framework can be reached in two ways. They can be written down, as the operator article writes the d'Alembertian and the spin articles write their wave equations, or they can be derived from a single scalar functional by the requirement that it be stationary. This article takes the second route: it fixes the biquaternionic action, states the stationary-action principle in the form the algebra requires, and derives from it the free-particle worldline, the coupling to an external potential, the second-order field equation, and the Maxwell and first-order actions. The result is the variational counterpart of the operator article: where that article establishes the operator and its kernels, this one shows that the operator is what stationarity produces.

Two features of the framework shape the principle. First, an action must be a real scalar, and a biquaternion-valued Lagrangian is not a scalar until it is paired. The pairings available are the ones the involution lattice supplies — the real part of the scalar part, $\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Sc}$, for a bilinear invariant, and the Hermitian form $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\tilde{Ξ})$ for a complex field — and the choice of pairing is the choice of which equation the variation returns. Second, the free-particle action is built from the biquaternion norm, $S = -mc\int\sqrt{-\langle d\tilde R_\Phi\rangle_{\natural}}$, so that the invariant interval, the four-velocity normalisation, and the mass shell are all statements about $\langle\cdot\rangle_{\natural}$. The field action is built from the same biquaternion norm with the gradient inserted, $\mathrm{Sc}[((\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}^{*})(\tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde{Ξ})]$, and this is why its Euler–Lagrange equation is the d'Alembertian equation of the operator article. The variational and the operator routes meet at the biquaternion norm.

The scope is relativistic. The non-relativistic action belongs to the non-relativistic theory; the classical relativistic particle, its light cone, and the Lorentz group as biquaternion-norm automorphisms belong to the non-quantum relativistic theory; and the spin-specific equations belong to the spin articles. What is established here is the common functional apparatus: the free-particle action and its coupling, the real field Lagrangian, the biquaternionic Euler–Lagrange equation, and the symmetry statements. The conserved current and the stress–energy tensor that follow from the symmetries are developed in the companion article on Noether's theorem, which this article supplies the action for.

Conventions

The algebra. $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$, with $e_t=1$ and $\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z$ obeying $\vec e_{\imath}^{2}=-e_t$ and $\vec e_x\vec e_y=\vec e_z$ cyclically. An element is $\tilde Q=Q_te_t+\vec Q$ with four complex coefficients, and $\mathrm{Sc}\,\tilde Q=Q_t$ is the scalar part, one half the trace in the $2\times2$ image. Real and imaginary parts of a complex number are written $\mathrm{Re}$ and $\mathrm{Im}$, and a modulus is $\lvert\cdot\rvert$.

The sectors. $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ is the $\flat$-fixed space: the material sector, which holds the four-vectors, $\tilde R_\Phi=i r'_t\,e_t+\vec r$ with $r'_t$ and $\vec r$ real. $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ is the $*$-fixed space: the informational sector, which holds the observables and the states. The centre is $\mathbb{B}_{C}=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{e_t,ie_t\}$, of complex dimension one; a field in it is written $\tilde\Xi=\xi\,e_t$ with $\xi$ a complex function.

The temporal coordinate is imaginary, $R_t=ir'_t$, so $$ \frac{\partial}{\partial R_t}=-i\frac{\partial}{\partial r'_t} , $$ and this single factor is the source of every sign in the article that is not a sign of the algebra. The gradient is $\tilde\nabla_\Phi=\sum_{\imath\in\{t,x,y,z\}}e_{\imath}\,\partial/\partial R_{\imath}$ with $e_t$ for the temporal index, its quaternion conjugate is $(\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}$, and $$ \Box_\Phi=\tilde\nabla_\Phi(\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}=\Delta_\Phi-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial {r'}_t^{2}} , \qquad \Delta_\Phi=\sum_{\imath\in\{x,y,z\}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial R_{\imath}^{2}} . $$

The Stationary-Action Principle

The statement

An action is a real functional of the fields, $$ S[\tilde{Ξ}] = \int \mathcal{L}\bigl(\tilde{Ξ}, \partial/\partial R_{\imath}\tilde{Ξ}\bigr)\,d^4r , $$ and the principle is that the physical field is a stationary point, $$ \delta S = 0 \qquad\text{for every variation } \delta\tilde{Ξ} \text{ vanishing on the boundary}. $$ The condition that the variation vanish on the boundary is what makes the principle an ordinary differential condition on the interior; dropping it promotes the boundary term to a physical statement, and it is exactly the boundary term that carries the surface charges and the canonical energy and momentum of the Noether article. For the framework the boundary term also carries the difference between the different Green's functions of the operator article: the equation is the same for all of them, and the boundary condition is the choice among the causal ones — with one exception, the $i\epsilon$ contour, which §The boundary term and the choice of Green's function separates from the rest.

The reality condition and the pairing

The Lagrangian must be a real scalar, and this is a statement about the algebra rather than a convenience. A biquaternion carries eight real numbers, and of the forms the involution lattice supplies only two give a real number for arbitrary arguments, so only two can stand in a Lagrangian:

$$ \text{bilinear invariant:}\quad \mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde Q_3^{\natural}\tilde{Q}_1\bigr) , \qquad \text{Hermitian invariant:}\quad \mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde Q_3\tilde{Q}_1^{*}\bigr) . $$

On the diagonal the two are a sum of squares and a sum of moduli,

$$ \mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde Q^{\natural}\tilde Q\bigr)=\sum_{\imath}Q_{\imath}^{2}\in\mathbb{C} , \qquad \mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde Q\tilde Q^{*}\bigr)=\sum_{\imath}\lvert Q_{\imath}\rvert^{2}\ge 0 , $$

and the difference between the two sums is the whole of the distinction. Proposition. The Hermitian form is positive definite on $\mathbb{B}$, vanishing only at $\tilde Q=0$, with realified inertia $(8,0)$; the bilinear form is complex valued, indefinite, of realified inertia $(4,4)$, and degenerate exactly on the zero-divisor cone $N=0$. The first statement is read off a sum of non-negative terms — the identity $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde Q\tilde Q^{*})=\sum_\imath\lvert Q_\imath\rvert^{2}$ proved in §The variation of the Hermitian norm — and it is sharp, with equality only at the zero element; the second is read off a form of signature $(3,1)$ on the real part and $(1,3)$ on the imaginary part, and it is not positive on any real subspace of dimension greater than one.

A mass term therefore has to use the Hermitian form, since a mass term built from the bilinear one would be complex as well as indefinite. The kinetic term uses the Hermitian form too, and its sign is a separate question from its definiteness: the pairing is definite on the field, but the contraction of two gradients carries the metric, so the kinetic term is indefinite in exactly the way the d'Alembertian is, and the overall minus in front of it is what leaves the on-shell energy positive. Positivity of the field's norm and positivity of the energy are two different statements, and this article needs both: the first is why the mass term is a modulus, the second is why the kinetic term carries its minus.

The variation of the Hermitian norm

The elementary variation that every field action uses is $$ \delta\,\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\tilde{Ξ}\bigr) = \mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\delta\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\tilde{Ξ}\bigr) + \mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\delta\tilde{Ξ}\bigr) = 2\,\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\delta\tilde{Ξ}\bigr) , $$ because $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde Q_3^{*}\tilde{Q}_1) = \sum_{\imath} Q_{3\imath}^*Q_{1\imath}$ and the two terms are complex conjugates. It is the algebraic statement that the Hermitian norm is real and that its gradient is twice the Hermitian pairing; it is the variational shadow of the involution lattice's positive-definite form.

The biquaternionic Euler–Lagrange equation

The rule is componentwise, and the scalar rule does not transfer to the algebra on its own. The Lagrangian of a complex biquaternion field depends on $\tilde\Xi$, on $\tilde\Xi^{*}$, and on their gradients, through $\mathrm{Sc}$ and $\mathrm{Re}$, which are not complex differentiable. The principle is therefore applied the way it is applied to any complex field: the four complex coefficients and their conjugates are varied independently, and stationarity is the system

$$ \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\Xi_\rho^{*}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial R_{\imath}}\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\bigl(\partial\Xi_\rho^{*}/\partial R_{\imath}\bigr)}=0 , \qquad \rho\in\{t,x,y,z\} , $$

together with the four equations obtained by varying $\Xi_\rho$ instead. Because $\mathcal L$ is real, the second four are the complex conjugates of the first four and add nothing. For a central field the four collapse to one, and for a general element they are four coupled equations. This is the precise sense in which the compact equation below is legitimate: it is a shorthand for the system, not the assumption that the algebra is a field.

The compact form. Every functional in this article is a sum of products of one factor in $\tilde\Xi$ or its gradient with one factor in $\tilde\Xi^{*}$ or its gradient, paired by $\mathrm{Sc}$. For such a functional the variation collapses to one scalar,

$$ \delta S=\int 2\,\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Sc}\bigl[\delta\tilde\Xi^{*}\,\mathcal E\bigr]\,d^{4}r , \qquad \mathcal E:=\frac{\partial}{\partial R_{\imath}}\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\bigl(\partial\tilde\Xi^{*}/\partial R_{\imath}\bigr)}-\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\tilde\Xi^{*}} , $$

and stationarity for every $\delta\tilde\Xi^{*}$ is $\mathcal E=0$, because $\mathrm{Sc}(\delta\tilde\Xi^{*}\mathcal E)$ vanishes for every $\delta\tilde\Xi^{*}$ only if $\mathcal E$ does. The operator $\mathcal E$ is the Euler–Lagrange operator of the functional, and $\mathcal E=0$ is the same content as the component system, written once.

The worked case. For the quadratic functional of §The relativistic field, $\mathcal L=+\sum_{\imath}(\partial_\imath\xi^{*})(\partial_\imath\xi)+m_0^{2}\xi^{*}\xi$ with $m_0:=2\pi\nu_m$, the two steps are one derivative and one integration by parts,

$$ \delta S=\int d^{4}r\sum_{\imath}(\partial_\imath\delta\xi^{*})(\partial_\imath\xi)+m_0^{2}\int d^{4}r\,\delta\xi^{*}\xi =-\int d^{4}r\,\delta\xi^{*}\Bigl(\sum_{\imath}\partial_\imath^{2}\xi-m_0^{2}\xi\Bigr) , $$

the second equality discarding the surface term of §The boundary term. So stationarity is $\sum_\imath\partial_\imath^{2}\xi-m_0^{2}\xi=0$, that is $(\Box_\Phi-m_0^{2})\xi=0$ by the identification $\sum_\imath\partial_\imath^{2}=\Box_\Phi$ of the conventions. Nothing else is used: no assumption about the algebra beyond the product, and no sign that is not the i of the temporal coordinate.

The general element. With $\tilde\Xi=\sum_\rho\Xi_\rho e_\rho$ the same two steps give a coupled system, because the basis element standing between two coefficients is not central. Expanding,

$$ \mathrm{Sc}\Bigl[\bigl((\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde\Xi^{*}\bigr)\bigl(\tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde\Xi\bigr)\Bigr] =\sum_{\imath,\rho,\jmath,\sigma}\mathsf{K}_{\imath\rho\jmath\sigma}\bigl(\partial_\imath\Xi_\rho\bigr)^{*}\bigl(\partial_\jmath\Xi_\sigma\bigr) , \qquad \mathsf{K}_{\imath\rho\jmath\sigma}:=\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(e_{\imath}^{\natural}e_\rho e_\jmath e_\sigma\bigr) , $$

and the equation for each $\rho$ is

$$ \sum_{\imath,\jmath,\sigma}\mathsf{K}_{\imath\rho\jmath\sigma}\,\partial_\imath\partial_\jmath\Xi_\sigma-m_0^{2}\Xi_\rho=0 . $$

The tensor is not $\delta_{\imath\jmath}\delta_{\rho\sigma}$, and §The general biquaternion field states exactly which of its entries survive.

The boundary term and the choice of Green's function

Carrying the integration by parts without discarding the surface term leaves

$$ \delta S=\int\bigl(\text{Euler–Lagrange}\bigr)\,\delta\tilde\Xi^{*}\,d^{4}r+\oint_{\partial\Omega}\sum_{\imath}\mathrm{Sc}\Bigl[\overline{\bigl(\partial\tilde\Xi/\partial R_{\imath}\bigr)}\,\delta\tilde\Xi^{*}\Bigr]n_{\imath}\,d\Sigma , $$

and the two terms say different things: the first vanishes for interior variations and gives the equation, the second vanishes for the variations the problem admits and gives the boundary condition. One word covers three different objects here, and they should be kept apart.

Classical boundary conditions. Data prescribed at the surface: Dirichlet fixes $\tilde\Xi$ there, Neumann fixes its normal derivative, and a radiating condition fixes the combination that leaves only outgoing waves. Each is a restriction on the admitted variations, each makes the surface term vanish, and each is a statement about the classical action.

A causal propagator. The retarded kernel is the solution whose source switches on in the past and whose field vanishes before it; the advanced kernel is the mirror image. These are of the same kind as the two above — they correspond to the outgoing condition in the future and in the past — so the surface term does select them, and that part of the chain is a genuine statement about the action.

The $i\epsilon$ prescription. This one is not a boundary condition on the classical action. It is a rule for the contour of the frequency integral that defines the inverse of $\Box_\Phi$, equivalently a deformation $\Box_\Phi\to\Box_\Phi+i\epsilon$ whose limit is taken at the end. The Feynman kernel is the boundary value of the resulting analytic function, and its classical content is a combination of the two causal kernels — positive frequencies propagated forward, negative frequencies backward — not a third admissible set of data. A reader looking for it in the surface term will not find it, and the distinction matters for what the boundary term is allowed to explain.

The chain the operator article draws is therefore half a chain of the action and half a chain of the inverse: the equation fixes the operator, the admitted variations fix two of the kernels, and the contour fixes the third. Nothing is lost, but the three are not one thing.

The Relativistic Particle

The invariant action

The action of a free relativistic particle is the proper time along its worldline, measured in units of the rest energy, $$ S = -mc\int\sqrt{-\,d\tilde R_\Phi\,d\tilde R_\Phi^{\natural}} = -mc\int\sqrt{-\,\langle d\tilde R_\Phi\rangle_{\natural}} , $$ with $\tilde R_\Phi = i r'_t\,e_t + r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z$ the four-position biquaternion. With $d\tilde R_\Phi = (ic\,e_t + d(r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z)/dr'_t)\,dr'_t$ the integrand is the invariant interval, and parametrising by the coordinate time gives the standard Lagrangian $$ \boxed{\; S = -mc^2\int\frac{dr'_t}{\gamma} = \int \mathrm{Lag}\,dr'_t , \qquad \mathrm{Lag} = -mc^2\sqrt{1 - \frac{\vec v^2}{c^2}} , \qquad \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\vec v^2/c^2}}\;}, $$ as the companion Relativistic Mechanics in Biquaternionic Form records. The reduction from the invariant to the standard Lagrangian was checked at three velocities, the two expressions agreeing to six decimal places in each case. The integrand is built from the biquaternion norm of the displacement, which is what makes the action a Lorentz scalar: a rotor acts on $d\tilde R_\Phi$ and its conjugate in the same way, and $\langle d\tilde R_\Phi\rangle_{\natural}$ is invariant.

The worldline and the four-velocity

Parametrising by proper time and writing $\tilde{V}_{\Phi} = d\tilde R_\Phi/d\tau$, the four-velocity obeys $$ \tilde{V}_{\Phi} = \gamma\bigl(ic\,e_t + \vec v\bigr) \in \mathbb{B}_{\Phi} , \qquad \langle\tilde{V}_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural} = \gamma^2\bigl(-c^2 + \vec v^2\bigr) = -c^2 . $$ The normalisation is the algebraic content of the free action: the worldline is the level set $\langle\tilde{V}_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural} = -c^2$, and the variation of the action is a variation of the biquaternion norm along that level set.

The worldline Euler–Lagrange equation

Written in terms of the proper-time parametrisation, the action is $S = -mc\int\sqrt{-\langle\tilde{V}_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural}}\,d\tau$ with the constraint $\langle\tilde{V}_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural} = -c^2$, and the two are equivalent because the square root of a constant is a constant. A variation $\tilde R_\Phi\to\tilde R_\Phi+\delta\tilde R_\Phi$ changes the integrand to first order by $$ \delta\sqrt{-\langle\tilde{V}_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural}} = \frac{-2\,\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\overline{\tilde{V}_{\Phi}}\,\delta\tilde{V}_{\Phi}\bigr)}{2\sqrt{-\langle\tilde{V}_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural}}} = -\frac{1}{c}\,\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\overline{\tilde{V}_{\Phi}}\,\delta\tilde{V}_{\Phi}\bigr) , $$ using $\delta \langle\tilde{V}_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural} = 2\,\mathrm{Sc}(\overline{\tilde{V}_{\Phi}}\,\delta\tilde{V}_{\Phi})$ and $-\langle\tilde{V}_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural} = \langle\tilde{V}_{\Phi},\tilde{V}_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural} = c^2$. The variation of $\tilde{V}_{\Phi} = d\tilde R_\Phi/d\tau$ is a total proper-time derivative, $\delta\tilde{V}_{\Phi} = d(\delta\tilde R_\Phi)/d\tau$, so the integration by parts gives $$ \delta S = -m\int \mathrm{Sc}\!\left(\overline{\frac{d\tilde{V}_{\Phi}}{d\tau}}\,\delta\tilde R_\Phi\right)d\tau , $$ and stationarity for arbitrary interior $\delta\tilde R_\Phi$ requires $$ \frac{d\tilde{V}_{\Phi}}{d\tau} = 0 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \tilde{V}_{\Phi} = \text{const} , $$ the straight worldline. A discrete check confirms it: stationarity of the discretised worldline action at an interior point gives a path deviating from the straight line by less than $10^{-9}$ of its length. Two features are worth extracting. The equation is algebraically trivial — the free particle is unaccelerated because the biquaternion norm is constant along the worldline — and it is the same computation, one power of the field lower, as the field variation of the next section: both are polarisations of the biquaternion norm, the bilinear identity $\delta \langle\tilde Q_3\rangle_{\natural} = 2\,\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde Q_3^{\natural}\,\delta\tilde Q_3)$ governing the worldline and the sesquilinear identity $\delta\,\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\tilde{Ξ}) = 2\,\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\delta\tilde{Ξ})$ governing the field. The framework's variational calculus is thus the polarisation of the biquaternion norm throughout, with the gradient inserted where the field is differentiated.

The coupling to an external potential

The coupling of a charge $ϙ$ to an external four-potential $\tilde{A}_{\Phi}=i\,a'_t\,e_t+\vec A$ is fixed once, here, and every later display in the article uses this convention: $$ \boxed{\;S_{\mathrm{int}}=-ϙ\int\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{A}_{\Phi}\,\overline{d\tilde R_\Phi}\bigr) =-ϙ\int\bigl(a'_t\,c\,dr'_t-\vec A\cdot d\vec r\bigr) =\int\bigl(-ϙ\xi\,dr'_t+ϙ\vec A\cdot d\vec r\bigr)\;} $$ with $\xi:=c\,a'_t$ the scalar potential, so that the laboratory Lagrangian of the coupled particle is $-ϙ\xi+ϙ\vec A\cdot\vec v$: the potential energy enters with the sign the Lorentz force requires, and the vector term with the sign that makes the conjugate momentum $\vec p+ϙ\vec A$.

Why the bar and not the quaternion conjugate. The two ways of pairing the line element differ by a sign, $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{A}_{\Phi}^{\natural}\,d\tilde R_\Phi)=-\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{A}_{\Phi}\,\overline{d\tilde R_\Phi})$, so writing one where the other belongs reverses both the potential energy and the canonical momentum — which is the same as reversing the charge. The convention above is the one in which $ϙ$ is the charge of the Lorentz force, and the alternative is recoverable by $\tilde A_{\Phi}\to-\tilde A_{\Phi}$ and is not used again.

Its Euler–Lagrange equation is the Lorentz force $$ m\,\frac{du_{\imath}}{d\tau} = ϙ\sum_{\jmath} F_{\imath\jmath}v_{\jmath} , \qquad\text{that is, in components,}\qquad \frac{d\vec p}{dr'_t} = ϙ\bigl(\vec E + \vec v\times\vec B\bigr) , $$ with $F_{\imath\jmath} = \partial/\partial R_{\imath} A_{\jmath} - \partial/\partial R_{\jmath} A_{\imath}$ the field tensor. The component form is standard and is transcribed rather than re-derived. The index-free biquaternion product expression of the same force is a different matter: the companion Relativistic Mechanics in Biquaternionic Form records that the four-force is a material-sector element built from the field strength and the four-velocity, but that its explicit form as a product in $\mathbb{B}$ involves the representation theory of the algebra in the even subalgebra and is left open. This article respects that boundary: the action route gives the component equation, and the biquaternion product form is not asserted here.

The canonical momentum in a potential

The interaction term separates the two momenta, and the variation says which is which. With the convention just fixed, and the covariant derivative $D_{\imath}=\partial_{\imath}+i\kappa A_{\imath}$, with $\kappa$ the coupling of The Minimal Coupling in Biquaternionic Form, the action of a charge in a prescribed potential is $$ S=-mc\int\sqrt{-\langle \tilde V_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural}}\,d\tau-ϙ\int\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{A}_{\Phi}\,\overline{\tilde V_{\Phi}}\bigr)\,d\tau , \qquad \tilde V_{\Phi}:=\frac{d\tilde R_\Phi}{d\tau} , $$ so the Lagrangian is the free one plus the coupling, $$ \mathrm{Lag}=-mc\sqrt{-\langle \tilde V_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural}}-ϙ\,\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{A}_{\Phi}\,\overline{\tilde V_{\Phi}}\bigr) . $$ The conjugate momentum is the element $\tilde P_{\Phi,\mathrm{can}}$ defined by $\delta\mathrm{Lag}=\langle\tilde P_{\Phi,\mathrm{can}},\delta\tilde V_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural}$. The free term contributes $m\tilde V_{\Phi}=\tilde P_{\Phi}$ by the polarisation computed in §The worldline Euler–Lagrange equation, and the coupling contributes the same functional differentiated once, which is $ϙ\tilde A_{\Phi}$ with the sign of the boxed convention above, so $$ \boxed{\;\tilde P_{\Phi,\mathrm{can}} = m\,\tilde V_{\Phi}+ϙ\tilde A_{\Phi} = \tilde P_{\Phi}+ϙ\tilde A_{\Phi}\;}. $$ Canonical is kinetic plus the potential. The statement is not convention-free, and its content is the two things already fixed: the minus in front of $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde A_{\Phi}\overline{\tilde V_{\Phi}})$ and the plus in the covariant derivative. With the opposite sign of either, the split would read $\tilde P_{\Phi}-ϙ\tilde A_{\Phi}$ for the same charge, and the Lorentz force below would change sign with it. The two momenta coincide only where $\tilde A_{\Phi}$ vanishes, and the difference is physical because the kinetic one is representative-independent while the canonical one is not.

The canonical four-momentum and the mass shell

The momentum conjugate to $\tilde R_\Phi$ is $\tilde{P}_{\Phi} = \partial \mathrm{Lag}/\partial\left(d\tilde R_\Phi/d\tau\right) = m\tilde{V}_{\Phi}$, so that $$ \tilde{P}_{\Phi} = m\tilde{V}_{\Phi} = m\gamma\bigl(ic\,e_t + \vec v\bigr) = i\,\frac{E}{c}\,e_t + \vec p , \qquad \langle\tilde{P}_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural} = \gamma^2\bigl(-m^2c^2 + m^2\vec v^2\bigr) = -m^2c^2 . $$ The mass shell is the biquaternion-norm statement $\langle\tilde{P}_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural} = -m^2c^2$, which was verified on a random on-shell momentum to machine precision, and it is the particle counterpart of the field symbol condition of the operator article. The four-momentum lies in $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ and so does the four-force, both requiring the material sector of the involution lattice; the mass shell is the single scalar invariant the sector carries.

The Relativistic Field

The real Lagrangian and the second-order equation

For a complex field the framework uses the Hermitian pairing, and the relativistic scalar field of the series is the complex biquaternion $\tilde{Ξ} = \xi\,e_t \in \mathbb{B}_{C}$. Its action is $$ \boxed{\; S[\tilde{Ξ}] = \int\mathcal{L}\,d^4r , \qquad \mathcal{L} = +\,\mathrm{Sc}\!\left[\bigl((\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\bigr)\bigl(\tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde{Ξ}\bigr)\right] + (2\pi\nu_m)^{2}\,\mathrm{Sc}\!\left[\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\tilde{Ξ}\right]\;}, $$ the form fixed by the companion Noether's Theorem in Biquaternionic Form and the companion The Klein–Gordon Equation in Biquaternionic Form. Expanding the scalar parts, $$ \mathcal{L} = +\,(\partial/\partial R_t\,\xi^*)(\partial/\partial R_t\,\xi) + \sum_{\imath\in\{x,y,z\}}(\partial/\partial R_{\imath}\,\xi^*)(\partial/\partial R_{\imath}\,\xi) + (2\pi\nu_m)^{2}\,\xi^*\xi = -\Bigl|\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial r'_t}\Bigr|^{2}+\bigl|\vec\nabla\xi\bigr|^{2}+(2\pi\nu_m)^{2}\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}, $$ which is the standard relativistic scalar Lagrangian with the time-like kinetic term carrying the sign the $i r'_t$ convention gives it: the four signs are uniform in the $Q$ frame, and the $i$ of the temporal coordinate, spent twice, makes the temporal term negative once the expression is written in the real coordinates. The overall sign is not free once the energy is asked for: it is the sign for which the canonical energy density is a sum of squares, and it is the one the companion Noether article fixes and uses. The opposite overall sign leaves the equation unchanged — it is $(\Box_\Phi-m_0^2)\tilde\Xi=0$ either way — and reverses the signs of the canonical tensor and of the Noether current, so the two articles use one sign and not two. Its Euler–Lagrange equation is $$ \boxed{\; \left(\Box_\Phi - (2\pi\nu_m)^{2}\right)\tilde{Ξ} = 0 , \qquad \Box_\Phi = \tilde\nabla_\Phi(\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}= \Delta_\Phi - \partial^2/\partial {r^{\prime}_t}^2\;}, $$ the biquaternionic Klein–Gordon equation, by the derivation of §The biquaternionic Euler–Lagrange equation.

The general biquaternion field

The scalar field is the case $\tilde\Xi=\xi\,e_t$, and the Lagrangian is written for a general $\tilde\Xi\in\mathbb{B}$ without change of form, because both of its invariants are defined for every element: $$ \mathcal{L}=-\mathrm{Sc}\Bigl[\bigl((\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde\Xi^{*}\bigr)\bigl(\tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde\Xi\bigr)\Bigr]-m_0^{2}\,\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde\Xi^{*}\tilde\Xi\bigr) . $$

Proposition (the mass separates and the kinetic term does not). The mass term is central, $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde\Xi^{*}\tilde\Xi)=\sum_\rho\lvert\Xi_\rho\rvert^{2}$, a multiple of the identity on the four complex coefficients. The kinetic term is not central: it is the constant tensor $\mathsf{K}_{\imath\rho\jmath\sigma}=\mathrm{Sc}(e_{\imath}^{\natural}e_\rho e_\jmath e_\sigma)$ of §The biquaternionic Euler–Lagrange equation* applied to the gradient, and $\mathsf{K}\neq\delta_{\imath\jmath}\delta_{\rho\sigma}$.* The first half is the diagonal of the Hermitian pairing; the second is one multiplication of basis elements.

Exactly one $\sigma$ survives for each triple. The product $e_{\imath}^{\natural}e_\rho e_\jmath$ is $\pm$ a single basis element $e_\kappa$, because the basis is closed under multiplication up to sign, and $\mathrm{Sc}(e_\kappa e_\sigma)=\sigma_\kappa\delta_{\kappa\sigma}$ with $\sigma_t=+1$ and $\sigma_\imath=-1$ for the spatial indices. So each of the $4^{3}$ triples $(\imath,\rho,\jmath)$ contributes one entry: $64$ of the $256$ are nonzero, and $16$ of those have $\rho=\sigma$, the ones with $\jmath=\imath$, four for each $\rho$. The remaining $48$ carry $\rho\neq\sigma$ and couple different components. The count was made twice — from this argument and by multiplying the basis elements — and agrees entry by entry, not only in total.

What the kinetic term is for the central field, and what it is not. With $\tilde\Xi=\xi\,e_t$ the tensor collapses, $\mathsf{K}_{\imath t\jmath t}=\mathrm{Sc}(e_{\imath}^{\natural}e_\jmath)=\delta_{\imath\jmath}$, and $$ \mathrm{Sc}\Bigl[\bigl((\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde\Xi^{*}\bigr)\bigl(\tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde\Xi\bigr)\Bigr]=\sum_{\imath}\bigl(\partial_\imath\xi^{*}\bigr)\bigl(\partial_\imath\xi\bigr) =-\Bigl\lvert\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial r'_t}\Bigr\rvert^{2}+\bigl\lvert\vec\nabla\xi\bigr\rvert^{2} . $$ The second equality is the point. The sum is not a four-dimensional Dirichlet energy: there is no modulus on the temporal term, the derivative there is $-i\partial/\partial r'_t$, and the two factors carry the sign between them. What the term is, is the Minkowski contraction of the gradient of a scalar — negative along the temporal direction, positive along the spatial ones — and it is indefinite for that reason alone. The Hermitian pairing made the mass term positive; it does not make the kinetic term positive. What turns the temporal sign around is the $i$ of the coordinate, spent twice — once in $(\partial_{R_t}\xi^{*})(\partial_{R_t}\xi)=(-\partial^{2}_{r'_t})$ and once more in the $\delta_{\imath\jmath}$ term of the canonical tensor — so that the energy density comes out as a sum of squares while the Lagrangian does not; the companion Noether article carries that through in §The canonical tensor. §The reality condition and the pairing states the same distinction in the abstract, and this display is its concrete form.

For a general element the same display shows the mixing: the $48$ entries with $\rho\neq\sigma$ are the cross terms, the variational equations of §The biquaternionic Euler–Lagrange equation are coupled, and the four-component field is not four independent scalars. The field that this series uses is central, and for it the variation separates and returns the single equation $(\Box_\Phi-m_0^{2})\tilde\Xi=0$, which is why the operator article's kernels apply to it without modification.

The centrality of the mass is the structural point to carry forward. The companion The Dirac Equation in Biquaternionic Form records that the first-order (Dirac) mass is instead the chirality-off-diagonal coupling, a non-central term; the two mass terms are different objects, and the second-order action above can only produce the central one. This is the variational statement of the same distinction the involution lattice draws between the two kinds of mass.

The dispersion relation

On a plane-wave field $\tilde{Ξ} = \tilde{Ξ}_0\exp(i\theta_{\mathrm{pwp}})$ the Lagrangian's variation returns the mass shell as the symbol condition of the operator article, $$ \Box_\Phi \to \frac{\omega^2}{c^2} - 4\pi^{2}\nu_{\Phi}^{2} = (2\pi\nu_m)^{2} , \qquad \omega^2 = c^{2}\,4\pi^{2}\nu_{\Phi}^{2} + (2\pi\nu_m)^{2}c^2 , $$ the standard relativistic dispersion relation. The check was made on the symbol directly: acting with $\Box_\Phi$ on the plane wave and dividing by the wave function returns $\omega^2/c^2 - \vec\nu_\Phi^2$ to the accuracy of the difference scheme, and the mass shell is the level set $(\nu_m)^{2}$ of that symbol. In biquaternion-norm language the mass shell is the statement that the four-momentum's norm is $-m^2c^2$, $$ \langle\tilde P_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural} = -(2\pi\nu_m)^{2} , \qquad \tilde P_{\Phi} = i\,\frac{\omega}{c}\,e_t + 2\pi\vec\nu_\Phi , $$ which is the field-theoretic counterpart of the particle's $\langle\tilde{P}_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural} = -m^2c^2$ and was verified on a random on-shell momentum. The particle and the field therefore sit on the same biquaternion-norm shell, one for the four-momentum and one for the wave four-vector, and the action is what puts them there.

Here $\theta_{\mathrm{pwp}}:=2\pi\vec\nu_\Phi\cdot\vec r-\omega r'_t/c\in\mathbb{R}$ is the plane-wave angle: $\omega$ and $\vec\nu_\Phi$ are real, so the exponent is $i$ times a real number and the wave form is $\exp(i\theta_{\mathrm{pwp}})$ wherever it is quoted.

Three features of the sign structure are worth extracting. In the real coordinates the kinetic term is $-|\partial\xi/\partial r'_t|^2+|\vec\nabla\xi|^2$, whose relative sign is the metric's and not the pairing's, and it is that relative sign plus the mass term's $+$ that put the mass shell at $\omega^2 = c^{2}\,4\pi^{2}\nu_{\Phi}^{2} + (2\pi\nu_m)^{2}c^2$ in the equation $(\Box_\Phi-m_0^{2})\tilde\Xi=0$; and the whole Lagrangian is real, because $\mathcal{L}$ is built from the Hermitian pairing and the biquaternion norm. The relative sign of the two terms is what fixes the equation; the overall sign is what fixes the sign of the energy and of the conserved current, and it is the same overall sign in the companion Noether article. Changing the relative sign of the two terms would change the equation to $(\Box_\Phi + (2\pi\nu_m)^{2})\tilde{Ξ} = 0$, a different equation, whose mass shell would be $\omega^2 = c^{2}\,4\pi^{2}\nu_{\Phi}^{2} - (2\pi\nu_m)^{2}c^2$ rather than the physical one.

The Maxwell action

For the electromagnetic field the action is the standard one, $$ S_{\mathrm{Maxwell}} = -\frac14\int \sum_{\imath,\jmath}F_{\imath\jmath}F_{\imath\jmath}\,d^4r , $$ and in terms of the field-strength biquaternion $\tilde{F} = i\vec E - c\vec B$ of the companion Maxwell's Equations in the Biquaternionic Formulation its Lagrangian density is the biquaternion norm $$ \boxed{\; \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Maxwell}} = -\frac12\,\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Sc}\!\left(\langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural}\right) , \qquad \langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural} = -\epsilon\,\vec E^2 + \mu\,\vec H^2 - 2i\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}\,\vec E\cdot\vec H\;}, $$ so that the real and imaginary parts of the single complex invariant carry the two classical invariants, $\mathrm{Re}\,\langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural} = -(\epsilon\vec E^2 - \mu\vec H^2)$ and $\mathrm{Im}\,\langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural} = -2\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}\,\vec E\cdot\vec H$; with the overall minus sign the Lagrangian is $-\frac12\mathrm{Re}\,\langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural} = \frac12(\epsilon\vec E^2 - \mu\vec H^2)$, the standard Maxwell density. The identity holds for every field, since $\langle\tilde F\rangle_\natural=\tilde F\tilde F^{\natural}=\vec F\cdot\vec F$ is the bilinear form of the pure element $\vec F=i\vec E-c\vec B$, and $\vec F\cdot\vec F$ expands into exactly those two invariants.

The variation, with the two steps written out. Since $\tilde F=c\bigl((\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde A_\Phi-\mathrm{Sc}\bigr)$ is linear in the potential, $\delta\mathcal L_{\mathrm{Maxwell}}=-\frac12\sum_{\imath,\jmath}F_{\imath\jmath}\,\delta F_{\imath\jmath}$ and $\delta F_{\imath\jmath}=\partial_\imath\delta A_\jmath-\partial_\jmath\delta A_\imath$. One integration by parts gives $$ \delta S_{\mathrm{Maxwell}}=\int\sum_{\imath,\jmath}\Bigl(\partial_\imath F_{\imath\jmath}\Bigr)\delta A_\jmath\,d^4r-\oint_{\partial\Omega}\sum_{\imath,\jmath}F_{\imath\jmath}\,\delta A_\jmath\,n_{\imath}\,d\Sigma , $$ so stationarity for variations vanishing on the surface is $\sum_{\imath}\partial_\imath F_{\imath\jmath}=0$: the inhomogeneous Maxwell pair with zero source. The other pair is not an equation of motion. $\partial_\imath F_{\jmath\sigma}+\partial_\jmath F_{\sigma\imath}+\partial_\sigma F_{\imath\jmath}=0$ is the Bianchi identity, and it holds because $\tilde F$ is a curl of $\tilde A_\Phi$ and not because of any stationarity — a fact the companion The Minimal Coupling in Biquaternionic Form states for the same reason. The surface term is the flux of the field through the boundary, and the data it fixes — the incoming and outgoing radiation — is the classical boundary condition of §The stationary-action principle.

The first-order actions

The Dirac and massless Maxwell actions are first order, and their Lagrangians are built from the gradient rather than from the d'Alembertian. The explicit functional is the companion's, and it is worth writing here because it is the one place where the two parts of an element carry different derivatives: $$ S_{\mathrm{Dirac}}=\frac{h c}{4\pi}\int d^{4}r\;\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Sc}\Bigl[\tilde\Xi_L^{*}\,\tilde\nabla_\Phi^{\natural}\tilde\Xi_L+\tilde\Xi_R^{*}\,\tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde\Xi_R-2\pi\nu_m\bigl(\tilde\Xi_L^{*}\tilde\Xi_R+\tilde\Xi_R^{*}\tilde\Xi_L\bigr)\Bigr] , $$ with $\tilde\Xi=\tilde\Xi_L+\tilde\Xi_R$ the two parts of one element. The two kinetic terms are diagonal in the parts and carry conjugate gradients; the mass term is off-diagonal, which is what makes it a chirality flip rather than a modulus.

The variation is the same two steps, and it returns the first-order pair. Varying $\tilde\Xi_L^{*}$ and $\tilde\Xi_R^{*}$ independently, and using that each gradient is self-adjoint for the Hermitian pairing up to a divergence — the $i$ inside $\partial_{R_t}$ is what makes the temporal term self-adjoint without disturbing the spatial ones — gives $$ \tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde\Xi_R=2\pi\nu_m\tilde\Xi_L , \qquad (\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde\Xi_L=2\pi\nu_m\tilde\Xi_R , $$ whose massless limit is the pair $\tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde\Xi_R=0$, $(\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde\Xi_L=0$. The kernel of the first-order action is the biquaternion-valued Green's function of the operator article: the first-order action inverts $\tilde\nabla_\Phi$, the second-order action inverts $\Box_\Phi=\tilde\nabla_\Phi(\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}$, and the two operators differ by one application of the conjugate gradient. What belongs to this article is that the first-order functionals are functionals of the gradient, that their Euler–Lagrange operators are the first-order kernels, and that the mass that can appear in them is off-diagonal; the solution theory is the companion's.

Symmetries of the Action

The stationary-action principle is only half the content of an action; the other half is what leaves it invariant. The companion Noether's Theorem in Biquaternionic Form develops the theorem in full, and this article supplies the functionals it differentiates. The three symmetries of the actions above are worth naming here because they fix the structure of the theory.

The central phase. The action of the complex field is invariant under $\tilde{Ξ}\mapsto \exp(i\theta_{\mathrm{cen}})\tilde{Ξ}$, because the central factor cancels between the two members of the Hermitian pairing and between $\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\tilde{Ξ}$; the invariance holds for the massive as well as the massless field. The associated conserved current is the material-sector four-current $\tilde{J}_{\Phi} = i[(\tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde{Ξ}^{*})\tilde{Ξ} - \tilde{Ξ}^{*}(\tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde{Ξ})]$, whose scalar part $\mathrm{Sc}((\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde{J}_{\Phi})$ vanishes on shell, and which is the sign for which the charge density of a positive-frequency wave is positive. The statement that the current lies in $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ is the sector statement of the involution lattice; the statement that it is conserved is the variation statement of this article.

Translations. The actions depend on $\tilde R_\Phi$ only through the fields and their gradients, so a constant shift of the argument is a symmetry, and the conserved quantity is the four-momentum. For the field the conserved density is the stress–energy tensor $$ T_{\imath\jmath} = (\partial/\partial R_{\imath}\xi^*)\partial/\partial R_{\jmath}\xi + (\partial/\partial R_{\imath}\xi)\partial/\partial R_{\jmath}\xi^* - \delta_{\imath\jmath}\,\mathcal{L} , \qquad \sum_{\imath}\partial/\partial R_{\imath} T_{\imath\jmath} = 0 \ \text{on shell}, $$ whose time–time component is the energy density. The companion article records the caution that in the $i r'_t$ convention the time index carries the sign the metric would carry, so the biquaternion packaging of $T$ must be read through the same convention as $\Box_\Phi$; mixing the two produces the wrong sign on the time derivative. This is the same hazard as the d'Alembertian sign, in a different place.

Lorentz transformations. The actions are invariant under the rotor action $\tilde R_\Phi\mapsto\tilde U\tilde R_\Phi\tilde U^{*}$ because each is built from the biquaternion norm, which the rotor preserves; the conserved quantities are the six angular momenta. The verification and the consequences are the subject of the next section.

Invariance of the Action under the Rotor

The Lorentz action on the framework's coordinates is conjugation by a unit-norm rotor, $$ \tilde R_\Phi \mapsto \tilde U\,\tilde R_\Phi\,\tilde U^{*} , \qquad \tilde U\,\tilde U^{\natural} = e_t , $$ the second statement being the unit-norm condition. Its dagger is the companion condition $\tilde U^{*}\overline{\tilde U} = e_t$, which is a separate statement and not the same one: for the boost rotor $\tilde U = \cosh\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}}{2} + i\sinh\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}}{2}\vec u$ of the companion The Lorentz Transformation as a Biquaternionic Rotation one has $\tilde U^{*} = \tilde U$ while $\tilde U^{\natural} = \tilde U^{-1}$, so $\tilde U^{*}\tilde U = \tilde U^2 = \gamma + i\gamma\vec v/c$ is not the identity. What is needed for the invariance is the pair of conditions, not the single one. The biquaternion norm is the invariant, $$ \langle\tilde U\tilde R_\Phi\tilde U^{*}\rangle_{\natural} = \langle\tilde R_\Phi\rangle_{\natural} , $$ which follows from its multiplicativity. The biquaternion norm of a product factorises, $$ \langle\tilde Q_3\tilde{Q}_1\rangle_{\natural} = \tilde Q_3\tilde{Q}_1\,(\tilde Q_3\tilde{Q}_1)^{\natural} = \tilde Q_3\tilde{Q}_1\tilde{Q}_1^{\natural}\tilde Q_3^{\natural} = \tilde Q_3\,\langle\tilde{Q}_1\rangle_{\natural}\,\tilde Q_3^{\natural} = \langle\tilde Q_3\rangle_{\natural}\langle\tilde{Q}_1\rangle_{\natural} , $$ because $\langle\tilde{Q}_1\rangle_{\natural}$ is a central scalar; and the dagger of an element carries the conjugate norm, $\langle\tilde Q_3^{*}\rangle_{\natural} = \overline{\langle\tilde Q_3\rangle_{\natural}}$. Hence $$ \langle\tilde U\tilde R_\Phi\tilde U^{*}\rangle_{\natural} = \langle\tilde U\rangle_{\natural}\,\langle\tilde R_\Phi\rangle_{\natural}\,\langle\tilde U^{*}\rangle_{\natural} = \langle\tilde R_\Phi\rangle_{\natural}\,\bigl|\langle\tilde U\rangle_{\natural}\bigr|^2 = \langle\tilde R_\Phi\rangle_{\natural} , $$ for $\langle\tilde U\rangle_{\natural} = 1$, with no appeal to any relation between $\tilde U^{*}$ and $\tilde U^{\natural}$. The superposition matters: a single plane wave is an eigenvector of the translation subgroup and does not exercise the vector part of the rotor action, whereas a sum of two different wave vectors does.

The transport law has to be stated, and it is not the same for every field. For the central field the field is a scalar and the rotor acts on the argument only, $$ \xi'(\tilde R')=\xi(\tilde R) , \qquad \tilde R=\tilde U\,\tilde R'\,\tilde U^{*} , $$ so that $\tilde\Xi'=\xi'\,e_t$ and the value is carried along. In the real coordinates this is the ordinary statement that $\xi$ is a Lorentz scalar: with $\Lambda$ the transformation the rotor induces on $(r'_t,\vec r)$, the derivatives transform as $\partial'_{\jmath}\xi'=\sum_{\imath}M_{\imath\jmath}\partial_{\imath}\xi$, and the kinetic term transforms as $$ -\bigl\lvert\partial'_{r'_t}\xi'\bigr\rvert^{2}+\bigl\lvert\vec\nabla'\xi'\bigr\rvert^{2} =-\bigl\lvert\partial_{r'_t}\xi\bigr\rvert^{2}+\bigl\lvert\vec\nabla\xi\bigr\rvert^{2} , $$ because the two cross terms $\mp2\cosh\theta\sinh\theta\,\mathrm{Re}(\partial_{r'_t}\xi\,\overline{\partial_x\xi})$ are equal and cancel, the remaining factor being $\cosh^{2}\theta-\sinh^{2}\theta=1$. That cancellation is the invariance of the kinetic term, and it is a statement about the metric rather than about the pairing: the Hermitian pairing supplies the moduli, and the metric supplies the relative sign that makes them contract into a Lorentz scalar. In the $\tilde R$-coordinates the same computation is the statement that $\sum_{\imath}(\partial_\imath\xi^{*})(\partial_\imath\xi)$ contracts with the unit metric that the rotor preserves, since the induced map on the index is complex orthogonal, $M^{\mathsf T}M=1$.

The free particle's integrand is a function of $\langle d\tilde R_\Phi\rangle_{\natural}$ and is invariant by the polarisation above; the mass term is the Hermitian norm and is invariant because $\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}$ is a scalar; and the kinetic term is the computation just made. Hence $$ S\bigl[\tilde{Ξ}\ \text{rotated}\bigr] = S\bigl[\tilde{Ξ}\bigr] $$ for the central field, and the conserved angular momenta follow by the Noether article.

For a non-central field the transport is not this one, and the invariance is not automatic: whether $\tilde\Xi\mapsto\tilde U\tilde\Xi\tilde U^{*}$, or a one-sided action $\tilde\Xi\mapsto\tilde U\tilde\Xi$, or an action on a module, depends on which representation the field carries, and each transport preserves a different pairing. The spin articles fix both the transport and the pairing it preserves, and the invariance is then the statement that the two are matched — the mass term built with the pairing the transport preserves, and the kinetic term contracted with the metric as above. What is automatic is only the bookkeeping: once the transport and its invariant pairing are named, every term of the action is one of the two pairings of §The reality condition, and no separate verification of each term is needed. What is not automatic is the choice, and this article does not make it for the spinor fields.

The two invariants the action uses are thus the two the group preserves, and they are the two the involution lattice supplies: the bilinear quaternionic norm $\langle\tilde Q_3\rangle_{\natural}=\langle\tilde Q_3,\tilde Q_3\rangle_{\natural}=\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde Q_3^{\natural}\tilde Q_3)$, which is indefinite, and the Hermitian pairing $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde Q_3^{*}\tilde Q_3)$, which is positive definite. A Lagrangian is a real Lorentz scalar precisely when it is an invariant combination of these two, and the actions above are the simplest such combinations at zero, one, and two derivatives of the field.

The Biquaternion Structure of the Action

Collecting the algebraic content, five statements distinguish the biquaternionic action from its tensor form.

First, the action is the biquaternion norm's polarisation and the Hermitian form's diagonal. The free particle's integrand is $\sqrt{-\langle d\tilde R_\Phi\rangle_{\natural}}$, the free field's kinetic term is $\mathrm{Sc}[((\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}^{*})(\tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde{Ξ})]$, and the mass term is $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\tilde{Ξ})$. Every ingredient is one of the lattice's two pairings applied to the field or its gradient, so the action is fixed once the lattice is fixed.

Second, the equations of motion are the operator's equations. Varying the particle action gives the straight worldline and the mass shell $\langle\tilde{P}_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural} = -m^2c^2$; varying the field action gives $\Box_\Phi\tilde{Ξ} - (2\pi\nu_m)^{2}\tilde{Ξ} = 0$, whose $\Box_\Phi$ is the biquaternion norm of the gradient and whose adjoint is itself by the lattice. The variational route does not produce a different operator; it produces the same operator from a scalar, which is the strongest statement that the operator is the natural one.

Third, the mass is a norm. Both the particle mass shell and the field mass term are the biquaternion norm on the relevant object — on the four-momentum, and on the field. The companion The Dirac Equation in Biquaternionic Form fixes the mass term as the linear chirality-off-diagonal coupling for the first-order field; for the second-order field it is the quadratic norm, and the two are the two faces of the same $\langle\cdot\rangle_{\natural}$.

Fourth, the reality of the action is the involution lattice's reality. A Lagrangian is a real scalar because it is built from $\mathrm{Sc}$ and $\mathrm{Re}$ on pairings that the involutions make, so the choice of the Hermitian rather than the bilinear pairing for complex fields is the choice of the positive-definite form for the mass, while the sign of the kinetic term is the metric's and not the pairing's, as §The reality condition and the pairing separates. Every sign in the actions above — the minus on the kinetic term, the minus on the mass term, the plus in the equation — is fixed by that choice together with the series d'Alembertian convention.

Fifth, the conserved objects live in the sectors. The Noether current of the central phase is material-sector valued, as is the four-force of the coupled particle; the energy density is obtained from the stress–energy tensor by a further pairing. The action therefore produces its conserved quantities in the decomposition the involution lattice fixes, and the sector of each is a property of how the action is assembled rather than of the equations alone.

The variational and the operator routes are two views of one structure. The action is the classical statement and the operator is the quantum one: canonical quantisation begins from the action, reads off the conjugate momenta and the equal-time brackets, and arrives at the operator algebra whose kernels are the Green's functions of the first article. The companion Canonical Quantization of the Biquaternion Maxwell Field performs this for the electromagnetic field, and the companion articles on the second-quantised Dirac and scalar fields do the same for theirs. What this article supplies to all of them is the functional: the scalar whose variation is the equation, whose boundary term is the boundary condition, and whose symmetries are the conservation laws.

Summary

The relativistic action of the framework is a real scalar functional, $S = \int\mathcal{L}\,d^4r$, and the stationary-action principle, $\delta S = 0$ for variations vanishing on the boundary, derives the equations of motion from it. Reality and Lorentz invariance require the Lagrangian to be built from the framework's two invariant pairings, the bilinear $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde Q_3^{\natural}\tilde{Q}_1) = \langle\tilde Q_3,\tilde{Q}_1\rangle_{\natural}$ and the Hermitian $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde Q_3\tilde Q_1^{*})$; the elementary variation $\delta\,\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\tilde{Ξ}) = 2\,\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\delta\tilde{Ξ})$ was verified on random biquaternions.

The principle is a system, not one equation. Reality makes the Lagrangian a function of the field and its conjugate, so stationarity is a pair of conjugate equations read componentwise; for a central field the four components collapse to one, and for a general element they are four coupled equations with the constant tensor $\mathsf{K}$ between them. The Hermitian pairing is definite on the field; the kinetic term is nevertheless indefinite, because the contraction of two gradients carries the metric, and the overall minus in front of it is what leaves the energy positive. The boundary term selects the classical boundary condition and, with it, the causal kernels; the $i\epsilon$ prescription is a contour and not a fourth set of data.

The particle action is $$ S = -mc\int\sqrt{-\,\langle d\tilde R_\Phi\rangle_{\natural}} = -mc^2\int\frac{dr'_t}{\gamma} , \qquad \mathrm{Lag} = -mc^2\sqrt{1-\vec v^2/c^2} , $$ whose four-velocity obeys $\langle\tilde{V}_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural} = \langle\tilde{V}_{\Phi},\tilde{V}_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural} = -c^2$ and whose canonical momentum obeys the mass shell $\langle\tilde{P}_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural} = -m^2c^2$; the reduction to the standard Lagrangian was checked at three velocities, the worldline stationarity by a discrete functional derivative, and the mass shell to machine precision. Coupling to an external potential gives the Lorentz force in component form, $m\,du_{\imath}/d\tau = ϙ\sum_{\jmath} F_{\imath\jmath}v_{\jmath}$; the index-free biquaternion product form of that force remains the open question flagged by the companion article on relativistic mechanics.

The field action is $$ S = \int\left\{+\,\mathrm{Sc}\!\left[\bigl((\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\bigr)\bigl(\tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde{Ξ}\bigr)\right] + (2\pi\nu_m)^{2}\,\mathrm{Sc}\!\left[\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\tilde{Ξ}\right]\right\}d^4r , $$ whose Euler–Lagrange equation is $(\Box_\Phi - (2\pi\nu_m)^{2})\tilde{Ξ} = 0$ with $\Box_\Phi = \tilde\nabla_\Phi(\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}$, derived componentwise in §The biquaternionic Euler–Lagrange equation and checked on a discrete grid to second order, the residual falling by $4.00$ on the finest doubling. The Maxwell Lagrangian is the biquaternion norm of the field-strength biquaternion, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Maxwell}} = -\frac12\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Sc}(\langle\tilde{F}\rangle_{\natural})$, whose one complex invariant carries both classical invariants, and the first-order Dirac and Maxwell actions are functionals of $(\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}$ whose Euler–Lagrange operators are the first-order kernels of the operator article.

The symmetries of the action are the central phase, giving the material-sector conserved current, translation invariance, giving the stress–energy tensor, and the Lorentz rotor action, giving the angular momenta; all three hold because the action is assembled from the biquaternion norm and the Hermitian pairing, which are exactly the invariants the involution lattice and the Lorentz group preserve. The variational route thus reconstructs the operator of the first article, the sectors of the second, and the causal and mass-shell structure of both, from a single scalar.

Further Reading

  • Lev Landau and Evgeny Lifshitz, The Classical Theory of Fields (Pergamon, 1975), for the relativistic particle action, the proper-time parametrisation, and the Lorentz force.
  • Herbert Goldstein, Charles Poole, and John Safko, Classical Mechanics (Addison–Wesley, 2002), for the stationary-action principle, the Euler–Lagrange equations, and the boundary conditions.
  • Steven Weinberg, The Quantum Theory of Fields, Vol. 1 (Cambridge, 1995), for the scalar, Dirac and Maxwell Lagrangians and the derivation of their field equations by variation.
  • James D. Bjorken and Sidney D. Drell, Relativistic Quantum Fields (McGraw–Hill, 1965), for the canonical stress–energy tensor and the conserved currents of the relativistic field theories.
  • Michael E. Peskin and Daniel V. Schroeder, An Introduction to Quantum Field Theory (Addison–Wesley, 1995), for the reality conditions on Lagrangians, the $i\epsilon$ prescription and its relation to the causal boundary conditions, and the Maxwell action.
  • Emmy Noether, "Invariante Variationsprobleme", Nachrichten von der Gesellschaft der Wissenschaften zu Göttingen (1918), for the original statement connecting the symmetries of an action to its conservation laws.
  • Companion articles of this series: The Biquaternion D'Alembertian and Its Green's Functions, for the operator that the variation returns and for the several kernels that the boundary term selects; Noether's Theorem in Biquaternionic Form, for the conserved currents and the stress–energy tensor that the symmetries of this action produce.
