Exercise: The Bloch Ball and the Geometry of Mixed States

Introduction

This article is a worked exercise on the geometry of the qubit state space. It is a set of six problems on one theme, and it applies the state-space results established in the companion article The Bloch Ball as the Normalised Slice of the Future Light Cone. The reader is assumed to have read that article; the exercise exists precisely to test it, so nothing is carried over except what that article establishes.

The conventions are those of the companion articles. The biquaternion algebra is $\mathbb{B} = \mathbb{C}\otimes_\mathbb{R}\mathbb{H}$, with the Hermitian subspace $\mathbb{M}_+$ and the anti-Hermitian subspace $\mathbb{M}_-$, and $\mathbb{B} = \mathbb{M}_+ \oplus \mathbb{M}_-$. The quaternion units are $e_t = 1, \vec e_x, \vec e_y, \vec e_z$ with $\vec e_\mu^2 = -e_t$, and $i$ is the scalar imaginary, $i^2 = -1$. The real-quaternion subspace is $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ and the centre is $\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{B}}$. The trace of an element of $\mathbb{M}_+$ is twice its scalar part, $\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde Q)=2\,\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde Q)$.

Assumed results. From the parent article we take, without rederivation, the following. A state is an element

$$ \tilde{Ϛ} = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(e_t + i\vec r\bigr), \qquad \vec r = r_1 \vec e_x + r_2 \vec e_y + r_3 \vec e_z \in \mathbb{R}^3, $$

with $\vec r$ the Bloch vector; it is a state exactly when $|\vec r| \leq 1$, i.e. when it lies in the closed unit ball $B^3$. The biquaternion norm on the trace-one slice is $\tilde{Ϛ}\tilde{Ϛ}^{\natural} = \tfrac{1}{4}(1 - |\vec r|^2)e_t$. The eigenvalues are $\lambda_\pm = \tfrac{1}{2}(1 \pm |\vec r|)$. The purity is $\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{Ϛ}^2) = \tfrac{1}{2}(1 + |\vec r|^2)$ and the linear entropy is $S_{\mathrm{lin}}(\tilde{Ϛ}) = 1 - \mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{Ϛ}^2) = \tfrac{1}{2}(1 - |\vec r|^2)$. The von Neumann entropy is $S(\tilde{Ϛ}) = -\lambda_+\log\lambda_+ - \lambda_-\log\lambda_-$. The Hermitian form on the slice is

$$ \langle\tilde{Ϛ}_1,\tilde{Ϛ}_2\rangle_{*} = \mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{Ϛ}_1\tilde{Ϛ}_2^{*}) = \tfrac14\bigl(1 + \vec r\cdot\vec s\bigr), \qquad \tilde{Ϛ}_1 = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(e_t + i\vec r\bigr),\ \tilde{Ϛ}_2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(e_t + i\vec s\bigr), $$

whose trace reading, at normalisation, is $\operatorname{Tr}(\tilde{Ϛ}_1\tilde{Ϛ}_2^{*}) = 2\langle\tilde{Ϛ}_1,\tilde{Ϛ}_2\rangle_{*} = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(1 + \vec r\cdot\vec s\bigr)$. Both states are Hermitian and positive, so the star is inert and the unstarred trace $\operatorname{Tr}(\tilde{Ϛ}_1\tilde{Ϛ}_2)$ is real and non-negative.

The squared Hilbert–Schmidt distance is $\mathrm{Tr}((\tilde{Ϛ}_1-\tilde{Ϛ}_2)^2) = \tfrac{1}{2}|\vec r-\vec s|^2$, the Uhlmann transition probability is $\tfrac{1}{2}(1 + \vec r\cdot\vec s + \sqrt{(1-|\vec r|^2)(1-|\vec s|^2)})$, and convex combinations act on Bloch vectors by the same weights. The pure states are the idempotents $\tilde\Pi_{1,2}(\vec u) = \tfrac{1}{2}(e_t \pm i\vec u)$ with $|\vec u| = 1$, forming the boundary sphere, and the maximally mixed state is the centre $\vec r = 0$.

What is to be shown. Six problems: (1) the parametrisation of a mixed state by its Bloch vector; (2) purity, linear entropy, and von Neumann entropy for explicit states; (3) the geometry of convex combinations; (4) the metric and distinguishability structure; (5) the centre and the boundary; (6) when two Bloch vectors give identical or orthogonal states. Each is solved in full, and numerical values are given where they aid the check.

Problem 1: Parametrising a Mixed State by Its Bloch Vector

Problem. (a) A qubit is prepared by mixing the spin-up and spin-down states along the $z$-axis with probabilities $\tfrac34$ and $\tfrac14$. Write the state in the form $\tilde{Ϛ} = \tfrac{1}{2}(e_t + i\vec r)$ and read off $\vec r$. (b) Conversely, for $\vec r = \tfrac12(\vec e_x + \vec e_y)$, write $\tilde{Ϛ}$ and its spectral decomposition. (c) Verify that under the isomorphism $\vec e_\mu \mapsto -i\sigma_\mu$ the state maps to $\tfrac12(I + \vec r\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})$, and identify the components $r_\mu$.

Solution. (a) The two pure states along $\pm z$ are the idempotents

$$ \tilde\Pi_1(\vec e_z) = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(e_t + i \vec e_z\bigr), \qquad \tilde\Pi_2(\vec e_z) = \tilde\Pi_1(-\vec e_z) = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(e_t - i \vec e_z\bigr). $$

The mixture is a convex combination, so its Bloch vector is the same weighted combination of $\vec e_z$ and $-\vec e_z$:

$$ \tilde{Ϛ} = \tfrac34 \tilde\Pi_1(\vec e_z) + \tfrac14 \tilde\Pi_2(\vec e_z) = \tfrac{3}{8}\bigl(e_t + i\vec e_z\bigr) + \tfrac{1}{8}\bigl(e_t - i\vec e_z\bigr) = \tfrac12 e_t + \tfrac14 i \vec e_z = \tfrac12\Bigl(e_t + i\,\tfrac12 \vec e_z\Bigr). $$

Hence $\vec r = \tfrac12 \vec e_z$, with $|\vec r| = \tfrac12 \leq 1$. The state is mixed, as expected from a nontrivial mixture, and its eigenvalues are $\lambda_\pm = \tfrac12(1 \pm \tfrac12) = \tfrac34, \tfrac14$: the two preparation probabilities, recovered from the Bloch vector.

(b) For $\vec r = \tfrac12(\vec e_x + \vec e_y)$ we have $|\vec r|^2 = \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac12$, so $|\vec r| = 1/\sqrt2$. The state is

$$ \tilde{Ϛ} = \tfrac12\Bigl(e_t + i\,\tfrac12(\vec e_x + \vec e_y)\Bigr) = \tfrac12 e_t + \tfrac14 i(\vec e_x + \vec e_y). $$

With $\vec u_r = \vec r/|\vec r| = (\vec e_x + \vec e_y)/\sqrt2$ and $\lambda_\pm = \tfrac12(1 \pm 1/\sqrt2)$, the spectral decomposition is

$$ \tilde{Ϛ} = \lambda_+ \tilde\Pi_1(\vec u_r) + \lambda_- \tilde\Pi_2(\vec u_r), \qquad \tilde\Pi_{1,2}(\vec u_r) = \tfrac12\bigl(e_t \pm i\vec u_r\bigr). $$

This is correct because the two idempotents reconstruct the state,

$$ \lambda_+ \tilde\Pi_1(\vec u_r) + \lambda_- \tilde\Pi_2(\vec u_r) = \tfrac12(\lambda_+ + \lambda_-)e_t + \tfrac12(\lambda_+ - \lambda_-) i\vec u_r = \tfrac12 e_t + \tfrac{1}{2\sqrt2}\, i\,\frac{\vec e_x + \vec e_y}{\sqrt2} = \tfrac12 e_t + \tfrac14 i(\vec e_x + \vec e_y), $$

where we used $\lambda_+ + \lambda_- = 1$ and $\lambda_+ - \lambda_- = 1/\sqrt2$. Every mixed state is thus a mixture of two orthogonal pure states along its own Bloch direction, with weights fixed by the radius.

(c) Since $e_t \mapsto I$ and $\vec e_\mu \mapsto -i\sigma_\mu$, we have $i \vec e_\mu \mapsto i(-i\sigma_\mu) = \sigma_\mu$, so

$$ \tilde{Ϛ} = \tfrac12\bigl(e_t + i\textstyle\sum_\mu r_\mu \vec e_\mu\bigr) \;\longmapsto\; \tfrac12\Bigl(I + \sum_\mu r_\mu \sigma_\mu\Bigr) = \tfrac12\bigl(I + \vec r\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\bigr), $$

the standard Bloch parametrisation of a qubit density matrix. The components are the Pauli expectations: using the normalised Hermitian form with the observable $i \vec e_\mu \mapsto \sigma_\mu$,

$$ r_\mu = \mathrm{Tr}\bigl(\tilde{Ϛ}\,(i \vec e_\mu)\bigr) = \langle \sigma_\mu\rangle_{\tilde{Ϛ}}, $$

so the Bloch vector is the vector of single-qubit expectation values, $\vec r = (\langle\sigma_1\rangle, \langle\sigma_2\rangle, \langle\sigma_3\rangle)$. In particular, measuring the spin along a unit direction $\vec u$ gives outcomes with probabilities $p_\pm = \tfrac12(1 \pm \vec u\cdot\vec r)$, so the projection of $\vec r$ along $\vec u$ is the bias of the measurement.

Problem 2: Purity, Linear Entropy, and von Neumann Entropy

Problem. For each of the following states compute the purity $\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{Ϛ}^2)$, the linear entropy $S_{\mathrm{lin}}$, and the von Neumann entropy $S(\tilde{Ϛ})$: (a) the pure state with $\vec u = (1,1,1)/\sqrt3$; (b) the maximally mixed state; (c) the state with $\vec r = \tfrac12 \vec e_z$; (d) the state with $\vec r = \tfrac12(\vec e_x + \vec e_y)$. Confirm that all three quantities depend on the state only through $|\vec r|$.

Solution. All three quantities are functions of the radius alone:

$$ \boxed{\;\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{Ϛ}^2) = \tfrac12\bigl(1 + |\vec r|^2\bigr), \qquad S_{\mathrm{lin}} = 1 - \mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{Ϛ}^2) = \tfrac12\bigl(1 - |\vec r|^2\bigr)\;}, $$

$$ S(\tilde{Ϛ}) = -\lambda_+\log\lambda_+ - \lambda_-\log\lambda_-, \qquad \lambda_\pm = \tfrac12\bigl(1 \pm |\vec r|\bigr), $$

so that states of equal radius — the concentric spheres of the ball — are equally pure and equally entropic.

(a) Pure state, $|\vec r| = 1$. The purity is $\tfrac12(1+1) = 1$, the linear entropy is $0$, the eigenvalues are $\lambda_\pm = 1, 0$, and $S = -1\log 1 - 0 = 0$. Purity is the boundary condition.

(b) Maximally mixed state, $|\vec r| = 0$. The purity is $\tfrac12(1+0) = \tfrac12$, the linear entropy is $\tfrac12$, the eigenvalues are $\lambda_\pm = \tfrac12, \tfrac12$, and

$$ S = -\tfrac12\log\tfrac12 - \tfrac12\log\tfrac12 = \log 2 \approx 0.693147 \ \text{nats} = 1 \ \text{bit}. $$

This is the maximum on the ball.

(c) $\vec r = \tfrac12 \vec e_z$, $|\vec r| = \tfrac12$. The purity is $\tfrac12(1 + \tfrac14) = \tfrac58$, the linear entropy is $1 - \tfrac58 = \tfrac38$, and the eigenvalues are $\lambda_\pm = \tfrac34, \tfrac14$, so

$$ S = -\tfrac34\log\tfrac34 - \tfrac14\log\tfrac14 = \tfrac34\log\tfrac43 + \tfrac14\log 4 \approx 0.562335 \ \text{nats} \approx 0.811278 \ \text{bits}. $$

(d) $\vec r = \tfrac12(\vec e_x+\vec e_y)$, $|\vec r| = 1/\sqrt2$. The purity is $\tfrac12(1 + \tfrac12) = \tfrac34$, the linear entropy is $\tfrac14$, and with $\lambda_\pm = \tfrac12(1 \pm 1/\sqrt2) \approx 0.853553, 0.146447$,

$$ S \approx 0.416496 \ \text{nats} \approx 0.600876 \ \text{bits}. $$

Comparing (c) and (d) illustrates the monotonicity: as the radius grows from $\tfrac12$ to $1/\sqrt2$, the purity grows from $\tfrac58$ to $\tfrac34$ and the entropy falls from about $0.5623$ to about $0.4165$ nats. As in the parent article, the base of the logarithm is a convention; natural logarithms (nats) are used here, and the bit values are the same numbers divided by $\log 2$.

Problem 3: Convex Combinations and Where Mixtures Land

Problem. (a) Show that a convex combination of states is a state whose Bloch vector is the same convex combination of the Bloch vectors. (b) For a mixture of two pure states with Bloch directions $\vec u, \vec u_\nu$ at angle $\theta$, with weights $p$ and $1-p$, compute the radius and the purity of the mixture. (c) Determine exactly when such a mixture is again pure. (d) Evaluate the mixture of the pure states along $z$ and along $x$ with equal weights, and the mixture with weights $\tfrac34, \tfrac14$.

Solution. (a) Write $\tilde{Ϛ}_i = \tfrac12(e_t + i\vec r_i)$ and let $p_i \geq 0$ with $\sum_i p_i = 1$. Then

$$ \sum_i p_i \tilde{Ϛ}_i = \tfrac12\sum_i p_i e_t + \tfrac12 i \sum_i p_i \vec r_i = \tfrac12\Bigl(e_t + i \sum_i p_i \vec r_i\Bigr). $$

Since each $\tilde{Ϛ}_i$ is Hermitian of trace one, so is the combination, and its Bloch vector is $\vec r = \sum_i p_i \vec r_i$. By convexity of the ball, $|\vec r| \leq \sum_i p_i |\vec r_i| \leq 1$, so the combination is again a state. The map $\vec r \leftrightarrow \tilde{Ϛ}$ is affine, so the ball is a faithful affine model of the state space.

(b) Let $\vec u, \vec u_\nu$ be unit vectors with $\vec u\cdot\vec u_\nu = \cos\theta$, and set $\vec r = p\vec u + (1-p)\vec u_\nu$. Then

$$ |\vec r|^2 = p^2 + (1-p)^2 + 2p(1-p)\cos\theta = 1 - 2p(1-p)\bigl(1 - \cos\theta\bigr). $$

The mixture therefore lies on the chord joining $\vec u$ and $\vec u_\nu$, at the point that divides it in the ratio $(1-p) : p$. Its purity and linear entropy are

$$ \boxed{\;\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{Ϛ}^2) = \tfrac12\bigl(1 + |\vec r|^2\bigr) = 1 - p(1-p)\bigl(1-\cos\theta\bigr), \qquad S_{\mathrm{lin}} = p(1-p)\bigl(1-\cos\theta\bigr)\;}. $$

(c) From the second form, $|\vec r| = 1$ requires $p(1-p)(1-\cos\theta) = 0$, i.e. $p = 0$, or $p = 1$, or $\cos\theta = 1$ (the two pure states coincide). Apart from these degenerate cases the mixture is strictly interior: $|\vec r| < 1$, and the ball is strictly convex. In particular, a nontrivial mixture of two distinct pure states is never pure.

(d) Take $\vec u = \vec e_z$ and $\vec u_\nu = \vec e_x$, so $\theta = \pi/2$ and $\cos\theta = 0$. With $p = \tfrac12$,

$$ \vec r = \tfrac12(\vec e_x + \vec e_z), \qquad |\vec r|^2 = 1 - 2\cdot\tfrac14\cdot 1 = \tfrac12, \qquad |\vec r| = 1/\sqrt2, $$

so the purity is $\tfrac34$, the linear entropy is $\tfrac14$, and the entropy is about $0.416496$ nats — the value found in Problem 2(d), as it must be. With $p = \tfrac34$,

$$ \vec r = \tfrac34 \vec e_z + \tfrac14 \vec e_x, \qquad |\vec r|^2 = 1 - 2\cdot\tfrac34\cdot\tfrac14 = \tfrac58, $$

so the purity is $\tfrac12(1 + \tfrac58) = \tfrac{13}{16}$ and the linear entropy is $\tfrac{3}{16}$. A useful special case is $\theta = \pi$ (complementary pure states $\vec u_\nu = -\vec u$): then $\vec r = (2p-1)\vec u$, $|\vec r| = |2p-1|$, and the equal mixture $p = \tfrac12$ gives $\vec r = 0$, the maximally mixed state, independently of the axis $\vec u$.

Problem 4: The Metric and Distinguishability Structure

Problem. (a) Compute the squared Hilbert–Schmidt distance $\mathrm{Tr}((\tilde{Ϛ}_1-\tilde{Ϛ}_2)^2)$ in terms of the Bloch vectors. (b) Compute the trace distance $D(\tilde{Ϛ}_1,\tilde{Ϛ}_2) = \tfrac12\mathrm{Tr}|\tilde{Ϛ}_1-\tilde{Ϛ}_2|$, verify the parent's formula for the distance to the centre, and relate the two distances. (c) Evaluate both for $\vec r = \tfrac12 \vec e_z$ and $\vec s = \tfrac12 \vec e_x$, and compute the trace distance between two pure states as a function of the angle. (d) State the range of $D$ on the ball.

Solution. (a) The difference of two states is

$$ \tilde{Ϛ}_1 - \tilde{Ϛ}_2 = \tfrac12 i(\vec r - \vec s), \qquad \vec r - \vec s \in \mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}} \text{ a pure real quaternion.} $$

For a pure real quaternion $\vec q = \vec r - \vec s$ one has $\vec q^2 = -|\vec q|^2 e_t$, hence

$$ (\tilde{Ϛ}_1 - \tilde{Ϛ}_2)^2 = \tfrac14 (i\vec q)^2 = -\tfrac14 \vec q^2 = \tfrac14 |\vec r-\vec s|^2\, e_t . $$

Taking the trace, $\mathrm{Tr}((\tilde{Ϛ}_1-\tilde{Ϛ}_2)^2) = 2\cdot\tfrac14|\vec r-\vec s|^2 = \tfrac12|\vec r-\vec s|^2$, which is the parent's Hilbert–Schmidt formula. Since $(\tilde{Ϛ}_1-\tilde{Ϛ}_2)^2$ is a scalar multiple of $e_t$, the traceless difference $\tilde{Ϛ}_1-\tilde{Ϛ}_2$ has eigenvalues $\pm\tfrac12|\vec r-\vec s|$.

(b) The absolute value is

$$ |\tilde{Ϛ}_1-\tilde{Ϛ}_2| = \sqrt{(\tilde{Ϛ}_1-\tilde{Ϛ}_2)^2} = \tfrac12|\vec r-\vec s|\,e_t, $$

the positive square root in $\mathbb{M}_+$. Hence

$$ \boxed{\;D(\tilde{Ϛ}_1,\tilde{Ϛ}_2) = \tfrac12\mathrm{Tr}|\tilde{Ϛ}_1-\tilde{Ϛ}_2| = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12|\vec r-\vec s|\cdot\mathrm{Tr}(e_t) = \tfrac12|\vec r-\vec s|\;}. $$

Setting $\vec s = 0$ recovers the parent's statement $D(\tilde{Ϛ},\tfrac12 e_t) = \tfrac12|\vec r|$. Comparing with (a), the trace distance and the Hilbert–Schmidt distance carry the same information on a qubit,

$$ \mathrm{Tr}\bigl((\tilde{Ϛ}_1-\tilde{Ϛ}_2)^2\bigr) = 2\,D(\tilde{Ϛ}_1,\tilde{Ϛ}_2)^2, \qquad D = \sqrt{\tfrac12\,\mathrm{Tr}\bigl((\tilde{Ϛ}_1-\tilde{Ϛ}_2)^2\bigr)} . $$

Both are monotone functions of the Euclidean separation $|\vec r-\vec s|$; the trace distance is the one with the operational meaning, being half the trace-norm difference of the two states.

(c) For $\vec r = \tfrac12 \vec e_z$ and $\vec s = \tfrac12 \vec e_x$, $|\vec r-\vec s| = \tfrac12\sqrt2 = 1/\sqrt2$, so the Hilbert–Schmidt distance squared is $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac14$, and

$$ D = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac1{\sqrt2} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt2} \approx 0.353553 . $$

For two pure states $\vec r = \vec u$, $\vec s = \vec u_\nu$ at angle $\theta$, $|\vec r-\vec s| = \sqrt{2 - 2\cos\theta} = 2\sin(\theta/2)$, so

$$ D\bigl(\tilde\Pi(\vec u), \tilde\Pi(\vec u_\nu)\bigr) = \sin\frac{\theta}{2}. $$

Together with the transition probability $\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde\Pi(\vec u)\tilde\Pi(\vec u_\nu)^{*}) = \cos^2(\theta/2)$ this gives the clean pair

$$ D^2 + \mathrm{Tr}\bigl(\tilde\Pi(\vec u)\tilde\Pi(\vec u_\nu)\bigr) = 1 \qquad \text{for pure states,} $$

so the two notions of separation are complementary on the boundary: coincident directions give $D = 0$ and transition probability $1$, antipodal directions give $D = 1$ and transition probability $0$.

(d) Since $|\vec r-\vec s| \leq |\vec r| + |\vec s| \leq 2$, we have $0 \leq D \leq 1$. The upper bound is attained exactly when $|\vec r| = |\vec s| = 1$ and $\vec s = -\vec r$, the case of orthogonal states treated in Problem 6. The trace distance is thus bounded by the information-theoretic maximum $1$, the probability of perfectly distinguishing the two states, and it reaches it only at the two ends of a diameter.

Problem 5: The Centre and the Boundary

Problem. (a) Characterize the centre of the ball and compute its purity, entropies, and biquaternion norm. (b) Characterize the boundary and show that a boundary state is an extreme point that cannot be written as a nontrivial convex combination. (c) Show that the elements of the trace-one hyperplane with $|\vec r| > 1$ are not states.

Solution. (a) The centre is $\vec r = 0$, i.e.

$$ \tilde{Ϛ} = \tfrac12 e_t, $$

the maximally mixed state. Its purity is $\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{Ϛ}^2) = \tfrac12(1+0) = \tfrac12$, the minimum on the ball; its linear entropy is $\tfrac12$, the maximum; its biquaternion norm is $\tilde{Ϛ}\tilde{Ϛ}^{\natural} = \tfrac14 e_t$, whose scalar coefficient $\tfrac14$ is the largest attainable on the slice; and its von Neumann entropy is $\log 2$, the maximum. In the spectral form it is the equal mixture $\tfrac12\tilde\Pi_1(\vec u) + \tfrac12\tilde\Pi_2(\vec u)$ of the complementary idempotents along any axis, and it is the unique state invariant under the full $*$-unitary group $\mathrm{U}(2)$, which acts on the ball by rotations. It is also the barycentre: the uniform average of the pure states over the boundary sphere is

$$ \frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{S^2}\tilde\Pi(\vec u)\,d\Omega = \tfrac12\Bigl(e_t + i\,\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{S^2}\vec u\,d\Omega\Bigr) = \tfrac12 e_t, $$

because the mean of the unit vector over the sphere vanishes.

(b) The boundary is $|\vec r| = 1$. For these states the biquaternion norm vanishes,

$$ \boxed{\;\tilde{Ϛ}\tilde{Ϛ}^{\natural} = \tfrac14(1 - |\vec r|^2)e_t = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad |\vec r| = 1\;}, $$

so a boundary state is a zero divisor of $\mathbb{B}$; the deviation from idempotency,

$$ \tilde{Ϛ}^2 - \tilde{Ϛ} = \tfrac14\bigl(|\vec r|^2 - 1\bigr)e_t, $$

vanishes exactly there, so a boundary state is an idempotent, hence a rank-one projection; and its eigenvalues are $\lambda_\pm = 1, 0$, so its purity is $1$ and its entropy $0$. The boundary is parametrized by the unit sphere $S^2$: $\vec r = \vec u$ gives the idempotent $\tilde\Pi_1(\vec u)$, and $-\vec u$ gives its orthogonal complement $\tilde\Pi_2(\vec u)$, with $\tilde\Pi_1 + \tilde\Pi_2 = e_t$ and $\tilde\Pi_1\tilde\Pi_2 = 0$.

A boundary state is an extreme point of the ball. Suppose $\tilde{P} = \lambda\tilde{Ϛ}_1 + (1-\lambda)\tilde{Ϛ}_2$ with $0 < \lambda < 1$. Then $\vec r = \lambda\vec r_1 + (1-\lambda)\vec r_2$ with $|\vec r_1|, |\vec r_2| \leq 1$ and $|\vec r| = 1$. By the strict convexity of the Euclidean norm, equality $|\vec r| = 1$ forces $\vec r_1 = \vec r_2 = \vec r$, so $\tilde{Ϛ}_1 = \tilde{Ϛ}_2 = \tilde{P}$: no boundary state is a nontrivial mixture. Conversely, every interior state is a nontrivial mixture (Problem 3), so the extreme points of the ball are exactly the pure states.

(c) On the trace-one hyperplane an element $\tilde{Ϛ} = \tfrac12(e_t + i\vec r)$ has eigenvalues $\lambda_\pm = \tfrac12(1 \pm |\vec r|)$. If $|\vec r| > 1$ then $\lambda_- = \tfrac12(1 - |\vec r|) < 0$, so $\tilde{Ϛ}$ is Hermitian of trace one but not positive semidefinite, and is not a state. Its biquaternion norm is $\tfrac14(1 - |\vec r|^2)e_t$ with negative scalar coefficient, so it lies in the spacelike region outside the future cone. For instance $\vec r = 2\vec e_z$ gives $\lambda_\pm = \tfrac32, -\tfrac12$. The ball $|\vec r| \leq 1$ is thus exactly the positivity domain on the slice.

Problem 6: Identical and Orthogonal States

Problem. (a) Determine when two states are identical. (b) Determine when two states have orthogonal support, i.e. when $\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{Ϛ}_1\tilde{Ϛ}_2^{*}) = 0$. (c) Specialize to pure states and express the result in terms of the angle. (d) Show that the Uhlmann transition probability vanishes under the same condition.

Solution. (a) Two states are identical exactly when their Bloch vectors are equal: from $\tilde{Ϛ}_1 - \tilde{Ϛ}_2 = \tfrac12 i(\vec r-\vec s)$ we have $\tilde{Ϛ}_1 = \tilde{Ϛ}_2$ iff $\vec r = \vec s$. In that case the Hilbert–Schmidt and trace distances vanish, the transition probability is $1$, and all radius-dependent quantities (purity, entropies) coincide. Conversely, equal purity does not imply identical states: by Problem 2 all states of a given radius share the same purity and entropy, and they are rotated into one another by $\mathrm{U}(2)$.

(b) The Hermitian form on the slice is $\langle\tilde{Ϛ}_1,\tilde{Ϛ}_2\rangle_{*}=\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{Ϛ}_1\tilde{Ϛ}_2^{*})=\tfrac14(1 + \vec r\cdot\vec s)$, with trace reading at normalisation $\operatorname{Tr}(\tilde{Ϛ}_1\tilde{Ϛ}_2^{*}) = \tfrac12(1 + \vec r\cdot\vec s)$. Because $\tilde{Ϛ}_1,\tilde{Ϛ}_2$ are positive and of trace one, orthogonality of their supports is equivalent to the vanishing of this pairing. Thus

$$ \boxed{\;\langle\tilde{Ϛ}_1,\tilde{Ϛ}_2\rangle_{*}=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \operatorname{Tr}(\tilde{Ϛ}_1\tilde{Ϛ}_2^{*})=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \vec r\cdot\vec s = -1\;}. $$

Since $|\vec r\cdot\vec s| \leq |\vec r|\,|\vec s| \leq 1$, the equality $\vec r\cdot\vec s = -1$ forces both Cauchy–Schwarz and the ball bounds to be saturated:

$$ |\vec r| = |\vec s| = 1, \qquad \vec s = -\vec r. $$

So two qubit states have orthogonal supports if and only if they are the two complementary pure states along a common axis.

(c) For pure states $\vec r = \vec u$, $\vec s = \vec u_\nu$, the transition probability is the normalised Hermitian form, whose trace reading at normalisation is

$$ \frac{\langle\tilde\Pi(\vec u),\tilde\Pi(\vec u_\nu)\rangle_{*}}{\langle\tilde\Pi(\vec u_\nu),e_t\rangle_{*}} = \frac{\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde\Pi(\vec u)\tilde\Pi(\vec u_\nu)\bigr)}{\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde\Pi(\vec u_\nu)\bigr)} = \frac{\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\tilde\Pi(\vec u)\tilde\Pi(\vec u_\nu)\bigr)}{\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\tilde\Pi(\vec u_\nu)\bigr)} = \tfrac12\bigl(1 + \vec u\cdot\vec u_\nu\bigr) = \cos^2\frac{\theta}{2}, $$

where $\theta$ is the angle between the Bloch directions. It equals $1$ for $\theta = 0$ (identical states) and $0$ for $\theta = \pi$ (orthogonal states, $\vec u_\nu = -\vec u$). Equivalently, $\vec u\cdot\vec u_\nu = -1$, the same condition as in (b). As a concrete check, take $\vec u = \vec e_z$, $\vec u_\nu = -\vec e_z$: then $\tilde\Pi_1(\vec e_z)\tilde\Pi_2(\vec e_z) = 0$ and $\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde\Pi_1(\vec e_z)\tilde\Pi_2(\vec e_z)^{*}) = \tfrac12(1 - 1) = 0$, while $\tilde\Pi_1 + \tilde\Pi_2 = e_t$.

(d) For general states the relevant overlap is the Uhlmann transition probability,

$$ T(\tilde{Ϛ}_1,\tilde{Ϛ}_2) = \tfrac12\Bigl(1 + \vec r\cdot\vec s + \sqrt{\bigl(1-|\vec r|^2\bigr)\bigl(1-|\vec s|^2\bigr)}\Bigr), $$

which for mixed states differs from the Hilbert–Schmidt pairing $\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{Ϛ}_1\tilde{Ϛ}_2^{*})$. Setting $T = 0$ gives

$$ \vec r\cdot\vec s = -1 - \sqrt{\bigl(1-|\vec r|^2\bigr)\bigl(1-|\vec s|^2\bigr)} \leq -1 . $$

Combined with $\vec r\cdot\vec s \geq -|\vec r|\,|\vec s| \geq -1$, every inequality is an equality, so $|\vec r| = |\vec s| = 1$, $\vec r\cdot\vec s = -1$, and $\vec s = -\vec r$ — the same pure antipodal pair. Hence the transition probability vanishes under exactly the same condition as orthogonal support, and for mixed states it is strictly positive: a mixed state is never perfectly distinguishable from another. For example, with $\vec r = \tfrac12 \vec e_z$ and $\vec s = \tfrac12 \vec e_x$ one has $\vec r\cdot\vec s = 0$ and $\sqrt{(1-\tfrac14)(1-\tfrac14)} = \tfrac34$, so $T = \tfrac12(1 + \tfrac34) = \tfrac78$, consistent with the nonzero trace distance $D = 1/(2\sqrt2)$ found in Problem 4.

Summary

We have worked six problems on the geometry of the qubit state space, using only the state-space results of the parent article.

Parametrisation. A state is $\tilde{Ϛ} = \tfrac12(e_t + i\vec r)$ with $|\vec r| \leq 1$; the components of $\vec r$ are the single-qubit expectation values $r_k = \langle\sigma_k\rangle$, and a measurement along $\vec u$ has outcomes $\tfrac12(1 \pm \vec u\cdot\vec r)$.

Purity and entropy. Purity, linear entropy, and von Neumann entropy are functions of the radius alone: $\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{Ϛ}^2) = \tfrac12(1+|\vec r|^2)$, $S_{\mathrm{lin}} = \tfrac12(1-|\vec r|^2)$, and $S = -\sum_\pm\lambda_\pm\log\lambda_\pm$ with $\lambda_\pm = \tfrac12(1\pm|\vec r|)$.

Convex combinations. A mixture of two pure states lies on the chord joining them, with $|\vec r|^2 = 1 - 2p(1-p)(1-\cos\theta)$ and linear entropy $p(1-p)(1-\cos\theta)$; it is pure only in the degenerate cases $p \in \{0,1\}$ or coincident directions.

Metric. The trace distance is $D = \tfrac12|\vec r-\vec s|$ and the squared Hilbert–Schmidt distance is $2D^2$. For pure states $D = \sin(\theta/2)$ and the transition probability is $\cos^2(\theta/2)$, so $D^2 + \mathrm{Tr}\bigl(\tilde\Pi(\vec r)\tilde\Pi(\vec s)\bigr) = 1$.

Centre and boundary. The centre is the maximally mixed state $\tfrac12 e_t$, with minimal purity $\tfrac12$ and maximal entropy $\log 2$; the boundary $|\vec r| = 1$ consists of the pure states, equivalently the idempotents, the zero divisors, and the extreme points. Elements with $|\vec r| > 1$ are not states.

Identical and orthogonal. Two states are identical iff $\vec r = \vec s$; they have orthogonal supports — and the Uhlmann transition probability vanishes — iff $\vec r\cdot\vec s = -1$, which forces $|\vec r| = |\vec s| = 1$ and $\vec s = -\vec r$. Only the two ends of a diameter are perfectly distinguishable.
