The Complementary Space of the Spinor Space and Its Physical Meaning

Introduction

The companion article The Spinor Space as a Module and as a Hilbert Space states what the spinor space $S=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+$ carries: a module structure from the general plain algebra and a Hilbert structure from the general plain sesqualgebra. This article is about the other half of the algebra. The left regular module splits,

$$ \mathbb{B} = \mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+ \oplus \mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_- = S \oplus \mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-, $$

and the second summand, the minimal left ideal generated by $\tilde\Pi_-=\tfrac12(e_0-ie_3)$, is the complementary space of the spinor space. It is the subject of this article.

The complement turns out to be more than "the other column". Two independent descriptions of it coincide. It is the module complement of $S$ — the other Peirce summand — and it is at the same time the Hilbert complement of $S$, the orthogonal complement for the sesquilinear form of the companion article. The two facts are proved separately and they agree, so the algebra's decomposition of $\mathbb{B}$ into two minimal left ideals is also an orthogonal decomposition of a Hilbert space into two two-dimensional pieces. That coincidence is the algebraic core of the article.

The physical interpretation is the reason the complement matters. The algebra carries two copies of the same state space, not one, both of them Hilbert spaces and both isomorphic as modules; an operator can therefore relate them, and the corpus's chirality-off-diagonal mass term is exactly such an operator. The complement is where the mass takes a state, and different corpus conventions name the doubling differently — a second chirality, a particle–hole pair, or the conjugate state space. The article states the algebra, then the interpretations.

The conventions are those of the companion articles: $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$, the basis $e_0=1,e_1,e_2,e_3$ with $e_k^2=-e_0$ and $e_1e_2=e_3$, the scalar imaginary $i$, the conjugations $\tilde Q^{\natural}$, $\tilde Q^{*}$ and $\tilde Q\mapsto\bar{\tilde Q}$ (conjugation of the four coefficients), and the sectors $\mathbb{M}_\pm$. The form is the general plain sesquilinear form $\langle u,v\rangle_*=\mathrm{Sc}(uv^{*})$, conjugate-linear in its second argument.

The Complement as an Ideal

The Idempotent and Its Complement

With

$$ \tilde\Pi_+=\tfrac12(e_0+ie_3),\qquad \tilde\Pi_-=\tfrac12(e_0-ie_3), $$

the two idempotents are orthogonal and complete in the plain product,

$$ \tilde\Pi_+^2=\tilde\Pi_+,\qquad \tilde\Pi_-^2=\tilde\Pi_-,\qquad \tilde\Pi_+\tilde\Pi_-=\tilde\Pi_-\tilde\Pi_+=0,\qquad \tilde\Pi_++\tilde\Pi_-=e_0 . $$

Here $ie_3$ is a root of $+1$, $(ie_3)^2=e_0$, and $\tilde\Pi_+$ and $\tilde\Pi_-$ are its two associated idempotents.

The relation $\tilde\Pi_-=e_0-\tilde\Pi_+$ is the algebraic form of the word complementary: the second idempotent is the complement of the first in the algebra's own unit. Both are Hermitian, both are projectors of unit trace, both are primitive — each generates a minimal left ideal — and the two resolve the identity (The Biquaternion Vacuum as a Minimal Idempotent).

The Two Summands

The complementary space is the second ideal,

$$ \mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_-,\hat\Upsilon_1\},\qquad \hat\Upsilon_1=\tfrac12(ie_1-e_2), $$

of complex dimension two, generated by $\tilde\Pi_-$ and closed under left multiplication. The two ideals are the two columns of the matrix model $\mathsf{M}_2(e_k)=-i\sigma_k$, in which $\tilde\Pi_+,\hat\Upsilon_1,\hat\Upsilon_2,\tilde\Pi_-$ are the matrix units $E_{11},E_{12},E_{21},E_{22}$:

$$ S=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+ \leftrightarrow \text{first column } \mathbb{C}\{E_{11},E_{21}\},\qquad \mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_- \leftrightarrow \text{second column } \mathbb{C}\{E_{12},E_{22}\}. $$

Each column is the sum of one diagonal Peirce corner and one off-diagonal corner, so each ideal's basis holds exactly one idempotent and one nilpotent: the reason is the subject of §Why the Basis Is One Idempotent and One Nilpotent of the companion article, and the complement's basis $(\tilde\Pi_-,\hat\Upsilon_1)$ is the mirror case of the spinor space's $(\tilde\Pi_+,\hat\Upsilon_2)$.

The Multiplication of the Complement

The two ideals share one multiplication table, the table of The Biquaternion Vacuum as a Minimal Idempotent and Ideals and Peirce Decomposition of the General Plain Algebra. On the complement's own basis $(\tilde\Pi_-,\hat\Upsilon_1)$ the left multiplications are

$$ L_{\tilde\Pi_+}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad L_{\tilde\Pi_-}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad L_{\hat\Upsilon_1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad L_{\hat\Upsilon_2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}, $$

so the complement is closed under every left multiplication, as a left ideal must be. The pattern is the mirror of the spinor space: on the basis $(\tilde\Pi_+,\hat\Upsilon_2)$ of $S$ the matrices are $\mathrm{diag}(1,0),\mathrm{diag}(0,1),E_{12},E_{21}$, so the left-action matrices on the complement are those on $S$ under the interchange of generators $\tilde\Pi_+\leftrightarrow\tilde\Pi_-$ and $\hat\Upsilon_1\leftrightarrow\hat\Upsilon_2$. That relabelling is a statement about the two actions, not a map between the modules: the module isomorphism $S\to\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$ is right multiplication by $\hat\Upsilon_1$, which carries $\tilde\Pi_+\mapsto\hat\Upsilon_1$ and $\hat\Upsilon_2\mapsto\tilde\Pi_-$ (§Its Module Structure).

The Two Complements Coincide

The Module Complement

The module complement of $S$ is immediate. The Peirce decomposition of $\mathbb{B}$ with respect to the pair $(\tilde\Pi_+,\tilde\Pi_-)$ cuts the algebra into four one-complex-dimensional corners,

$$ \tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_+,\qquad \tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-=\mathbb{C}\hat\Upsilon_1,\qquad \tilde\Pi_-\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+=\mathbb{C}\hat\Upsilon_2,\qquad \tilde\Pi_-\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_-, $$

and produces the direct sum

$$ \mathbb{B}=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+\oplus\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-=S\oplus\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_- $$

of left modules. The complement of $S$ in this sense is $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$, the only proper direct summand.

The Hilbert Complement

The Hilbert complement is the orthogonal complement for the form of the companion article,

$$ S^{\perp}=\{v\in\mathbb{B}:\langle u,v\rangle_*=0\ \text{for all}\ u\in S\}. $$

It is computed on the four matrix units. The form is the normalised Hilbert–Schmidt form of the matrix model, and the four units are mutually orthogonal of norm $\tfrac12$:

$$ \langle\tilde\Pi_+,\tilde\Pi_+\rangle_*=\langle\hat\Upsilon_2,\hat\Upsilon_2\rangle_*=\langle\hat\Upsilon_1,\hat\Upsilon_1\rangle_*=\langle\tilde\Pi_-,\tilde\Pi_-\rangle_*=\tfrac12,\qquad \langle\tilde P,\tilde R\rangle_*=0\ \text{for}\ \tilde P\neq\tilde R\ \text{among the four}. $$

Every element of $\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_+,\hat\Upsilon_2\}$ is therefore orthogonal to every element of $\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_-,\hat\Upsilon_1\}$, and since both are two-dimensional while $\mathbb{B}$ is four-dimensional,

$$ S^{\perp}=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_-,\hat\Upsilon_1\}=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_- . $$

The Orthogonal Direct Sum

The two computations agree:

Proposition. The module complement and the Hilbert complement of the spinor space are the same subspace, $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$, and the algebra decomposes both ways at once, $$ > \mathbb{B}=S\oplus\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-=S\perp\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-, > $$ a direct sum of left modules and an orthogonal direct sum of Hilbert spaces.

The decomposition is a Peirce decomposition and an orthogonal decomposition simultaneously. The same two idempotents that split the module split the metric, because in the matrix model the Peirce corners are the matrix units and the matrix units are orthonormal. This is why the two copies of the module are not merely isomorphic but also independent: no state of one has a component in the other, in the sense of the form.

The Complement's Own Two Structures

Its Module Structure

The complement carries the same two structures as $S$, by the same arguments. It is a left module of the general plain algebra: it is closed under the plain left action by the table of §The Multiplication of the Complement, it is minimal, and it is isomorphic to $S$. The isomorphism is realised concretely by right multiplication by the nilpotent $\hat\Upsilon_1$ (§Right Multiplication by the Nilpotents),

$$ \tilde\Pi_+\hat\Upsilon_1=\hat\Upsilon_1,\qquad \hat\Upsilon_2\hat\Upsilon_1=\tilde\Pi_-, $$

which carries the basis $(\tilde\Pi_+,\hat\Upsilon_2)$ of $S$ to the basis $(\hat\Upsilon_1,\tilde\Pi_-)$ of the complement. A caution belongs here: the naive "basis swap" $\tilde\Pi_+\leftrightarrow\tilde\Pi_-$, $\hat\Upsilon_2\leftrightarrow\hat\Upsilon_1$ is not a module map, because it does not commute with the left action ($\tilde\Pi_-\tilde\Pi_+=0$ while $\tilde\Pi_-\tilde\Pi_-=\tilde\Pi_-$); the module isomorphism is right multiplication, not the relabelling. Both ideals are simple and both are isomorphic to $S$; by the uniqueness of the simple module of $\mathbb{B}$ there is no other left module for them to be.

Its Hilbert Structure

It is also a Hilbert space of the general plain sesqualgebra. On its own basis $(\tilde\Pi_-,\hat\Upsilon_1)$ the Gram matrix of the restricted form is

$$ G'=\begin{pmatrix}\langle\tilde\Pi_-,\tilde\Pi_-\rangle_*&\langle\tilde\Pi_-,\hat\Upsilon_1\rangle_*\\[2pt]\langle\hat\Upsilon_1,\tilde\Pi_-\rangle_*&\langle\hat\Upsilon_1,\hat\Upsilon_1\rangle_*\end{pmatrix}=\tfrac12 I_2, $$

the same as on $S$. So the complement is a two-dimensional Hilbert space with the same metric, and the total state space $\mathbb{B}$ is the orthogonal direct sum of two identical copies of it. The complement is not a dual (that is the right ideal, §Not the Dual) and not a twisted copy; it is a second, metrically identical, module-identical copy.

Its Own Two Amplitudes

A general element of the complement is

$$ \tilde\phi=\phi_1\tilde\Pi_-+\phi_2\hat\Upsilon_1,\qquad \phi_1,\phi_2\in\mathbb{C}, $$

with norm

$$ \langle\tilde\phi,\tilde\phi\rangle_*=\tfrac12\bigl(|\phi_1|^2+|\phi_2|^2\bigr), $$

the same expression as for the spinor of $S$ with the coefficients $(\phi_1,\phi_2)$ carried by the other pair of basis elements. The complement has its own two amplitudes, its own two-component coordinate system, and its own normalisation.

What the Complement Is Not

Not the Dual

The complement is a left ideal. The dual of $S$ — the space of the bras — is reached by the Hermitian conjugation ${}^{*}$, which sends

$$ \tilde\Pi_+\mapsto\tilde\Pi_+,\qquad \hat\Upsilon_2\mapsto\hat\Upsilon_1, $$

so that

$$ S^{*}=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_+,\hat\Upsilon_1\}=\tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}, $$

the right ideal: the first row of the matrix model, $\mathbb{C}\{E_{11},E_{12}\}$. The dual of the spinor space is therefore not its complement. The two operations — reversing the arrows, which is ${}^{*}$ and produces the dual, and conjugating, which is $\bar{\cdot}$ and produces the complement — act differently on the same space. This is the algebraic form of the standard caution that a bra is not an antiparticle. A caution on the two words: the dual $S^{*}$ is the right ideal $\tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}$ and the conjugate $\bar S$ is the second left ideal $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$, so the two reversals name different objects and must not be exchanged. (That the defining representation is self-dual in the representation-theoretic sense, $S\cong S^{\vee}$ through the invariant symplectic form, is a third statement and does not identify the dual with the complement.)

Not a Second Representation

The complement is isomorphic to $S$ as a left module, so it carries the same defining representation of the double cover. The set of left-action matrices on the basis $(\tilde\Pi_-,\hat\Upsilon_1)$ is the set of those on the basis $(\tilde\Pi_+,\hat\Upsilon_2)$, with the two idempotents and the two nilpotents interchanged as generators; that relabelling is a coordinate statement, and the module-theoretic content is simply that the two representations are equivalent. The two ideals are therefore not two different representations and not the two chiral halves of the Lorentz group in the module-theoretic sense: the chiral halves are $S$ and its conjugate module $\bar S$, which are non-isomorphic as complex representations, whereas the two ideals are isomorphic. A statement that the two ideals carry different physics is a statement about a different product, about a real structure, or about one of the corpus's other conventions for the word chirality (§The Mass Couples the Two, §Conventions and Traps).

Reached by the Conjugate, Not the Adjoint

The map that does carry $S$ onto the complement is the coefficient conjugation $\tilde Q\mapsto\bar{\tilde Q}$:

$$ \overline{\tilde\Pi_+}=\tilde\Pi_-,\qquad \overline{\hat\Upsilon_2}=-\hat\Upsilon_1, $$

so

$$ \bar S=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_-,\hat\Upsilon_1\}=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_- . $$

The complement is thus the complex conjugate of the spinor space, and the conjugation is an anti-linear real structure on $\mathbb{B}$ that exchanges the two ideals. Stated as a pair: $S^{*}$ is the dual and $\bar S$ is the complement, and $\mathbb{B}=S\perp\bar S$.

The Map That Reaches the Complement

Coefficient Conjugation

The conjugation $\bar{\cdot}$ fixes the four basis elements $e_\mu$ and negates the scalar imaginary $i$; it is an anti-linear involution of $\mathbb{B}$ and a real structure in the sense that it is not complex-linear. It exchanges the two idempotents and the two ideals, so it is the algebraic operation that the word complementary names, and it is the reason the complement is naturally read as a conjugate sector.

Right Multiplication by the Nilpotents

A second, inner map reaches the complement, and it is a genuine module isomorphism rather than an anti-linear exchange. Right multiplication by the nilpotent $\hat\Upsilon_1$ sends the spinor basis to the complement basis,

$$ \tilde\Pi_+\hat\Upsilon_1=\hat\Upsilon_1,\qquad \hat\Upsilon_2\hat\Upsilon_1=\tilde\Pi_-, $$

so

$$ S\,\hat\Upsilon_1=\mathbb{C}\{\hat\Upsilon_1,\tilde\Pi_-\}=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_- . $$

Because it is a right multiplication it commutes with the left action, $\tilde Q(x\hat\Upsilon_1)=(\tilde Qx)\hat\Upsilon_1$, so it is a $\mathbb{B}$-linear map $S\to\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$; being nonzero between simple modules of the same dimension it is an isomorphism. Symmetrically $\hat\Upsilon_2$ carries the complement back to $S$. This is the concrete algebra behind the corpus's statement that the mass term is a right multiplication, "which is why it can relate the two minimal left ideals while a central scalar cannot" (Bosonization in Biquaternionic Form). The reason a central element cannot is that it commutes with everything and acts as the same scalar on both ideals, so its right action equals its left action and leaves each ideal in place; the nilpotent's right action is not a left action, and it is the asymmetry between the two that moves a state across.

The Physical Meaning of the Complement

The Second Copy of the State Space

The first physical fact is a counting one. The algebra is two copies of the state space, $\mathbb{B}\cong S\oplus S$ as left modules and $\mathbb{B}=S\perp S$ as Hilbert spaces, and an operator can relate the two copies. This is the algebraic origin of the framework's two-component spinor structure: the qubit's state vectors occupy one copy, and the second copy is what allows an operator to mix the two components (The Native Qubit and the Defining Module of the Biquaternion Algebra). The complement is that second copy. The physical interpretation is that the framework's state space is doubled by construction, and the doubling is available for any operator that acts on the right.

The Occupation Reading

The two ideals can be read as the two levels of a single mode. Writing the matrix units as occupation projectors,

$$ \tilde\Pi_+=|0\rangle\langle0|,\qquad \tilde\Pi_-=|1\rangle\langle1|,\qquad \hat\Upsilon_1=|0\rangle\langle1|,\qquad \hat\Upsilon_2=|1\rangle\langle0|, $$

the spinor space is the ideal of the level $|0\rangle$ and the complement is the ideal of the level $|1\rangle$. The two summands of $\mathbb{B}$ are then the two choices of which level generates the module, and the second choice is the complement. The physical interpretation is a level/occupation doubling: the ground and the excited ideal, or the empty and occupied ideal of a single fermionic mode (The Harmonic Oscillator in Biquaternionic Form, Fock Space and Creation–Annihilation Operators in Biquaternionic Form). The nilpotents that exchange the two ideals are the raising and lowering operators of the mode.

The Conjugate Reading and the Dual Reading

Because the complement is $\bar S$ and not $S^{*}$, the two operations have different physical names. The dual $S^{*}$ is the space of bras, the linear functionals, and it exists for any module; the complement $\bar S$ is the conjugate state space, reached by conjugating the coefficients. The framework therefore separates the two reversals: the adjoint reverses the algebra and gives the dual, the conjugation reverses the coefficients and gives the complement. The physical interpretation is that a bra is not an antiparticle: the conjugate sector is the complement, and the dual is a different object with a different algebraic origin.

The Mass Couples the Two

The corpus's Dirac mass is linear and chirality-off-diagonal, and it is a right multiplication,

$$ \tilde\nabla\tilde\Psi_R=m\tilde\Psi_L,\qquad \tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde\Psi_L=m\tilde\Psi_R , $$

so the mass is precisely an operator of the kind that §Right Multiplication by the Nilpotents shows relates the two ideals. The physical interpretation is that the mass is the coupling of the state space to its complement, and the complement is where a massive state takes its other component. In the spectral-triple interpretation of The Spectral Triple of the Biquaternion Algebra the same fact appears as the off-diagonal finite Dirac operator that couples the two minimal left ideals and is odd under the chirality; there the two ideals are named the two chiralities, a convention distinct from the module-level chirality $S$ versus $\bar S$ of §Not a Second Representation (see §Conventions and Traps). Both conventions describe the one fact: the complement is the second component that the mass mixes in.

The Other Vacuum

The choice of idempotent selects the module, and it is a choice: the vacuum idempotent $\tilde\Pi_+$ generates $S$, and the complementary idempotent $\tilde\Pi_-$ generates $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$ (The Biquaternion Vacuum as a Minimal Idempotent). The module is unique but the vacuum is not, so the complement is what the other choice of vacuum produces. The physical interpretation is that the doubling of the state space is the doubling of the vacuum choice: the two ideals are the two one-mode vacua, and no algebraic fact prefers one of them.

The Sectors Cut Across the Two Ideals

The complement is not a sector. Each of the two ideals meets each of the sectors $\mathbb{M}_\pm$ in exactly one real dimension:

$$ S\cap\mathbb{M}_+=\mathbb{R}\{\tilde\Pi_+\},\qquad S\cap\mathbb{M}_-=\mathbb{R}\{i\tilde\Pi_+\},\qquad \mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-\cap\mathbb{M}_+=\mathbb{R}\{\tilde\Pi_-\},\qquad \mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-\cap\mathbb{M}_-=\mathbb{R}\{i\tilde\Pi_-\}. $$

The material–informational split therefore cuts across both ideals rather than aligning with them: neither ideal is material and neither is informational, and each carries one real direction of each sector. The physical interpretation is that the complement is a second copy of the state space and not a second sector, so the two structures of the algebra — the sector split and the ideal split — are independent, and a physical statement that uses one must say which.

Which Peirce Corner Carries the Sectors

The one real dimension of each intersection is carried entirely by the diagonal corner. On the two Peirce lines of $S$ the intersections are

$$ \mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_+\cap\mathbb{M}_+=\mathbb{R}\{\tilde\Pi_+\},\qquad \mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_+\cap\mathbb{M}_-=\mathbb{R}\{i\tilde\Pi_+\},\qquad \mathbb{C}\hat\Upsilon_2\cap\mathbb{M}_+=\mathbb{C}\hat\Upsilon_2\cap\mathbb{M}_-=\{0\}, $$

so the idempotent line supplies one real direction to each sector and the nilpotent line supplies none: the diagonal corner is $(1+1)$ in the two sectors, the off-diagonal corner is $(0+0)$. The mirror statement holds on the complement, with $(\tilde\Pi_-,\hat\Upsilon_1)$ in place of $(\tilde\Pi_+,\hat\Upsilon_2)$. The nilpotent direction is sector-neutral because the sector test splits an element into its Hermitian and anti-Hermitian parts: for $\hat\Upsilon_2=\tfrac12(ie_1+e_2)$ these are

$$ \tfrac12\bigl(\hat\Upsilon_2+\hat\Upsilon_2^{*}\bigr)=\tfrac12 ie_1\in\mathbb{M}_+,\qquad \tfrac12\bigl(\hat\Upsilon_2-\hat\Upsilon_2^{*}\bigr)=\tfrac12 e_2\in\mathbb{M}_-, $$

and neither lies in the line $\mathbb{C}\hat\Upsilon_2$. The physical interpretation is that a sector label attaches to the projector direction of an ideal and to no part of the raising and lowering direction: the ideal's idempotent is the informational datum, and the nilpotent that shifts the level carries none.

Why the Ideal Split Can Never Be a Sector Split

The independence is not an accident of the coordinate pair, and it is worth stating as a prohibition, because the reverse is tempting once the ideal has been read as a two-component space.

Proposition. Multiplication by the imaginary scalar swaps the two sectors, $i\,\mathbb{M}_+=\mathbb{M}_-$ and $i\,\mathbb{M}_-=\mathbb{M}_+$, and the only complex subspace of $\mathbb{B}$ contained in a sector is $\{0\}$.

Proof. The conjugation negates the scalar imaginary, $i^{*}=-i$, since it conjugates the coefficients. Being anti-multiplicative and real-linear, $(i\tilde Q)^{*}=\overline{i}\,\tilde Q^{*}=-i\,\tilde Q^{*}$, so $\tilde Q^{*}=\pm\tilde Q$ gives $(i\tilde Q)^{*}=\mp(i\tilde Q)$ and exchanges the two signs. A complex subspace $V$ satisfies $iV\subseteq V$; if $V\subseteq\mathbb{M}_+$ then the exchange puts $iV$ in $\mathbb{M}_-$ and the containment puts it back in $\mathbb{M}_+$, so $V\subseteq\mathbb{M}_+\cap\mathbb{M}_-=\{0\}$, and the case $V\subseteq\mathbb{M}_-$ is the same.

Consequence. The two ideals are complex two-dimensional and they can never be split as the two sectors: $S$ is a $\mathbb{C}^2$, but its $\mathbb{C}^2$ splits along the Peirce corners of §The Two Summands and never along $\mathbb{M}_\pm$. A second consequence sharpens the complement. The Peirce complement of a Hermitian idempotent is Hermitian again — $\tilde\Pi_-=e_0-\tilde\Pi_+$ and $e_0^{*}=e_0$ give $\tilde\Pi_-^{*}=\tilde\Pi_-$ — so the complement of $\tilde\Pi_+$ stays in $\mathbb{M}_+$ and not in $\mathbb{M}_-$. There is in fact no nonzero idempotent in $\mathbb{M}_-$: if $p^2=p$ and $p^{*}=-p$ then $-p=p^{*}=(p^{2})^{*}=(p^{*})^{2}=p^{2}=p$, so $p=0$. The sector that the word complement might suggest is therefore empty of projectors, and the two roles — the complement in the Peirce sense and the complement in the sector sense — are different objects. The structure that is compatible with a module over the sectors is the grading they induce on the symmetrised product, and it is stated in the companion article The Spinor Space as a Module and as a Hilbert Space, §The Sector Split Is a Third Structure, and It Is Real.

Conventions and Traps

  • The complement is a left ideal, the dual is a right ideal. $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$ (second column) is the complement; $\tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}$ (first row) is the dual of $S$. They are different subspaces and different objects.
  • The complement is reached by $\bar{\cdot}$, not by ${}^{*}$. $\bar S=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$; $S^{*}=\tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}$. Confusing the two confuses conjugation with duality.
  • Isomorphic, not identical. The complement is isomorphic to $S$ as a left module and metrically identical as a Hilbert space, but it is a different subspace of $\mathbb{B}$; the module isomorphism is right multiplication by $\hat\Upsilon_1$, and the anti-linear conjugation $\bar{\cdot}$ also carries $S$ onto it.
  • Two conventions for the word chirality. At the module level the two chiral halves are $S$ and $\bar S$ and the two ideals are not the two chiralities, since they are isomorphic; in the spectral-triple and chirality-off-diagonal-mass conventions the two ideals are called the two chiralities. The two uses coexist in the corpus; a reader should check which is meant.
  • The complement is not a sector. Neither ideal is contained in $\mathbb{M}_+$ or $\mathbb{M}_-$; each meets each sector in one real dimension, all of it carried by the diagonal Peirce corner, while the nilpotent direction meets neither sector.
  • No complex subspace lies in a sector. Multiplication by $i$ swaps $\mathbb{M}_+$ and $\mathbb{M}_-$, so a complex subspace contained in a sector is $\{0\}$ and a complex two-dimensional ideal can never split as the two sectors. The Peirce complement $\tilde\Pi_-$ is Hermitian and lies in $\mathbb{M}_+$; $\mathbb{M}_-$ holds no nonzero idempotent.
  • Idempotent does not mean Hermitian. The two idempotents of the pair that generate the two ideals are Hermitian and lie in $\mathbb{M}_+$, and it is their Hermiticity — not their idempotence — that makes the module complement and the Hilbert complement coincide. A general idempotent $\tfrac12(e_0+\tilde\Xi)$ with $\tilde\Xi^2=e_0$ need not be Hermitian: the roots outside the purely imaginary family give a four-real-parameter family lying in none of the four four-dimensional remarkable subspaces, and for such a $\tilde\Pi$ the two summands are still complementary left modules while $\langle\tilde\Pi,e_0-\tilde\Pi\rangle_*\neq0$ — for $\tilde\Xi=\sqrt3\,e_1+2ie_3$ the value is $-\tfrac32$ — so the two complements separate (Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the General Plain Algebra).
  • Right multiplication does not preserve the ideals. The ideal decomposition is stable under the left action and not under the right; this asymmetry is what lets a right multiplication (the mass) relate the two ideals while a left multiplication (the Lorentz action) does not.

Boundaries

  • Cited rather than re-derived. The idempotents, the Peirce decomposition and the ideals are Ideals and Peirce Decomposition of the General Plain Algebra, Modules over the General Plain Algebra of Biquaternions and The Biquaternion Vacuum as a Minimal Idempotent; the two structures of a minimal left ideal are the companion article The Spinor Space as a Module and as a Hilbert Space; the module and its Lorentz action are The Spinor Module in Biquaternionic Form and Its Lorentz Action; the form is The Canonical Hermitian Form on the Regular Module of the Biquaternion Algebra; the mass and the spectral triple are Bosonization in Biquaternionic Form and The Spectral Triple of the Biquaternion Algebra.
  • Not claimed. The article identifies no physical degree of freedom with the complement; it does not choose between the occupation, conjugate and chirality interpretations of the doubling; and it states the two coincident complements of the spinor space, not a new structure of the algebra.
  • Narrower than the general vacuum frontier. Only the pair $(\tilde\Pi_+,\tilde\Pi_-)$, the two coordinate columns, is treated. The general idempotent $\tilde\Pi(\hat u)=\tfrac12(e_0+i\hat u)$ generates a minimal left ideal for every unit vector $\hat u$, and the corresponding complement is a different subspace; the family is the projective line of Modules over the General Plain Algebra of Biquaternions and is left there.

Worked Verification

The claims are recomputed on the basis $(\tilde\Pi_+,\hat\Upsilon_2,\hat\Upsilon_1,\tilde\Pi_-)$ and on the matrix units $E_{ij}$.

  • The idempotents and the complement. $\tilde\Pi_+^2=\tilde\Pi_+$, $\tilde\Pi_-^2=\tilde\Pi_-$, $\tilde\Pi_+\tilde\Pi_-=\tilde\Pi_-\tilde\Pi_+=0$, $\tilde\Pi_++\tilde\Pi_-=e_0$, so $\tilde\Pi_-=e_0-\tilde\Pi_+$.
  • The complement is a left ideal. Every left multiplication maps $\{\tilde\Pi_-,\hat\Upsilon_1\}$ into $\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_-,\hat\Upsilon_1\}$, and the action matrices are those of §The Multiplication of the Complement.
  • The two complements coincide. $\langle\tilde P,\tilde R\rangle_*=0$ for $\tilde P\in\{\tilde\Pi_+,\hat\Upsilon_2\}$ and $\tilde R\in\{\tilde\Pi_-,\hat\Upsilon_1\}$, and $\dim_{\mathbb{C}}S=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-=2$, so $S^{\perp}=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$.
  • The four units are orthonormal up to $\tfrac12$. $\langle\tilde P,\tilde P\rangle_*=\tfrac12$ on each of the four matrix units and $0$ between any two distinct ones, so the form is $\tfrac12$ times the Hilbert–Schmidt form of the matrix model.
  • The conjugate reaches it, the adjoint does not. $\bar{\tilde\Pi_+}=\tilde\Pi_-$ and $\bar{\hat\Upsilon_2}=-\hat\Upsilon_1$, so $\bar S=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$; while $\tilde\Pi_+^{*}=\tilde\Pi_+$ and $\hat\Upsilon_2^{*}=\hat\Upsilon_1$, so $S^{*}=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_+,\hat\Upsilon_1\}=\tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}\neq\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$.
  • Right multiplication exchanges the ideals. $\tilde\Pi_+\hat\Upsilon_1=\hat\Upsilon_1$, $\hat\Upsilon_2\hat\Upsilon_1=\tilde\Pi_-$, so $S\hat\Upsilon_1=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$; symmetrically $\tilde\Pi_-\hat\Upsilon_2=\hat\Upsilon_2$, $\hat\Upsilon_1\hat\Upsilon_2=\tilde\Pi_+$, so $(\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-)\hat\Upsilon_2=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+$.
  • The sector intersections. With $\mathbb{M}_\pm$ the fixed spaces of $\pm{}^{*}$, one has $S\cap\mathbb{M}_+=\mathbb{R}\{\tilde\Pi_+\}$, $S\cap\mathbb{M}_-=\mathbb{R}\{i\tilde\Pi_+\}$, $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-\cap\mathbb{M}_+=\mathbb{R}\{\tilde\Pi_-\}$, $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-\cap\mathbb{M}_-=\mathbb{R}\{i\tilde\Pi_-\}$, one real dimension each.
  • The sector obstruction. Multiplication by $i$ swaps the sectors on each of the four basis elements, $i\,\mathbb{M}_+=\mathbb{M}_-$ and $i\,\mathbb{M}_-=\mathbb{M}_+$, so no nonzero complex subspace lies in a sector; $\mathbb{C}\hat\Upsilon_2$ meets neither sector while $\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_+$ meets $\mathbb{M}_+$ in $\mathbb{R}\{\tilde\Pi_+\}$ and $\mathbb{M}_-$ in $\mathbb{R}\{i\tilde\Pi_+\}$, and $\tfrac12(\hat\Upsilon_2+\hat\Upsilon_2^{*})=\tfrac12 ie_1$, $\tfrac12(\hat\Upsilon_2-\hat\Upsilon_2^{*})=\tfrac12 e_2$ lie outside the line $\mathbb{C}\hat\Upsilon_2$. Also $\tilde\Pi_-^{*}=\tilde\Pi_-$, so the Peirce complement lies in $\mathbb{M}_+$ and not in $\mathbb{M}_-$.
  • Hermiticity is what makes the two complements coincide. For the Hermitian pair $\langle\tilde\Pi_+,\tilde\Pi_-\rangle_*=0$; but the non-Hermitian idempotent $\tilde\Pi=\tfrac12(e_0+\tilde\Xi)$ with $\tilde\Xi=\sqrt3\,e_1+2ie_3$ satisfies $\tilde\Xi^2=e_0$, $\tilde\Pi^2=\tilde\Pi$, $\tilde\Pi^{*}\neq\tilde\Pi$ and $\langle\tilde\Pi,e_0-\tilde\Pi\rangle_*=-\tfrac32$, so its Peirce summands are complementary left modules but not an orthogonal decomposition. No nonzero idempotent lies in $\mathbb{M}_-$.

Physical Readings

  • The state space is doubled, and the complement is the second copy. The interpretation is that the algebra offers two identical state spaces rather than one, so any operator that acts on the right can mix them, and the framework's two-component spinor structure is this doubling. Boundary: a counting statement about the algebra; it adds no second particle by itself.
  • The mass couples the state space to its complement. The interpretation is that the chirality-off-diagonal mass is a right multiplication, and a right multiplication is exactly what moves a state from $S$ to $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$, so the mass's other component is the complement. Boundary: the article gives the algebraic mechanism of the coupling and no value or spectrum of the mass; the number belongs to the dynamical articles.
  • A bra is not an antiparticle. The interpretation is that the dual $S^{*}$ (the right ideal, from the adjoint) and the complement $\bar S$ (the second left ideal, from the conjugation) are different subspaces, so reversing the arrows and conjugating the coefficients are different operations. Boundary: an algebraic separation of two operations, offered as the framework's reading of the dual/conjugate distinction.
  • The second vacuum is the complement. The interpretation is that the two one-mode ideals are the two choices of vacuum idempotent, so the complement is what the other vacuum generates, and no algebraic fact prefers one vacuum. Boundary: the module is unique; only the vacuum is a choice.
  • The doubling is not a chirality in the module sense. The interpretation is that the complement is isomorphic to $S$, so at the module level the doubling is a copy and not a chirality; where the corpus calls the two ideals the two chiralities (spectral triple, chirality-off-diagonal mass) it is using a second, different convention. Boundary: a convention warning, not a new result.
  • The sector label lives on the projector, not on the ladder. The interpretation is that the two ideals are distinguished by their idempotents, $\tilde\Pi_+$ and $\tilde\Pi_-$, which are orthogonal idempotents of $\mathbb{M}_+$, while the nilpotents that shift the level carry no material or informational label. The doubling is therefore an informational doubling — two projectors of one sector — and not a material–informational pair. Boundary: it accounts for where the sector split is visible on the ideal and assigns no new quantum number.

Summary

The complementary space of the spinor space $S=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+$ is the second minimal left ideal

$$ \mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_-,\hat\Upsilon_1\},\qquad \tilde\Pi_-=e_0-\tilde\Pi_+, $$

the second column of the matrix model. Two independent descriptions of it coincide: it is the module complement $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$ of the Peirce decomposition, and it is the Hilbert complement $S^{\perp}$ for the form $\langle u,v\rangle_*=\mathrm{Sc}(uv^{*})$, because the four matrix units are mutually orthogonal of norm $\tfrac12$. So

$$ \mathbb{B}=S\oplus\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-=S\perp\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_- $$

is a Peirce decomposition and an orthogonal decomposition of a Hilbert space at the same time.

The complement carries the same two structures as $S$: a left module of the general plain algebra with $L_{\tilde\Pi_+}=\mathrm{diag}(0,1)$, $L_{\tilde\Pi_-}=\mathrm{diag}(1,0)$ and the two nilpotents off-diagonal, and a Hilbert space with Gram matrix $G'=\tfrac12 I_2$ and norm $\tfrac12(|\phi_1|^2+|\phi_2|^2)$. It is isomorphic to $S$ as a module and metrically identical as a Hilbert space, but it is a different subspace.

It is reached by the coefficient conjugation $\bar{\cdot}$ — $\bar S=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$ — and not by the Hermitian conjugation ${}^{*}$, which gives the dual $S^{*}=\tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}$, the first row. The complement and the dual are different subspaces, and the complement is not a second representation: it carries the same defining representation of the double cover.

Physically the complement is the second copy of the state space, $\mathbb{B}=S\perp S$, which an operator acting on the right can mix with the first. Read as occupation it is the ideal of the level $|1\rangle=\tilde\Pi_-$, the other one-mode vacuum; read as conjugation it is the conjugate state space, distinct from the dual; and it is where the chirality-off-diagonal mass, itself a right multiplication, takes a state. Different corpus conventions name the doubling a chirality, a particle–hole pair, or the conjugate state space; the algebra is the same subspace. Neither ideal is contained in a sector: each meets $\mathbb{M}_+$ and $\mathbb{M}_-$ in one real dimension, all of it carried by the idempotent direction, so the ideal split and the sector split are independent. The independence is a prohibition and not a coincidence — the imaginary scalar swaps the two sectors, so no complex subspace of the algebra lies in a sector and the complex two-dimensional ideal can never split as the two sectors, while the Peirce complement $\tilde\Pi_-$ stays in $\mathbb{M}_+$ and $\mathbb{M}_-$ holds no nonzero idempotent.
