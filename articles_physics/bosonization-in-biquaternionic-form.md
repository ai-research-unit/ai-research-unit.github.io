# __Bosonization in Biquaternionic Form__

## Introduction

Bosonization is the statement that a theory of fermions in two spacetime dimensions is the same theory as a theory of bosons. The equivalence is exact and non-perturbative: the massless Dirac field is equivalent to a free scalar field, the massive Thirring model is equivalent to the sine-Gordon model, and the correspondence extends to the current algebra, the stress tensor, and the solitons of the bosonic side, which are the fermions of the fermionic side. The construction is standard (Coleman 1975; Mandelstam 1975; and, in the non-abelian case, Witten 1984), and its two-dimensionality is not an accident: it is tied to the current algebra. A massless two-component spinor carries two chiral currents, and their algebra closes on the derivative of a single real scalar; the central charges match, $c_{\text{Dirac}}=2\times\tfrac12=1=c_{\text{boson}}$. The equivalence is not a naive component count — a real scalar has one real component — but an identity of the operator algebras built on the two sides.

This article asks what the biquaternion framework contributes to that statement. The findings are the following.

1. **Bosonization is a correspondence between the framework's module and its centre.** This is the genuinely algebraic observation of the article. The fermionic side of the duality is carried by the spinor module $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi(\vec u)\cong\mathbb{C}^2$: the Dirac field is module-valued, its bilinears are built from the module's inner product, and its current algebra is a module-valued current algebra. The bosonic side is carried by a **central** scalar: the boson field is an element of the centre $\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{B}}$ (or of the real scalars), its exponentials are central, and its stress tensor is a central scalar. Bosonization is thus, in the framework's terms, an isomorphism between the operator algebra of the module and the operator algebra generated by the exponential of a central field, and the equivalence's "one complex fermion equals one real boson" is a statement about the dimension of the module's complex structure against the central field's single real component. Stating the correspondence this way makes the framework's contribution precise and modest: it names which two objects are being identified.

2. **The framework's linear, chirality-off-diagonal mass is what bosonization requires.** The series convention for the Dirac mass is linear and chirality-off-diagonal, $\tilde\nabla\tilde\Psi_R=m\tilde\Psi_L$, $\tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde\Psi_L=m\tilde\Psi_R$, and it is the **off-diagonal pairing** of the two chiral halves: it carries one half into the other and back, and a central scalar cannot do that, because it multiplies both halves by the same number. In the two-sided form of the same equation the mass sits on the right, $m(-i\overline{\tilde\Psi}\vec e_z)$, which is the chirality cell $-iL_{e_t}R_{\vec e_z}$ applied to the conjugate field. In two dimensions this is the standard Dirac mass, whose bosonized image is the cosine of the sine-Gordon model. The framework's mass term therefore maps to the bosonic interaction without a change of form, and the phase of the mass term — the framework's central phase $\exp(i\theta)$ multiplying the mass — maps to a **shift of the boson field**, which is the standard statement that the fermion mass's phase is the bosonic field's vacuum angle. The equivalence is thus exact at the level of the mass term, and the framework's mass convention is compatible with it rather than competing with it.

3. **The anomaly is the reason the correspondence is not trivial.** A naive operator correspondence between a fermion bilinear and a boson derivative fails by a c-number; the bosonization dictionary is exact only with that c-number included, and the c-number is the two-dimensional anomaly of *The Trace Anomaly in Biquaternionic Form*. The framework's contribution here is again a counting one: the central charges of the two sides must match, and the matching fixes the normalisation of the boson. The framework supplies the module's dimension on the fermionic side and the trace on the bosonic side.

4. **The two-dimensional arena is a restriction, and the framework must be reduced to it.** The algebra $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_\mathbb{R}\mathbb{H}$ is the even Clifford algebra $\mathbb{B}\cong\mathrm{Cl}^+_{1,3}$ of four-dimensional Minkowski spacetime; two-dimensional bosonization uses the two-dimensional Clifford algebra and its chirality operator, and the reduction from four to two dimensions is a restriction of the framework, not a property of it. The reduction is performed explicitly below, and it is the reason this article is a statement about a *reduced* framework. The lesson is the general one: bosonization is a feature of two dimensions, and the biquaternion approach carries it because it carries the spinor module, not because the algebra is four-dimensional.

5. **The non-abelian and the Wess–Zumino term are inherited.** Non-abelian bosonization identifies fermions in a representation of a group with a sigma model carrying a Wess–Zumino term, and the level of the term is fixed by the fermion's representation; the framework's non-commutative gauge-field material is the appropriate place for the group theory, and this article cites the result rather than deriving it.

The article proceeds as follows. The next section reduces the framework to the two-dimensional arena and fixes the two-dimensional Clifford data. A section states the abelian bosonization dictionary in the framework's notation. A section identifies the module–centre correspondence and states the counting it implies. A section shows how the framework's mass term and its central phase bosonize, and a section treats the anomaly and the central-charge matching. A section develops the vertex operators and the current algebra. A section states the non-abelian result and its status. A section separates what is established from what is interpretation, and the article closes with the open questions.

## The Two-Dimensional Arena

Bosonization lives in two dimensions, and the framework must be reduced before it can be stated.

**The reduction.** The framework's algebra is the even subalgebra of the four-dimensional Clifford algebra; in two dimensions the analogous object is the even subalgebra of $\mathrm{Cl}_{1,1}$. Write $L_{a}R_{b}\psi=a\psi b$ for the two-sided cell. The two-dimensional even Clifford algebra is $\mathrm{span}\{L_{e_t}R_{e_t},L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z}\}\cong\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}$, generated by the chirality cell, and it is two-real-dimensional; the reduction of the framework's "even" structure is therefore a real pair, and the spinor module's complex structure is carried by the full $\mathrm{Cl}_{1,1}(\mathbb{C})$, not by the even part. The algebra itself does not shrink: $\mathrm{Cl}_{1,1}(\mathbb{C})\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})\cong\mathbb{B}$, so the two-dimensional complexified Clifford algebra is the same algebra as the framework's, and the reduction changes the frame and the chirality rather than the algebra. The framework's four-dimensional structure is retained as the two-dimensional Dirac spinor's two complex components, and the spinor module remains two-complex-dimensional, which is what gives the counting below.

**The two-dimensional Clifford data.** With $g=\mathrm{diag}(+1,-1)$, the two cells $iR_{\vec e_x}$ and $iL_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_y}$ carry the two-dimensional Clifford relations,
$$
\{iL_{e_\mu}R_{\vec e_{\nu(\mu)}},iL_{e_\rho}R_{\vec e_{\nu(\rho)}}\}=2g_{\mu\rho}L_{e_t}R_{e_t},
\qquad \mu,\rho\in\{t,x\},
$$
and the four-cell product is in the section *The Gamma Formulation*.

The chirality cell is
$$
L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z}
= iR_{\vec e_x}\,iL_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_y}
= -R_{\vec e_x}L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_y},
\qquad
(L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z})^2 = +L_{e_t}R_{e_t},
$$
where the second equality uses $i^2=-1$, and the identification with $L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z}$ uses $R_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_y}=R_{\vec e_y\vec e_x}=-R_{\vec e_z}$. The chiral projectors
$$
P_L=\tfrac12\bigl(L_{e_t}R_{e_t}-L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z}\bigr),
\qquad
P_R=\tfrac12\bigl(L_{e_t}R_{e_t}+L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z}\bigr)
$$
project onto the two components; on the two-dimensional module each has trace $1$, while on the four-dimensional carrier the same cells have trace $2$, the carrier carrying each half twice.

**The square of the chirality.** Both chirality cells are involutions: the two-dimensional $L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z}$ squares to $+L_{e_t}R_{e_t}$, and so does the four-dimensional $-iL_{e_t}R_{\vec e_z}$ of the section *The Gamma Formulation*. The two dimensions differ in the bare product of the frame's generators, not in the square. In two dimensions the product of the two generators is the chirality cell itself, $iR_{\vec e_x}\,iL_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_y}=i^2R_{\vec e_x}L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_y}=L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z}$ with $i^2=-1$; in four dimensions the product of the four is
$$
iR_{\vec e_x}\,iL_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_y}\,iL_{\vec e_y}R_{\vec e_y}\,iL_{\vec e_z}R_{\vec e_y}
= i^4\, R_{\vec e_x}L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_y}L_{\vec e_y}R_{\vec e_y}L_{\vec e_z}R_{\vec e_y}
= -L_{e_t}R_{\vec e_z},
$$
the volume cell, and it squares to $-L_{e_t}R_{e_t}$ because $i^4=+1$. The $i$ that the literature attaches to the four-dimensional chirality, $-iL_{e_t}R_{\vec e_z}$, is what turns that product into an involution.

**The real structure.** The real structure of the reduction is the coefficient conjugation,
$$
\mathcal C(\tilde\Psi)=\overline{\tilde\Psi},
$$
which conjugates the complex coefficients and fixes the four units $e_t,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z$. It is an antilinear involution, $\mathcal C^2=1$, and it reverses exactly those cells that carry the central $i$: it fixes the real chirality cell $L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z}$ and reverses the two generators $iR_{\vec e_x}$ and $iL_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_y}$, which each carry one factor of $i$. Hence it commutes with the two-dimensional chirality and preserves each chiral half. The two-dimensional chirality cell is a **real** cell, $(L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z})^2=+L_{e_t}R_{e_t}$ with no factor of $i$; the four-dimensional one is $-iL_{e_t}R_{\vec e_z}$, a real cell times the central $i$. The same conjugation therefore reverses $\Gamma$ in four dimensions and exchanges the halves, so a real spinor there is the sum of a half and its conjugate, a **Majorana** spinor and not a **Majorana–Weyl** one, the exclusion *The Neutrino and Majorana Fermions in Biquaternionic Form* states. The whole two-dimensional/four-dimensional difference is that single factor of $i$. The facts were verified on the cells and are quoted in the established list below. This is the fermionic side's true content, and the bosonic side must match it.

Here $iL_{e_\mu}R_{\vec e_{\nu(\mu)}}$ is the generator cell of the coordinate $x_\mu$, with $\nu(t)=x$ and, in four dimensions, $\nu(k)=y$. The real structure $\mathcal C$ is not the Dirac adjoint $\bar\psi$ used in the dictionary below: $\mathcal C$ is the antilinear coefficient conjugation on the module, while $\bar\psi=\tilde\Psi^{*}iR_{\vec e_x}$ is the Dirac adjoint, built with the Hermitian conjugation $\tilde\Psi^{*}=\overline{\tilde\Psi^{\natural}}$ and carrying the Clifford-odd cell $iR_{\vec e_x}$.

**The boson.** The boson is a single real scalar field $\phi(x)$, whose exponentiated field $\exp(2i\sqrt{\pi}\phi)$ is, in the framework's terms, a **central** object: the exponent carries the central $i$, and a central scalar commutes with every element of $\mathbb{B}$. The boson's stress tensor is a central scalar, and its free action
$$
S_{\mathrm{b}}=\int\mathcal L_{\mathrm{b}}\,d^2x ,
\qquad
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{b}}
=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu\in\{t,x\}}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial Q_\mu}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial Q_\mu}
$$
is the two-dimensional restriction of the corpus's real-scalar Lagrangian, and the massless field equation it gives is $\Box\phi=0$ with the series d'Alembertian $\Box=\tilde\nabla\tilde\nabla^\natural=\partial^2/\partial Q_t^2+\Delta_Q$. The bosonic side is therefore the central sector's dynamics, and this is the identification the next sections use.

## The Abelian Bosonization Dictionary

The dictionary is stated first in its standard form, then in the framework's notation.

**The standard dictionary.** For a massless Dirac field $\psi$ and a real scalar $\phi$, with a normalisation constant $\alpha$ setting the short-distance cutoff and with the convention $\epsilon_{01}=+1$,
$$
\boxed{\;
\bar\psi iL_{e_\mu}R_{\vec e_{\nu(\mu)}}\psi \;\longleftrightarrow\;
-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_\nu\epsilon_{\mu\nu}\varepsilon_\nu\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial Q_\nu} ,
\qquad
\bar\psi\psi \;\longleftrightarrow\;
-\frac{1}{\pi\alpha}\cos\big(2\sqrt{\pi}\,\phi\big)\;},
$$
and the fermion number current's divergence vanishes while the axial current's does not:
$$
\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu\frac{\partial j_\mu}{\partial Q_\mu} = 0 ,
\qquad
\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu\frac{\partial j_{5\mu}}{\partial Q_\mu}
= -\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial Q_\mu^2} ,
$$
the metric signs sitting inside the divergence, $\nabla_\mu j^\mu=\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu\partial j_\mu/\partial Q_\mu$. The operator on the right is $\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu\partial^2/\partial Q_\mu^2=\partial^2/\partial Q_t^2-\Delta_Q$, the quaternionic norm of the gradient, and not the series d'Alembertian $\Box=\partial^2/\partial Q_t^2+\Delta_Q$; the two differ in the sign of the spatial part, and the anomaly carries the first. The boxed pair is the dictionary itself: the first entry is the current correspondence and the second is the mass correspondence, the sine-Gordon interaction. The second display states the two divergences: the vector current is conserved and the axial current is not, the second relation being the anomaly.

**The sign of the anomaly.** With $g=\mathrm{diag}(+1,-1)$ the signature signs are $\varepsilon_0=+1$ and $\varepsilon_1=-1$. The Hodge dual of the dictionary's current
$$
V_\mu=-\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\sum_\nu\epsilon_{\mu\nu}\varepsilon_\nu\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial Q_\nu}
$$
is
$$
A_\mu=\sum_\nu\epsilon_{\mu\nu}\varepsilon_\nu V_\nu
=-\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial Q_\mu},
$$
a gradient, and its divergence is
$$
\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu\frac{\partial A_\mu}{\partial Q_\mu}
=-\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial Q_\mu^2}.
$$
The signature sign matters: read as the plain contraction $B_\mu=\sum_\nu\epsilon_{\mu\nu}V_\nu$, the dual is $\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}(\partial_t\phi,-\partial_x\phi)$, whose two components have opposite relative signs and so do not form a gradient; that reading is not the axial current. The invariant contraction
$$
\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu A_\mu A_\mu=-\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu V_\mu V_\mu
$$
that *The Nonlinear Dirac Equation and the Thirring Model in Biquaternionic Form* verifies holds for $B$ as well, and therefore does not by itself select the Hodge dual; the gradient property, and with it the anomaly, does. These are standard (Coleman 1975; Mandelstam 1975; von Delft and Schoeller 1998), and the framework does not alter them.

**The dictionary in the framework's notation.** The Dirac adjoint is the element twisted by the Clifford-odd cell,
$$
\bar\psi=\tilde\Psi^{*}iR_{\vec e_x},
$$
and the two bilinears are module objects traced into the centre:
$$
\bar\psi iL_{e_\mu}R_{\vec e_{\nu(\mu)}}\psi
=\mathrm{Sc}\big(\tilde\Psi^{*}iR_{\vec e_x}iL_{e_\mu}R_{\vec e_{\nu(\mu)}}\tilde\Psi\big),
\qquad
\bar\psi\psi
=\mathrm{Sc}\big(\tilde\Psi^{*}iR_{\vec e_x}\tilde\Psi\big),
$$
where the Hermitian form, read through the trace, extracts the central scalar. In two dimensions the double $i$ simplifies by $i^2=-1$:
$$
iR_{\vec e_x}\,iR_{\vec e_x}=i^2R_{\vec e_x}^2=L_{e_t}R_{e_t},
\qquad
iR_{\vec e_x}\,iL_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_y}
=i^2R_{\vec e_x}L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_y}=L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z}
\quad(\text{with }R_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_y}=-R_{\vec e_z}).
$$
The two current components therefore carry the identity cell and the chirality cell, and the mass bilinear carries the timelike generator. The correspondence is a map
$$
\text{module bilinear}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{central field} ,
$$
and the bosonization dictionary is the explicit realisation of that map. Read in the framework's terms, the map's domain is the pairing of the spinor module with its dual (which decomposes into the current and the mass bilinear) and its target is the centre's derivative and exponential. The framework's contribution to the dictionary is thus not a new identity but the identification of the two sides' value spaces; the identities themselves are the standard ones and are cited.

**The cutoff and the normal ordering.** The constant $\alpha$ is a short-distance cutoff, and the correspondence holds after normal ordering of both sides. This is worth recording because it is the first appearance of the regularisation that the anomaly section will need: the dictionary is exact only for normal-ordered operators, and the cutoff $\alpha$ is where the short-distance subtraction enters, which is the same subtraction that produces the anomaly. In the framework the normal ordering is not algebraic — it is a subtraction of the short-distance behaviour of a two-point function — and it is transcribed as such.

## The Module–Centre Correspondence

This section states the article's genuinely algebraic observation and its counting consequence.

**The two objects.** On the fermionic side stands the spinor module,
$$
\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi(\vec u) \cong \mathbb{C}^2 ,
$$
two complex dimensions, carrying the Dirac field, its Clifford action, and the current algebra built from its bilinears. On the bosonic side stands the centre,
$$
\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{B}} = \mathrm{span}_\mathbb{R}\{e_t,ie_t\} \cong \mathbb{C} ,
$$
one complex dimension, and the real boson is its real part $\mathbb{R}e_t$ — the fixed space of coefficient conjugation — a single real dimension carrying the exponentiated field. Bosonization identifies an operator algebra built on the first with an operator algebra built on the second.

**The counting.** The equivalence's counting is the standard match between a complex fermion and a real boson. A complex (Dirac) fermion in two dimensions has four real components, which assemble into two Majorana fields of central charge $c=\tfrac12$ each; a real boson has $c=1$. The two sides therefore agree, and the agreement is the arithmetic
$$
2\times\tfrac12 = 1,
$$
the factor $2$ being the number of Majorana fields in the Dirac field. In the framework's terms the fermionic side is the spinor module $\mathbb{C}^2$, four real components, and the bosonic side is the centre's single real scalar; the module's two complex dimensions carry the two chiral eigenspaces of the chirality cell $L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z}$, each of one complex dimension, and the boson carries the one real dimension of the central field. The counting is a component count of the kind the partition-function and harmonic-oscillator articles perform, and it is the statement that the two sides' operator algebras agree, not that the two spaces are isomorphic.

**What the correspondence is not.** It is not an identity of the algebras, and the reason is structural: $\mathbb{B}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$ is simple, so every module over it is a sum of copies of $\mathbb{C}^2$ and has even complex dimension, which leaves $\mathbb{C}$ with no $\mathbb{B}$-module structure at all. The identification is between operator algebras built on the two spaces, not between the spaces. It is not a statement that the biquaternion algebra "contains" a boson; the boson is the centre's field and the fermion is the module's field, and both are placed in the algebra rather than derived from it. And it is not a four-dimensional statement: the equivalence fails in four dimensions because the counting fails there, and the framework's four-dimensional algebra is where the counting fails.

**Why the module and the centre.** The reason the correspondence takes this form is the one stated in the arena section: the four-dimensional framework's spinor module and its centre are the two objects whose two-dimensional reductions have the same size in real components, and bosonization is the statement that the two reduced theories are the same. The framework's contribution is to make the two objects named and their dimensions explicit; it does not derive the equivalence.

## The Mass Term and the Central Phase

The framework's mass convention is used here rather than merely cited, because it is where the framework's structure meets the dictionary.

**The series mass term.** As *The Dirac Equation in Biquaternionic Form* fixes, the Dirac mass is linear and chirality-off-diagonal. The **chiral pair** is
$$
\boxed{\;
\begin{aligned}
\tilde\nabla\tilde\Psi_R &= m\tilde\Psi_L,\\[4pt]
\tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde\Psi_L &= m\tilde\Psi_R.
\end{aligned}
\;}
$$
The gradient left-multiplies, and the mass is the **off-diagonal pairing** of the two halves: the map that carries one half into the other and back, scaled by $m$. The scaling cannot supply the pairing — a central scalar multiplies the two halves by the same number and stays diagonal — so the off-diagonality is a separate structure, and the two-sided form exhibits it on the other side of the field. The **two-sided form** of the same equation, Kravchenko's dictionary, writes it on a single element with the derivative from the left and the mass from the right,
$$
\tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde\Psi=m\,\bigl(-i\,\overline{\tilde\Psi}\,\vec e_z\bigr)=m\,\Gamma\,\mathcal C\tilde\Psi ,
$$
with $\Gamma=-iL_{e_t}R_{\vec e_z}$ the chirality cell and $\mathcal C$ the coefficient conjugation. The second reading is the informative one: the mass is the chirality cell applied to the conjugate field, and it is off-diagonal because $\mathcal C$ reverses $\Gamma$ — $\Gamma$ carries the central $i$, and the conjugation conjugates it. In the two-dimensional reduction the reversal has a different carrier: $\mathcal C$ commutes with the real chirality cell $L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z}$, so only a right multiplication can flip it, and the right factor of the second generator does: $R_{\vec e_y}$ anticommutes with $L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z}$ while $R_{\vec e_z}$ commutes with it. The two-dimensional mass direction is therefore $\vec e_y$. The mass's phase is central, $\exp(i\theta)e_t$, and multiplies $m$ in either form. In two dimensions, with the chiral projectors $P_{L,R}$, the mass term is
$$
\bar\psi\psi = \bar\psi_R\psi_L+\bar\psi_L\psi_R ,
$$
which is the bilinear the dictionary sends to the cosine.

**The bosonization of the mass.** Under the dictionary, for real $m$,
$$
\boxed{\;m\,\bar\psi\psi \;\longleftrightarrow\; -\frac{m}{\pi\alpha}\cos\big(2\sqrt{\pi}\,\phi\big) = -\frac{2\mu}{\beta^2}\cos(\beta\phi) ,
\qquad
\mu = \frac{2m}{\alpha}\;},
$$
the second form exhibiting the sine-Gordon parameters $\beta=2\sqrt\pi$ and $\mu\propto m/\alpha$, and the fermion's mass $m$ becoming the *coupling* of the bosonic cosine. The equivalence is therefore a statement about the mass: the fermion's mass is the boson's interaction strength, and the massless fermion is the free boson. This is the standard content of the Coleman–Mandelstam equivalence and is cited.

**The phase of the mass.** A mass term with phase $\theta$ is equivalent, by a chiral rotation of the fermion, to a real mass $|m|$ together with a shift of the boson:
$$
|m|\,\bar\psi\psi \;\longleftrightarrow\; -\frac{|m|}{\pi\alpha}\cos\big(2\sqrt{\pi}\,\phi+\theta\big).
$$
The phase is therefore a **shift of the boson field**. In the framework the mass's phase is a central phase $\exp(i\theta)e_t$, the same object as the $\theta$ vacuum's weighting of *The Theta Vacuum in Biquaternionic Form*, and the shift it induces is the two-dimensional instance of the statement that a $\theta$ angle is a bosonic vacuum angle. The framework's statement is therefore exact and structural: the central phase of the mass is the central phase of the vacuum, and bosonization realises it as a field shift.

**Why a cosine and not a phase.** The mass term pairs the two chiralities. The two real bilinears are the scalar $\bar\psi\psi$ and the pseudoscalar $i\,\bar\psi\,L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z}\psi$, with $L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z}$ the two-dimensional chirality; the $i$ is needed because the timelike insertion $iR_{\vec e_x}$ anticommutes with the chirality cell, $\{iR_{\vec e_x},L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z}\}=0$, so $\bar\psi\,L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z}\psi$ is anti-Hermitian and $i$ times it is real. A chiral rotation by $\theta$ rotates the two densities into one another, and a mass $m=|m|\exp(i\theta)$ selects the direction $\theta$ in that plane. In two dimensions the real structure $\mathcal C$ preserves each chiral half, so the two densities are real with respect to $\mathcal C$ and the rotation is a real rotation; its bosonized image is the real, doubly-periodic shifted cosine of the previous paragraph, not a phase. In four dimensions the charge conjugation exchanges the halves, so a real spinor pairs a half with its conjugate and the two chiralities are not two independent real planes; the two-dimensional cosine image has no four-dimensional analogue. A chiral rotation still exists in four dimensions, but it is anomalous and becomes a $\theta$ term rather than a shift of a boson. This is the algebraic face of the counting failure recorded below, and it was verified on the cells.

## The Anomaly and the Central-Charge Matching

The equivalence is exact only with the anomaly included, and the framework's counting enters through the central charges.

**The anomaly's role.** The dictionary's current relation and mass relation cannot both hold with the free-field stress tensor: the fermionic stress tensor and the bosonic one differ by a c-number, and the difference is the anomaly. Equivalently, the two-point function of the fermion current has a contact term that the boson's two-derivative two-point function reproduces only with the correct normalisation, and that normalisation is the central charge. In two dimensions the anomalous trace is
$$
\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu T_{\mu\mu} = \frac{c}{24\pi}R
$$
from *The Trace Anomaly in Biquaternionic Form*, and the matching of $c$ on the two sides is the consistency condition the dictionary must satisfy.

**The matching.** The matching is the arithmetic of the previous section:
$$
\boxed{\;c_{\text{Dirac}} = 2\,c_{\text{Majorana}} = 2\times\tfrac12 = 1 = c_{\text{boson}}\;}.
$$
The framework supplies the factor $2$ as the two Majorana fields into which the module's four real components assemble, and the boson's $1$ as the real part of the centre. The matching is therefore a component count, and it is the same kind of count that appears in the partition-function and harmonic-oscillator articles; once again, it is a count and not a claim of a multiplicity of independent fields.

**What the anomaly forbids.** The anomaly forbids a bosonization that preserves both the vector and the axial current, and it forbids a strictly local and free dictionary for a massive fermion. The bosonized mass is a cosine rather than a free term for this reason, and the sine-Gordon model's solitons — the fermions — are its consequence. The framework adds nothing to these statements; it supplies the value spaces and the counting, as before.

**The Schwinger model as the standard illustration.** A massless fermion coupled to a two-dimensional abelian gauge field has a massive bosonic spectrum, and the anomaly is what supplies the mass; the framework's interpretation is that the gauge field's longitudinal mode is eaten by the would-be Goldstone mode of the boson, with the anomaly providing the mass. The statement is standard (Schwinger 1962; Coleman, Jackiw, and Susskind 1975) and is recorded only as the illustration of the anomaly's role.

## Vertex Operators and the Current Algebra

The fermionic side of the dictionary is built from vertex operators, and their dimensions are the sharpest arithmetic of the equivalence.

**The current algebra.** In the bosonized language the current is $j_\mu=-\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\sum_\nu\epsilon_{\mu\nu}\varepsilon_\nu\partial_\nu\phi$ (the dictionary above), whose components are $j_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\partial_1\phi$ and $j_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\partial_0\phi$, and the equal-time commutator of two lower-index components in $1+1$ dimensions is
$$
\big[j_0(x),j_1(y)\big] = \frac{i}{\pi}\,\frac{d\delta}{dx}(x-y),
$$
the **Schwinger term**. The sign and the coefficient are the canonical commutator's: $[\phi(x),\partial_0\phi(y)]=i\delta(x-y)$ with $\hbar=1$, the corpus's convention, gives $\frac{1}{\pi}[\partial_1\phi(x),\partial_0\phi(y)]=\frac{i}{\pi}\delta'(x-y)$. Both indices are low, so no metric sign enters this commutator; the signature signs are already carried by the dictionary's $\epsilon_{\mu\nu}\varepsilon_\nu$. The coefficient is $1/\pi$, the square of the anomaly's $1/\sqrt\pi$ because it is bilinear in the current, the anomaly being
$$
\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu\frac{\partial j_{5\mu}}{\partial Q_\mu}
=-\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial Q_\mu^2},
\qquad
j_{5\mu}=\bar\psi iL_{e_\mu}R_{\vec e_{\nu(\mu)}} L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z}\psi .
$$
In the framework the normalisation of the current algebra is a statement about the $\mathrm{U}(1)$ current built from the module's bilinear; the Schwinger term lives in the coefficient and not in the algebra, exactly as before.

**Vertex operators.** The bosonized fermion is an exponential of the boson field, the **vertex operator**
$$
\psi_{L,R}(x)\;\sim\;:\!\exp\bigl(i\beta\phi_{L,R}(x)\bigr)\!:,
\qquad
\Delta_{\mathrm{ch}}(\beta)=\frac{\beta^{2}}{8\pi},
\qquad
\Delta(\beta)=\frac{\beta^{2}}{4\pi},
$$
where $\phi_{L,R}$ are the chiral halves of $\phi=\phi_L+\phi_R$, $\Delta_{\mathrm{ch}}$ is the dimension of a vertex built on one chiral half and $\Delta$ the dimension of a vertex built on the full field. The two differ by the factor two of the half-propagator: $\langle\phi_L\phi_L\rangle$ carries half of $\langle\phi\phi\rangle$, so a chiral vertex of coupling $\beta$ has half the dimension of a full one. The arithmetic was verified, and one coupling supplies both values: $\beta=2\sqrt\pi$ gives $\Delta_{\mathrm{ch}}(2\sqrt\pi)=\tfrac12$, the dimension of a free fermion field, and $\Delta(2\sqrt\pi)=1$, the dimension of the mass operator $\cos(2\sqrt\pi\phi)$, whose exponential halves are the products of the two chiral vertices, one from each half, $e^{i2\sqrt\pi\phi_L}e^{i2\sqrt\pi\phi_R}=e^{i2\sqrt\pi\phi}$. The fermion and the perturbation that gives it a mass are therefore the same vertex at the same coupling, read on one chiral half or on the pair; no second coupling is needed. This is the clearest form of the equivalence: the fermion is the vertex with the fermion's dimension, and the mass is the vertex with the mass's dimension.

**The framework's interpretation of a vertex.** The operator $:\!\exp(i\beta\phi)\!:$ is built from the **exponential of a central field**: $\phi$ is central, so the vertex operator is the exponential of a central element, and it is the only kind of local operator the bosonic side has. The fermion on the other side is a **module-valued, anticommuting** object. The equivalence therefore identifies a central exponential with an anticommuting, module-valued field, and the identification is not an isomorphism of the algebras as graded objects: the anticommutation is the spin–statistics statement, carried on the bosonic side by the cocycle of the vertex algebra, and the cocycle's coefficient is the same anomaly as before. Read this way, the bosonization dictionary is a map between algebras with **different gradings**, made an isomorphism of operator algebras by the vertex algebra's central extension. This is the algebraic content of the equivalence and it is worth stating plainly, because it shows the dictionary's non-triviality: an associative central exponential and an anticommuting module field are not the same object, and only the extension makes them equivalent.

**Sugawara.** The bosonic stress tensor is the Sugawara form of the current of the previous subsection. Written with the dictionary's $j_\mu$ and $g=\mathrm{diag}(+1,-1)$, the traceless symmetric combination is
$$
T_{\mu\nu}=\frac{\pi}{2}\bigl(j_\mu j_\nu+j_\nu j_\mu-g_{\mu\nu}j_\rho j^\rho\bigr)
=\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial_\nu\phi-\tfrac12\,g_{\mu\nu}\sum_\rho\varepsilon_\rho\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial Q_\rho}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial Q_\rho},
$$
the coefficient $\pi/2$ being the inverse of the dictionary's $1/\pi$, and the right-hand side is the canonical stress tensor of the free boson, so the central charge is $c=1$, matching the fermionic $c=1$ of the central-charge section. The framework adds nothing beyond naming the current's $\mathrm{U}(1)$ as the abelian part of the module's symmetry; the non-abelian Sugawara construction belongs to the group theory of the next section.

## The Non-Abelian Case and the Wess–Zumino Term

The non-abelian extension is stated and its status fixed.

**The result.** A theory of fermions transforming in a representation of a group is equivalent to a sigma model on the group manifold with a Wess–Zumino term whose coefficient — the level $k$ — is fixed by the representation, $k=1$ for the fundamental representation of $SU(N)$ at the appropriate normalisation. The correspondence maps the fermion's symmetry currents to the sigma model's currents and the fermion's stress tensor to the Sugawara form of the current algebra. This is the standard non-abelian bosonization (Witten 1984), and it is cited.

**The framework's status regarding it.** The group theory of the non-abelian case belongs to the framework's gauge-field material, not to this subcategory, and the level's quantisation belongs to the topology that the $\theta$-vacuum article discusses. What the framework contributes here is the same as in the abelian case: the fermionic side is module-valued and the bosonic side's group-valued field is exponentiated from elements whose logarithms lie in the appropriate sector. The framework does not derive the Wess–Zumino term; it places it.

**The level and the anomaly.** The level's integrality and the anomaly's coefficient are the same kind of statement — a quantised coefficient read from a topological density — and the $\theta$-vacuum and instanton articles supply the topological objects. This enters the article only as a pointer, because the non-abelian bosonization is a gauge-field statement and is out of this subcategory's scope.

## What Is Established and What Is Interpretation

**Established (framework and algebra).**

- Bosonization is a correspondence between the operator algebra of the spinor module $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi(\vec u)\cong\mathbb{C}^2$ and the operator algebra generated by the exponential of a central scalar field; the module's four real components assemble into two Majorana fields and the centre's real part supplies the single real boson, the two sides matching in the counting $2\times\tfrac12=1$.
- In the two-dimensional reduction with $g=\mathrm{diag}(+1,-1)$ the chirality cell is the involution $L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z}=iR_{\vec e_x}\,iL_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_y}=i^2R_{\vec e_x}L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_y}$ with $i^2=-1$ and $R_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_y}=-R_{\vec e_z}$, and $(L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z})^2=+L_{e_t}R_{e_t}$. The real structure is the coefficient conjugation $\mathcal C\tilde\Psi=\overline{\tilde\Psi}$, an involution, $\mathcal C^2=1$, that fixes the chirality cell because it is a real cell while reversing the two generators $iR_{\vec e_x}$ and $iL_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_y}$ because each carries one factor of $i$ (verified on the cells); each chiral half is therefore preserved by the real structure, and the mass term rotates the scalar and pseudoscalar densities in a real plane. This is the algebraic reason the bosonized mass is a cosine. In four dimensions the charge conjugation exchanges the halves, and the counting that makes bosonization possible fails.
- The chiral projectors $P_L=\tfrac12(L_{e_t}R_{e_t}-L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z})$ and $P_R=\tfrac12(L_{e_t}R_{e_t}+L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z})$ satisfy $P_{L,R}^2=P_{L,R}$, $P_RP_L=0$, $\mathrm{tr}\,P_{L,R}=1$ on the module, and $P_L+P_R=L_{e_t}R_{e_t}$; verified.
- The Dirac adjoint is $\bar\psi=\tilde\Psi^{*}iR_{\vec e_x}$; the current bilinear therefore carries the Clifford-odd cell $iR_{\vec e_x}$, and the two-dimensional products $iR_{\vec e_x}\,iR_{\vec e_x}=L_{e_t}R_{e_t}$ and $iR_{\vec e_x}\,iL_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_y}=L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z}$ reduce the double $i$ by $i^2=-1$.
- The framework's mass term is the off-diagonal pairing of the chiral halves; in the two-sided form it is the chirality cell applied to the coefficient conjugate, $m\Gamma\mathcal C\tilde\Psi$ with $\Gamma=-iL_{e_t}R_{\vec e_z}$, and in two dimensions the chirality flip is carried by the right multiplication $R_{\vec e_y}$. It bosonizes to the cosine, and its central phase $\exp(i\theta)e_t$ bosonizes to a shift of the boson field, $\cos(2\sqrt\pi\phi)\to\cos(2\sqrt\pi\phi+\theta)$.
- The vertex-operator arithmetic is exact: with $\Delta_{\mathrm{ch}}(\beta)=\beta^2/(8\pi)$ on one chiral half and $\Delta(\beta)=\beta^2/(4\pi)$ on the full field, the single coupling $\beta=2\sqrt\pi$ gives $\Delta_{\mathrm{ch}}=\tfrac12$, the free-fermion dimension, and $\Delta=1$, the mass dimension; the factor two between them is the half-propagator of the chiral field, not a second coupling. The bosonized fermion is therefore a central exponential while the fermion itself is module-valued and anticommuting, so the dictionary maps algebras with different gradings.
- The central charges match, $c_{\text{Dirac}}=2\times\tfrac12=1=c_{\text{boson}}$, as a component count.

**Standard, and transcribed.**

- The abelian dictionary: $\bar\psi iL_{e_\mu}R_{\vec e_{\nu(\mu)}}\psi\leftrightarrow-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\sum_\nu\epsilon_{\mu\nu}\varepsilon_\nu\partial\phi/\partial Q_\nu$, $\bar\psi\psi\leftrightarrow-\tfrac{1}{\pi\alpha}\cos(2\sqrt\pi\phi)$; the axial anomaly $\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu\,\partial j_{5\mu}/\partial Q_\mu=-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu\partial^2\phi/\partial Q_\mu^2$; the sine-Gordon parameters $\beta=2\sqrt\pi$.
- The Schwinger term $[j_0(x),j_1(y)]=\tfrac{i}{\pi}\frac{d\delta}{dx}(x-y)$, with both indices lowered; the vertex operators $\psi_{L,R}\sim\,:\!\exp(i\beta\phi_{L,R})\!:$ with $\Delta_{\mathrm{ch}}=\beta^2/8\pi$ at $\beta=2\sqrt\pi$; the Sugawara stress tensor and its $c=1$.
- The Coleman–Mandelstam equivalence of the massive Thirring model and sine-Gordon; the soliton–fermion correspondence.
- The central charge $c=1$ for a complex fermion and for a real boson, and $c=\tfrac12$ for a Majorana–Weyl fermion.
- The Schwinger model's bosonic spectrum; the non-abelian bosonization and the Wess–Zumino level.

**Interpretation.**

- Reading bosonization as the module–centre correspondence is the framework's presentation of the standard equivalence; the equivalence is not derived here, and the dictionary's identities are the standard ones.
- Reading the mass phase as a central phase of the same kind as the $\theta$ vacuum's weighting is the framework's identification of two appearances of the centre; the physical statement (a mass phase shifts the boson) is standard.

**Open.**

- Whether the framework has a natural bosonization in the presence of its full four-dimensional structure — for instance whether a two-dimensional reduction of a biquaternion gauge theory bosonizes with a framework-specific level — is not addressed.
- The precise operator-algebraic isomorphism between the module's current algebra and the centre's exponentiated algebra is stated but not proved; the article gives the correspondence and the counting, and cites the standard construction for the identity itself.
- Whether the coefficient conjugation $\mathcal C=\overline{\cdot}$ is forced by the reduction or is one of several equivalent real structures, and whether the framework's sector structure selects it, is not resolved here.

## The Gamma Formulation

The gamma symbols are used only in this section; every other section writes the same objects as cells. The gammas are a frame of $\mathrm{Cl}_{1,3}(\mathbb{C})=M_4(\mathbb{C})$, not a different algebra; in the two-dimensional reduction of this article the two generators are $\gamma_0=iL_{e_t}R_{\vec e_x}=iR_{\vec e_x}$ and $\gamma_1=iL_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_y}$ with $L_{a}R_{b}$, and the two-dimensional chirality is their product, the cell $L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z}$, not the four-dimensional $\gamma_5$.

Here $L_a$ and $R_b$ are the left and right multiplications, $L_a\psi=a\psi$ and $R_b\psi=\psi b$, and $c(M)$ is the coefficient matrix of $M$ in the basis of the sixteen products.

$$
\gamma_0=iL_{e_t}R_{\vec e_x},\quad \gamma_1=iL_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_y},\quad \gamma_2=iL_{\vec e_y}R_{\vec e_y},\quad \gamma_3=iL_{\vec e_z}R_{\vec e_y},\quad \gamma_5=-iL_{e_t}R_{\vec e_z} .
$$

In two dimensions only $\gamma_0$ and $\gamma_1$ are present, and their product is the chirality cell:
$$
\gamma_0\gamma_1=L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z},
\qquad
(\gamma_0\gamma_1)^2=I_4 .
$$
The four-dimensional $\gamma_5$ is not this product; it is the volume cell times $i$, and its square is also $I_4$ because $i^2=-1$ turns the volume cell's $-L_{e_t}R_{e_t}$ into $+L_{e_t}R_{e_t}$.

A single product is a pure tensor; the products form the seven-dimensional cone $\operatorname{rank}c(M)\le1$, and a general element is a sum of at most four, with $\ell(M)=\operatorname{rank}c(M)$.

$\gamma_5$ is the reference-frame cell of the chirality $\Gamma$; the central grading $\chi=i\tilde\Xi_{\mathrm{ad}}$ — the difference of the two central idempotents of the complexified algebra, so $\chi^2=e_t$ — is a different object, represented by $\gamma_5$ on the Dirac module.

The dictionary is complex-linear; over $\mathbb{R}$ the products span $\mathrm{Cl}_{1,3}(\mathbb{R})\cong M_2(\mathbb{H})$ and the cells span $\mathrm{Cl}_{3,1}(\mathbb{R})\cong M_4(\mathbb{R})$.

**The standard gamma identities.** In the $\gamma$ reading, with $\epsilon_{0123}=+1$,
$$
\{\gamma_\mu,\gamma_\nu\}=2g_{\mu\nu}I_4,\qquad
(\gamma_k)^2=-I_4,\qquad
\gamma_5^2=I_4,\qquad
\{\gamma_\mu,\gamma_5\}=0,
$$
$$
[\gamma_\mu,\gamma_\nu]=2\gamma_\mu\gamma_\nu\quad(\mu\ne\nu),\qquad
[\gamma_a,S_{cd}]=g_{ac}\gamma_d-g_{ad}\gamma_c,\qquad
S_{cd}=\tfrac14[\gamma_c,\gamma_d],
$$
$$
\mathrm{tr}(\gamma_\mu\gamma_\nu)=4g_{\mu\nu},\qquad
\mathrm{tr}(\gamma_5\gamma_\mu\gamma_\nu\gamma_\rho\gamma_\sigma)=-4i\,\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma},
$$
$$
\mathrm{tr}(\gamma_\mu\gamma_\nu\gamma_\rho\gamma_\sigma)
=4\bigl(g_{\mu\nu}g_{\rho\sigma}-g_{\mu\rho}g_{\nu\sigma}+g_{\mu\sigma}g_{\nu\rho}\bigr).
$$
The four contraction sums depend on the frame, because the individual squares do. In the Euclidean frame, where every generator squares to $+I_4$, they are
$$
\sum_\mu\gamma_\mu\gamma_\mu=4I_4,\qquad
\sum_\mu\gamma_\mu\gamma_\alpha\gamma_\mu=-2\gamma_\alpha,\qquad
\sum_\mu\gamma_\mu\gamma_\alpha\gamma_\beta\gamma_\mu=4\delta_{\alpha\beta}I_4,\qquad
\sum_\mu\gamma_\mu\gamma_\delta\gamma_\beta\gamma_\alpha\gamma_\mu=+2\gamma_\delta\gamma_\beta\gamma_\alpha ,
$$
the general rule being $\sum_\mu\gamma_\mu A\gamma_\mu=(-1)^n(4-2n)A$ for $A$ a product of $n$ distinct generators. In the $\mathbb{M}_-$ frame the three spacelike generators square to $-I_4$, the sums are no longer proportional to single generators, and only the first keeps its closed form, $\sum_\mu\gamma_\mu\gamma_\mu=-2I_4$.

## Summary

Bosonization in biquaternionic form is the standard two-dimensional equivalence, with the framework's naming of the two sides. In the two-dimensional reduction the Clifford data are
$$
\{iL_{e_\mu}R_{\vec e_{\nu(\mu)}},iL_{e_\rho}R_{\vec e_{\nu(\rho)}}\}=2g_{\mu\rho}L_{e_t}R_{e_t},
\qquad g=\mathrm{diag}(+1,-1),
$$
the chirality cell is
$$
L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z}
=iR_{\vec e_x}\,iL_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_y}
=-R_{\vec e_x}L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_y},
\qquad
(L_{\vec e_x}R_{\vec e_z})^2=+L_{e_t}R_{e_t},
$$
where $i^2=-1$, and the real structure $\mathcal C\tilde\Psi=\overline{\tilde\Psi}$ is an involution that commutes with it, because the chirality cell is a real cell, so the two chiralities are a real splitting. The dictionary is
$$
\bar\psi iL_{e_\mu}R_{\vec e_{\nu(\mu)}}\psi
\longleftrightarrow
-\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\sum_\nu\epsilon_{\mu\nu}\varepsilon_\nu\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial Q_\nu} ,
\qquad
\bar\psi\psi\longleftrightarrow-\frac{1}{\pi\alpha}\cos\big(2\sqrt\pi\,\phi\big),
\qquad
\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu\frac{\partial j_{5\mu}}{\partial Q_\mu}
=-\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial Q_\mu^2} ,
$$
and the framework's contribution is to name the two sides: the fermion is the spinor module $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi(\vec u)\cong\mathbb{C}^2$ and the boson is a central scalar, so that the equivalence is a correspondence between a module-valued operator algebra and the centre's exponentiated algebra. The counting $c_{\text{Dirac}}=2\times\tfrac12=1=c_{\text{boson}}$ matches the central charges, the framework's linear chirality-off-diagonal mass bosonizes to the cosine, and the mass's central phase $\exp(i\theta)e_t$ bosonizes to a shift of the boson field, the same central phase that weights the $\theta$ vacuum. The dictionary, the sine-Gordon equivalence, the Schwinger model, and the non-abelian case with its Wess–Zumino term are standard and transcribed.
