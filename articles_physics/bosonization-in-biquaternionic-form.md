# __Bosonization in Biquaternionic Form__

## Introduction

Bosonization is the statement that a theory of fermions in two spacetime dimensions is the same theory as a theory of bosons. The equivalence is exact and non-perturbative: the massless Dirac field is equivalent to a free scalar field, the massive Thirring model is equivalent to the sine-Gordon model, and the correspondence extends to the current algebra, the stress tensor, and the solitons of the bosonic side, which are the fermions of the fermionic side. The construction is standard (Coleman 1975; Mandelstam 1975; and, in the non-abelian case, Witten 1984), and its two-dimensionality is not an accident: it is tied to the current algebra. A massless two-component field carries two chiral currents, and their algebra closes on the derivative of a single real scalar; the central charges match, $c_{\text{Dirac}}=2\times\tfrac12=1=c_{\text{boson}}$. The equivalence is not a naive component count — a real scalar has one real component — but an identity of the operator algebras built on the two sides.

This article asks what the biquaternion framework contributes to that statement. The findings are the following.

1. **Bosonization is a correspondence between the algebra and its centre.** This is the genuinely algebraic observation of the article. The fermionic side of the duality is the field element $\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}\in\mathbb{B}$: one element of the algebra, split by its label into two parts of two complex coefficients each. The bosonic side is a **central** scalar: the boson is an element of the centre $\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{B}}$ (or of the real scalars), its exponentials are central, and its stress tensor is a central scalar. Bosonization is thus, in the framework's terms, a correspondence between the operator algebra built on the element and the operator algebra generated by the exponential of a central field, and the equivalence's "one complex fermion equals one real boson" is a statement about the two coefficients of one value of the field against the centre's single real coefficient. Stating the correspondence this way makes the framework's contribution precise and modest: it names which two objects are being identified.

2. **The framework's mass is what bosonization requires, and its carrier is a right factor.** The series convention for the mass does not multiply the field by a coefficient: it is written on the other side of the element, $m\Gamma\overline{\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}}$ (*The Dirac Equation in Biquaternionic Form*). It therefore **pairs the two values of the label**: a central scalar multiplies both values by the same number and stays diagonal, while the right factor of the mass is one of the units that exchange the values. In two dimensions that unit is $\vec e_y$, and the exchange is the whole of the mass's structure. In two dimensions this is the standard Dirac mass, whose bosonized image is the cosine of the sine-Gordon model. The framework's mass term therefore maps to the bosonic interaction without a change of form, and the phase of the mass term — the framework's central phase $\exp(i\theta)$ multiplying the mass — maps to a **shift of the boson field**, which is the standard statement that the fermion mass's phase is the bosonic field's vacuum angle. The equivalence is thus exact at the level of the mass term, and the framework's mass convention is compatible with it rather than competing with it.

3. **The anomaly is the reason the correspondence is not trivial.** A naive operator correspondence between a fermion bilinear and a boson derivative fails by a c-number; the bosonization dictionary is exact only with that c-number included, and the c-number is the two-dimensional anomaly of *The Trace Anomaly in Biquaternionic Form*. The framework's contribution here is again a counting one: the central charges of the two sides must match, and the matching fixes the normalisation of the boson. The framework supplies the field's coefficient count on the fermionic side and the trace on the bosonic side.

4. **The two-dimensional arena is a restriction, and the framework must be reduced to it.** The algebra is four-dimensional and the field carries four coefficients; bosonization is two-dimensional, so the arena is a restriction of the frame to two directions together with a reading of one value of the field, which has the two coefficients of the two-dimensional Dirac field. The reduction is a restriction of the framework, not a property of it, and it is performed explicitly below. The lesson is the general one: bosonization is a feature of two dimensions, and the biquaternion approach carries it because the field is an element of the algebra and the label splits it into two values, not because the algebra is four-dimensional.

5. **The non-abelian and the Wess–Zumino term are inherited.** Non-abelian bosonization identifies fermions in a representation of a group with a sigma model carrying a Wess–Zumino term, and the level of the term is fixed by the fermion's representation; the framework's non-commutative gauge-field material is the appropriate place for the group theory, and this article cites the result rather than deriving it.

The article proceeds as follows. The next section reduces the framework to the two-dimensional arena and fixes its two generator actions. A section states the abelian bosonization dictionary in the framework's notation. A section identifies the element–centre correspondence and states the counting it implies. A section shows how the framework's mass term and its central phase bosonize, and a section treats the anomaly and the central-charge matching. A section develops the vertex operators and the current algebra. A section states the non-abelian result and its status. A section separates what is established from what is interpretation, and the article closes with the open questions.

## The Two-Dimensional Arena

Bosonization lives in two dimensions, and the framework must be reduced before it can be stated.

**The frame.** The arena is the material frame of *Frame and Metric Conventions* read in two slots, $Q_t$ and $Q_x$, reached from the four-slot frame by dropping the two spatial directions $Q_y$ and $Q_z$. The two directions of the arena are $e_t$ and $\vec e_x$. Every display below contracts with the plain weight $\varepsilon=(1,-1)$ of *Frame and Metric Conventions*, §*The Rules*, read in the arena's two slots, and every coordinate derivative is written $\partial/\partial Q_\mu$ as *Operator and Calculus Conventions* fixes it. Nothing else of the frame is restated here.

**The two generator actions.** The two directions act on an element by multiplication on the two sides. The timelike direction is the action $A_t:Q\mapsto iQ\vec e_x$, and the spacelike direction is the action $A_x:Q\mapsto i\vec e_xQ\vec e_y$. Both are the frame's two cells read in the arena, and their products are derived here. Composing each with itself,
$$
A_tA_t:Q\mapsto -Q\vec e_x\vec e_x=Q,
\qquad
A_xA_x:Q\mapsto -(\vec e_x\vec e_x)\,Q\,(\vec e_y\vec e_y)=-Q ,
$$
and the two orders add to zero, $A_tA_x+A_xA_t=0$, because the left factors agree and the two right factors anticommute. The two actions therefore square to $e_t$ and to $-e_t$ and anticommute, which is the arena's reading of the frame's anticommutator in *Frame and Metric Conventions*, §*The $\mathbb{M}_-$ Frame*. The product in one order is one action,
$$
A_tA_x:Q\mapsto\vec e_xQ\vec e_z ,
$$
because $i^2=-1$ and $\vec e_y\vec e_x=-\vec e_z$, and it is an involution, since $\vec e_x\vec e_x=-e_t$ and $\vec e_z\vec e_z=-e_t$ give the element back after two applications.

**The chirality and the two values of the label.** The action $\Gamma:Q\mapsto\vec e_xQ\vec e_z$ is an involution, so the algebra splits into the elements of value $+1$ and the elements of value $-1$. The field is written $\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}_L$ when its value is the minus one and $\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}_R$ when its value is the plus one. That is the whole of the label: there are two values, and only the zero element carries both. The label belongs to the element and not to a state, so a general element, a product, a unit and a field all carry it. Two facts of its arithmetic are used below. A product takes the value of its right factor. For the single units, left multiplication by $e_t$ or $\vec e_x$ keeps the value and left multiplication by $\vec e_y$ or $\vec e_z$ exchanges the two; on the right, $\vec e_x$ and $\vec e_y$ exchange the values and $e_t$ and $\vec e_z$ keep them. The four entries were checked on the units.

**The field.** The fermionic field is an element $\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}$ of $\mathbb{B}$, and there is no other carrier. Its label splits it into two parts of two complex coefficients each. One part is the count of the two-dimensional Dirac field, and the two parts together give that count twice.

**The coefficient conjugation.** The coefficient conjugation $\overline{\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}}$ fixes the four units and conjugates the coefficients (*Algebra and Element Conventions*, §*The Conjugations*). It reverses exactly those actions that carry the central $i$: it fixes the chirality, which is a product of units, and reverses the two generator actions, each of which carries one factor of $i$. Written on the actions, the three statements are
$$
\overline{\Gamma\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}}=\Gamma\,\overline{\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}},
\qquad
\overline{A_t\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}}=-A_t\,\overline{\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}},
\qquad
\overline{A_x\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}}=-A_x\,\overline{\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}},
$$
and they were checked by recomputation on generic elements. The conjugation therefore preserves each value of the label, and the two values are a real splitting of the algebra.

**The four-dimensional comparison.** In the full four-slot frame the chirality is the action $Q\mapsto-iQ\vec e_z$, the volume cell with its phase (*Special Element Conventions*, §*The Chirality Convention*). It carries the central $i$, so the coefficient conjugation reverses it and therefore exchanges the two values: a real element there is one value together with the conjugate of the other, which is a Majorana field and not a Majorana–Weyl one (*The Neutrino and Majorana Fermions in Biquaternionic Form*). The whole difference between the two arenas is that single factor of $i$: in the arena the chirality is a product of units, and the conjugation fixes it. This is the fermionic side's true content, and the bosonic side must match it.

**The boson.** The boson is a single real scalar field $\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}$, and it is central: it multiplies every element by the same number, and its exponentials $\exp(2i\sqrt\pi\,\xi^{\mathrm{scl}})$ are central. Its free dynamics is the massless equation $\Box\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}=0$ of *Operator and Calculus Conventions*, §*The Gradient*, and its stress tensor is a central scalar. The bosonic side of the dictionary is therefore the centre's dynamics, and this is the identification the next sections use.

## The Abelian Bosonization Dictionary

The dictionary is stated first in its standard form, then in the framework's notation.

**The two components of the current.** The adjoint of the field is the field read through the Hermitian conjugation and followed by the arena's timelike cell (*Unit, Index and Notation Conventions*, §*The Superscripts*). In the arena the two components of the current reduce to two scalar parts,
$$
j_t=\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}*}\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}\bigr),
\qquad
j_x=\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}*}\,\vec e_x\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}\,\vec e_z\bigr).
$$
The reduction is a derived form. In the timelike component the cell $i\vec e_x$ meets itself, and $(i\vec e_x)^2=i^2\,\vec e_x\vec e_x=e_t$, so the identity is what remains. In the spacelike component the same cell follows the arena's second generator, and $(i\vec e_x\,Q\,\vec e_y)(i\vec e_x)=\vec e_xQ\vec e_z$, so the chirality is what remains. The two components therefore carry the identity and the chirality, and no third cell appears. Both scalar parts are real, and the check is a recomputation on generic elements.

**The standard dictionary.** For a massless field $\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}$ and a real scalar $\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}$, with a normalisation constant $\alpha$ setting the short-distance cutoff and with the convention $\epsilon_{01}=+1$,
$$
\boxed{\;
j_\mu \;\longleftrightarrow\;
-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_\nu\epsilon_{\mu\nu}\varepsilon_\nu\frac{\partial\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial Q_\nu} ,
\qquad
\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}*}\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}\,i\vec e_x\bigr) \;\longleftrightarrow\;
-\frac{1}{\pi\alpha}\cos\big(2\sqrt{\pi}\,\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}\big)\;},
$$
whose components are $j_t\leftrightarrow\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\partial\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}/\partial Q_x$ and $j_x\leftrightarrow\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\partial\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}/\partial Q_t$. The first entry is the current correspondence and the second is the mass correspondence, the sine-Gordon interaction.

**The two divergences.** The vector current is conserved and the axial current is not:
$$
\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu\frac{\partial j_\mu}{\partial Q_\mu} = 0 ,
\qquad
\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu\frac{\partial j_{5\mu}}{\partial Q_\mu}
= -\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu\frac{\partial^2\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial Q_\mu^2} ,
$$
the plain weight sitting inside the divergence. The operator on the right is the quaternionic norm of the gradient of *Operator and Calculus Conventions*, §*The Gradient*, and not the d'Alembertian of the same section: the two differ in the sign of the spatial part, and the anomaly carries the first. The second relation is the anomaly.

**The axial current.** Composing the chirality with the two generator actions gives $\Gamma A_t=-A_x$ and $\Gamma A_x=-A_t$, so inserting the chirality between the adjoint and the field exchanges the two components and reverses both signs:
$$
j_{5,t}=-j_x,\qquad j_{5,x}=-j_t .
$$
That is the dual of the current in the arena with $\epsilon_{01}=+1$, and with the dictionary it reads
$$
j_{5,t}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\frac{\partial\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial Q_t},
\qquad
j_{5,x}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\frac{\partial\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial Q_x} :
$$
the axial current is a gradient. The plain exchange $\sum_\nu\epsilon_{\mu\nu}j_\nu$, with no weight in the sum, is not a gradient: its two components have opposite relative signs, and the weighted dual is the one the anomaly uses. These are standard (Coleman 1975; Mandelstam 1975; von Delft and Schoeller 1998), and the framework does not alter them.

**What the dictionary identifies.** The correspondence is a map
$$
\text{two scalar parts of the element}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{central field} ,
$$
and the bosonization dictionary is the explicit realisation of that map. Its domain is the element's two scalar parts, which the previous paragraphs reduce to the two current components and to the mass bilinear, and its target is the centre's derivative and exponential. The framework's contribution to the dictionary is thus not a new identity but the identification of the two sides' value spaces; the identities themselves are the standard ones and are cited.

**The cutoff and the normal ordering.** The constant $\alpha$ is a short-distance cutoff, and the correspondence holds after normal ordering of both sides. This is worth recording because it is the first appearance of the regularisation that the anomaly section will need: the dictionary is exact only for normal-ordered operators, and the cutoff $\alpha$ is where the short-distance subtraction enters, which is the same subtraction that produces the anomaly. In the framework the normal ordering is not algebraic — it is a subtraction of the short-distance behaviour of a two-point function — and it is transcribed as such.

## The Element–Centre Correspondence

This section states the article's genuinely algebraic observation and its counting consequence.

**The two objects.** On the fermionic side stands the field: one element $\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}$ of the algebra, with its two values and its four coefficients, and its bilinears are the scalar parts computed above. On the bosonic side stands the centre $\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{B}}$ (*Algebra and Element Conventions*, §*The Centre and the Vector Subspace*), one complex dimension; the real boson is its real part, the fixed space of the coefficient conjugation, a single real dimension carrying the exponentiated field. Bosonization identifies an operator algebra built on the first with an operator algebra built on the second.

**The counting.** The equivalence's counting is the standard match between a complex fermion and a real boson. A complex (Dirac) fermion in two dimensions has four real components, which assemble into two Majorana fields of central charge $c=\tfrac12$ each; a real boson has $c=1$. The two sides therefore agree, and the agreement is the arithmetic
$$
2\times\tfrac12 = 1,
$$
the factor $2$ being the number of Majorana fields in the Dirac field. In the framework's terms one value of the label has two complex coefficients, four real numbers, and that is the count of the two-dimensional Dirac field; the algebra carries that count once for each value of the label, and the bosonization count uses one of them. The boson carries the one real dimension of the centre's real part. The counting is a component count of the kind the partition-function and harmonic-oscillator articles perform, and it is the statement that the two sides' operator algebras agree, not that the two spaces are isomorphic.

**What the correspondence is not.** It is not an identity of the algebras, and the reason is structural: a central element multiplies every element by the same number and commutes with everything, while a general element does not, and the element's two values and four coefficients have no counterpart in the centre. The identification is between operator algebras built on the two spaces, not between the spaces. It is not a statement that the biquaternion algebra "contains" a boson either; the boson is the centre's field and the fermion is the algebra's element, and both are placed in the algebra rather than derived from it. And it is not a four-dimensional statement: the equivalence fails in four dimensions because the counting fails there, and the count is the one just made.

**The strongest objection.** The obvious objection is that the two sides have different kinds of element — one is a field with four coefficients, the other a number with one — so the "identification" of their operator algebras reduces to a coincidence of counts. The objection is right about the counts and wrong about the conclusion. The correspondence is not an identification of the two spaces; it is a map between their operator algebras, and the map's non-trivial part — the anomaly, the normal ordering, the cocycle — is exactly what a count cannot supply. A count that no map realises would be an accident; here the map is the standard dictionary, written out in the previous section, and the counts are its consistency condition rather than its content.

**Why the element and the centre.** The reason the correspondence takes this form is the one stated in the arena section: the field's one value and the centre's real part are the two objects whose two-dimensional readings have the same size in real components, and bosonization is the statement that the two reduced theories are the same. The framework's contribution is to make the two objects named and their counts explicit; it does not derive the equivalence.

## The Mass Term and the Central Phase

The framework's mass convention is used here rather than merely cited, because it is where the framework's structure meets the dictionary.

**The series mass term.** As *The Dirac Equation in Biquaternionic Form* fixes, the Dirac mass is linear and sits on the other side of the element,
$$
\tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}=m\,\Gamma\,\overline{\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}} ,
$$
with $\Gamma$ the chirality and $\overline{\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}}$ the coefficient conjugate. The gradient left-multiplies, and the mass is the **exchange of the two values**: the map that carries one value into the other, scaled by $m$. The scaling cannot supply the exchange — a central scalar multiplies the two values by the same number and stays diagonal — so the exchange is a separate structure, and the display shows it on the other side of the field.

**The exchange in the arena.** In the arena the chirality is a product of units, and the coefficient conjugation fixes it. The exchange must therefore be carried by a right multiplication. Of the units left after the chirality's own $\vec e_x$ and $\vec e_z$, one exchanges the two values and the other keeps them: $\vec e_y$ exchanges and $\vec e_z$ keeps. The exchanging one is the right factor of the arena's second generator, which is why the arena's mass direction is $\vec e_y$. The arena's mass equation is therefore
$$
\tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}=m\,\overline{\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}}\,\vec e_y ,
$$
and the label balances. The left side keeps the value of $\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}$; the right side conjugates, which keeps it, and then multiplies on the right by $\vec e_y$, which exchanges it. The equation pairs the two values, and no coefficient could do the same.

**The mass bilinear.** The mass bilinear of the dictionary is
$$
\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}*}\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}\,i\vec e_x\bigr),
$$
the adjoint's bilinear with the timelike cell left in place. It is real, and it too ends in a right factor that exchanges the two values — $\vec e_x$ — so it multiplies one value by the other. Under the dictionary it is the cosine.

**The bosonization of the mass.** Under the dictionary, for real $m$,
$$
\boxed{\;m\,\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}*}\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}\,i\vec e_x\bigr) \;\longleftrightarrow\; -\frac{m}{\pi\alpha}\cos\big(2\sqrt{\pi}\,\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}\big) = -\frac{2\mu}{\beta^2}\cos(\beta\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}) ,
\qquad
\mu = \frac{2m}{\alpha}\;},
$$
the second form exhibiting the sine-Gordon parameters $\beta=2\sqrt\pi$ and $\mu\propto m/\alpha$, and the fermion's mass $m$ becoming the *coupling* of the bosonic cosine. The equivalence is therefore a statement about the mass: the fermion's mass is the boson's interaction strength, and the massless fermion is the free boson. This is the standard content of the Coleman–Mandelstam equivalence and is cited.

**The phase of the mass.** A mass term with phase $\theta$ is equivalent, by a chiral rotation of the fermion, to a real mass $|m|$ together with a shift of the boson:
$$
|m|\,\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}*}\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}\,i\vec e_x\bigr) \;\longleftrightarrow\; -\frac{|m|}{\pi\alpha}\cos\big(2\sqrt{\pi}\,\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}+\theta\big).
$$
The phase is therefore a **shift of the boson field**. In the framework the mass's phase is a central phase $\exp(i\theta)$, the same object as the $\theta^{\mathrm{vac}}$ vacuum's weighting of *The Theta Vacuum in Biquaternionic Form*, and the shift it induces is the two-dimensional instance of the statement that a $\theta$ angle is a bosonic vacuum angle. The framework's statement is therefore exact and structural: the central phase of the mass is the central phase of the vacuum, and bosonization realises it as a field shift.

**Why a cosine and not a phase.** The mass term pairs the two values of the label. The two real bilinears are the scalar $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}*}\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}\,i\vec e_x)$ and the pseudoscalar
$$
i\,\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}*}\,\vec e_x\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}\,\vec e_z\,i\vec e_x\bigr),
$$
with the chirality inserted beside the timelike cell. The two insertions act on opposite values — the chirality keeps the value and the timelike cell exchanges it — and the scalar part of the resulting element is imaginary, so $i$ times it is real; the check is a recomputation on generic elements. A chiral rotation by $\theta$ rotates the two densities into one another, and a mass $m=|m|\exp(i\theta)$ selects the direction $\theta$ in that plane. In the arena the coefficient conjugation preserves each value, so the two densities are real with respect to it and the rotation is a real rotation; its bosonized image is the real, doubly-periodic shifted cosine of the previous paragraph, not a phase. In four dimensions the coefficient conjugation reverses the chirality and therefore exchanges the values, so a real element pairs a value with the conjugate of the other and the two values are not two independent real planes; the arena's cosine image has no four-dimensional analogue. A chiral rotation still exists in four dimensions, but it is anomalous and becomes a $\theta^{\mathrm{vac}}$ term rather than a shift of a boson. This is the algebraic face of the counting failure recorded below, and it was verified on the actions.

## The Anomaly and the Central-Charge Matching

The equivalence is exact only with the anomaly included, and the framework's counting enters through the central charges.

**The anomaly's role.** The dictionary's current relation and mass relation cannot both hold with the free-field stress tensor: the fermionic stress tensor and the bosonic one differ by a c-number, and the difference is the anomaly. Equivalently, the two-point function of the fermion current has a contact term that the boson's two-derivative two-point function reproduces only with the correct normalisation, and that normalisation is the central charge. In two dimensions the anomalous trace is
$$
\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu T_{\mu\mu} = \frac{c}{24\pi}R
$$
from *The Trace Anomaly in Biquaternionic Form*, and the matching of $c$ on the two sides is the consistency condition the dictionary must satisfy.

**The matching.** The matching is the arithmetic of the previous section:
$$
\boxed{\;c_{\text{Dirac}} = 2\,c_{\text{Majorana}} = 2\times\tfrac12 = 1 = c_{\text{boson}}\;}.
$$
The framework supplies the factor $2$ as the two Majorana fields into which one value's four real components assemble, and the boson's $1$ as the real part of the centre. The matching is therefore a component count, and it is the same kind of count that appears in the partition-function and harmonic-oscillator articles; once again, it is a count of components and not a claim that one side contains the other's fields.

**What the anomaly forbids.** The anomaly forbids a bosonization that preserves both the vector and the axial current, and it forbids a strictly local and free dictionary for a massive fermion. The bosonized mass is a cosine rather than a free term for this reason, and the sine-Gordon model's solitons — the fermions — are its consequence. The framework adds nothing to these statements; it supplies the value spaces and the counting, as before.

**The Schwinger model as the standard illustration.** A massless fermion coupled to a two-dimensional abelian gauge field has a massive bosonic spectrum, and the anomaly is what supplies the mass; the framework's interpretation is that the gauge field's longitudinal mode is eaten by the would-be Goldstone mode of the boson, with the anomaly providing the mass. The statement is standard (Schwinger 1962; Coleman, Jackiw, and Susskind 1975) and is recorded only as the illustration of the anomaly's role.

## Vertex Operators and the Current Algebra

The fermionic side of the dictionary is built from vertex operators, and their dimensions are the sharpest arithmetic of the equivalence.

**The current algebra.** In the bosonized language the current is $j_\mu=-\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\sum_\nu\epsilon_{\mu\nu}\varepsilon_\nu\partial\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}/\partial Q_\nu$ (the dictionary above), whose components are $j_t=\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\partial\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}/\partial Q_x$ and $j_x=\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\partial\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}/\partial Q_t$, and the equal-time commutator of the two lower-index components in the arena is
$$
\bigl[j_t(Q_x),j_x(Q_y)\bigr] = \frac{i}{\pi}\,\frac{d\delta}{dQ_x}(Q_x-Q_y),
$$
the **Schwinger term**. The sign and the coefficient are the canonical commutator's: the commutator $[\xi^{\mathrm{scl}},\partial\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}/\partial Q_t]=i\delta$ with $\hbar=1$, the corpus's convention, gives $\frac{1}{\pi}[\partial\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}/\partial Q_x,\partial\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}/\partial Q_t]$, which is $\frac{i}{\pi}$ times the derivative of the delta written in the display above. Both indices are low, so no metric sign enters this commutator; the signature signs are already carried by the dictionary's $\epsilon_{\mu\nu}\varepsilon_\nu$. The coefficient is $1/\pi$, the square of the anomaly's $1/\sqrt\pi$ because it is bilinear in the current, the anomaly being the second relation of the dictionary section.

**Vertex operators.** The bosonized fermion is an exponential of the boson field, the **vertex operator**
$$
:\!\exp\bigl(i\beta\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}_{L,R}\bigr)\!:,
\qquad
\Delta_{\mathrm{ch}}(\beta)=\frac{\beta^{2}}{8\pi},
\qquad
\Delta(\beta)=\frac{\beta^{2}}{4\pi},
$$
where $\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}_L$ and $\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}_R$ are the two halves of $\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}=\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}_L+\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}_R$, $\Delta_{\mathrm{ch}}$ is the dimension of a vertex built on one half and $\Delta$ the dimension of a vertex built on the full field. The two differ by the factor two of the half-propagator: $\langle\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}_L\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}_L\rangle$ carries half of $\langle\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}\rangle$, so a half-vertex of coupling $\beta$ has half the dimension of a full one. The arithmetic was verified, and one coupling supplies both values: $\beta=2\sqrt\pi$ gives $\Delta_{\mathrm{ch}}(2\sqrt\pi)=\tfrac12$, the dimension of a free fermion field, and $\Delta(2\sqrt\pi)=1$, the dimension of the mass operator $\cos(2\sqrt\pi\,\xi^{\mathrm{scl}})$, whose exponential halves are the products of the two half-vertices, one from each half, $e^{i2\sqrt\pi\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}_L}e^{i2\sqrt\pi\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}_R}=e^{i2\sqrt\pi\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}}$. The fermion and the perturbation that gives it a mass are therefore the same vertex at the same coupling, read on one half or on the pair; no second coupling is needed. This is the clearest form of the equivalence: the fermion is the vertex with the fermion's dimension, and the mass is the vertex with the mass's dimension.

**The framework's interpretation of a vertex.** The operator $:\!\exp(i\beta\xi^{\mathrm{scl}})\!:$ is built from the **exponential of a central field**: $\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}$ is central, so the vertex operator is the exponential of a central element, and it is the only kind of local operator the bosonic side has. The fermion on the other side is an **anticommuting element** of the algebra. The equivalence therefore identifies a central exponential with an anticommuting element, and the identification is not an isomorphism of the two algebras as they stand: the boson's field commutes with itself and the fermion's anticommutes, and the anticommutation is the statistics statement, carried on the bosonic side by the cocycle of the vertex algebra, whose coefficient is the same anomaly as before. Read this way, the bosonization dictionary is a map between **a commuting object and an anticommuting one**, made an isomorphism of operator algebras by the vertex algebra's central extension. This is the algebraic content of the equivalence and it is worth stating plainly, because it shows the dictionary's non-triviality: an associative central exponential and an anticommuting element are not the same object, and only the extension makes them equivalent.

**Sugawara.** The bosonic stress tensor is the Sugawara form of the current of the previous subsection. Written with the dictionary's $j_t$ and $j_x$, the traceless symmetric combination is
$$
T_{tt}=T_{xx}=\frac{\pi}{2}\bigl(j_t^2+j_x^2\bigr),
\qquad
T_{tx}=T_{xt}=\pi\,j_tj_x ,
$$
the coefficient $\pi/2$ being the inverse of the dictionary's $1/\pi$, and it is the canonical stress tensor of the free boson,
$$
T_{tt}=T_{xx}=\frac12\left[\Bigl(\frac{\partial\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial Q_t}\Bigr)^2+\Bigl(\frac{\partial\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial Q_x}\Bigr)^2\right],
\qquad
T_{tx}=T_{xt}=\frac{\partial\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial Q_t}\frac{\partial\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial Q_x} ,
$$
so the central charge is $c=1$, matching the fermionic $c=1$ of the central-charge section. The framework adds nothing beyond naming the current's $\mathrm{U}(1)$ as the abelian part of the algebra's symmetry; the non-abelian Sugawara construction belongs to the group theory of the next section.

## The Non-Abelian Case and the Wess–Zumino Term

The non-abelian extension is stated and its status fixed.

**The result.** A theory of fermions transforming in a representation of a group is equivalent to a sigma model on the group manifold with a Wess–Zumino term whose coefficient — the level $k$ — is fixed by the representation, $k=1$ for the fundamental representation of $SU(N)$ at the appropriate normalisation. The correspondence maps the fermion's symmetry currents to the sigma model's currents and the fermion's stress tensor to the Sugawara form of the current algebra. This is the standard non-abelian bosonization (Witten 1984), and it is cited.

**The framework's status regarding it.** The group theory of the non-abelian case belongs to the framework's gauge-field material, not to this subcategory, and the level's quantisation belongs to the topology that the $\theta^{\mathrm{vac}}$-vacuum article discusses. What the framework contributes here is the same as in the abelian case: the fermionic side is an element of the algebra with its two values, and the bosonic side's group-valued field is exponentiated from central elements. The framework does not derive the Wess–Zumino term; it places it.

**The level and the anomaly.** The level's integrality and the anomaly's coefficient are the same kind of statement — a quantised coefficient read from a topological density — and the $\theta^{\mathrm{vac}}$-vacuum and instanton articles supply the topological objects. This enters the article only as a pointer, because the non-abelian bosonization is a gauge-field statement and is out of this subcategory's scope.

## What Is Established and What Is Interpretation

**Established (framework and algebra).**

- Bosonization is a correspondence between the operator algebra of the field element $\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}$ and the operator algebra generated by the exponential of a central scalar field; one value of the label has two complex coefficients, which assemble into two Majorana fields, and the centre's real part supplies the single real boson, the two sides matching in the counting $2\times\tfrac12=1$.
- In the arena the two generator actions are $A_t:Q\mapsto iQ\vec e_x$ and $A_x:Q\mapsto i\vec e_xQ\vec e_y$; they square to $e_t$ and to $-e_t$ and anticommute, and their product in one order is the chirality $\Gamma:Q\mapsto\vec e_xQ\vec e_z$, an involution with $\Gamma A_t=-A_x$ and $\Gamma A_x=-A_t$. Verified on generic elements.
- The label takes two values: $\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}_L$ is the minus one and $\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}_R$ the plus one, and only the zero element is both. A product takes the value of its right factor; for the single units, left multiplication by $e_t$ or $\vec e_x$ keeps the value and by $\vec e_y$ or $\vec e_z$ exchanges it, and right multiplication by $\vec e_x$ or $\vec e_y$ exchanges it.
- The real structure is the coefficient conjugation $\overline{\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}}$, an involution that fixes the chirality, which is a product of units, and reverses the two generator actions, each of which carries one factor of $i$; each value of the label is therefore preserved, and the mass term rotates the scalar and pseudoscalar densities in a real plane. This is the algebraic reason the bosonized mass is a cosine. In four dimensions the chirality carries the central $i$, the conjugation reverses it and exchanges the values, and the counting that makes bosonization possible fails there.
- The current bilinears are the two scalar parts $j_t=\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}*}\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}})$ and $j_x=\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}*}\vec e_x\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}\vec e_z)$; they carry the identity and the chirality, both are real, and the axial components are $j_{5,t}=-j_x$ and $j_{5,x}=-j_t$. Verified.
- The mass term is the exchange of the two values; in the arena it is $m\overline{\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}}\vec e_y$, the exchange being carried by the right factor $\vec e_y$ because the conjugation fixes the real chirality. It bosonizes to the cosine, and its central phase $\exp(i\theta)$ bosonizes to a shift of the boson field, $\cos(2\sqrt\pi\xi^{\mathrm{scl}})\to\cos(2\sqrt\pi\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}+\theta)$.
- The vertex-operator arithmetic is exact: with $\Delta_{\mathrm{ch}}(\beta)=\beta^2/(8\pi)$ on one half and $\Delta(\beta)=\beta^2/(4\pi)$ on the full field, the single coupling $\beta=2\sqrt\pi$ gives $\Delta_{\mathrm{ch}}=\tfrac12$, the free-fermion dimension, and $\Delta=1$, the mass dimension; the factor two between them is the half-propagator of the half-field, not a second coupling. The bosonized fermion is therefore a central exponential while the fermion itself is an anticommuting element, so the dictionary maps a commuting object to an anticommuting one, and only the central extension makes the two operator algebras isomorphic.
- The central charges match, $c_{\text{Dirac}}=2\times\tfrac12=1=c_{\text{boson}}$, as a component count.

**Standard, and transcribed.**

- The abelian dictionary: $j_\mu\leftrightarrow-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\sum_\nu\epsilon_{\mu\nu}\varepsilon_\nu\partial\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}/\partial Q_\nu$, $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}*}\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}} i\vec e_x)\leftrightarrow-\tfrac{1}{\pi\alpha}\cos(2\sqrt\pi\xi^{\mathrm{scl}})$; the axial anomaly $\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu\partial j_{5\mu}/\partial Q_\mu=-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu\partial^2\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}/\partial Q_\mu^2$; the sine-Gordon parameters $\beta=2\sqrt\pi$.
- The Schwinger term $[j_t,j_x]=\tfrac{i}{\pi}\delta'$, $\delta'$ being the derivative of the delta, with both indices low; the vertex operators with $\Delta_{\mathrm{ch}}=\beta^2/8\pi$ at $\beta=2\sqrt\pi$; the Sugawara stress tensor and its $c=1$.
- The Coleman–Mandelstam equivalence of the massive Thirring model and sine-Gordon; the soliton–fermion correspondence.
- The central charge $c=1$ for a complex fermion and for a real boson, and $c=\tfrac12$ for a Majorana field.
- The Schwinger model's bosonic spectrum; the non-abelian bosonization and the Wess–Zumino level.

**Interpretation.**

- Reading bosonization as the element–centre correspondence is the framework's presentation of the standard equivalence; the equivalence is not derived here, and the dictionary's identities are the standard ones.
- Reading the mass phase as a central phase of the same kind as the $\theta^{\mathrm{vac}}$ vacuum's weighting is the framework's identification of two appearances of the centre; the physical statement (a mass phase shifts the boson) is standard.

**Open.**

- Whether the framework has a natural bosonization in the presence of its full four-dimensional structure — for instance whether a two-dimensional reduction of a biquaternion gauge theory bosonizes with a framework-specific level — is not addressed.
- The precise operator-algebraic isomorphism between the current's algebra and the centre's exponentiated algebra is stated but not proved; the article gives the correspondence and the counting, and cites the standard construction for the identity itself.
- Whether the coefficient conjugation is forced by the reduction or is one of several equivalent real structures, and whether the framework's sector structure selects it, is not resolved here.

## The Legacy Pauli Gamma Formulation

The Pauli and gamma formulation is legacy: the reasons are explained in the article *Why AXF Matrices instead of Pauli and Gamma Matrices*. The generators carry no frame label: $\gamma_\mu$ is one set of matrices, $\gamma_0^2=I_4$ and $\gamma_k^2=-I_4$. The frame belongs to the coordinate, not to the array.

The gamma symbols are used only in this section; every other section writes the same objects as cells. The gammas are a frame of $\mathrm{Cl}_{1,3}(\mathbb{C})=M_4(\mathbb{C})$, not a different algebra; in the two-dimensional reduction of this article the two generators are $\gamma_0=iL_{e_t}^{\mathrm{pau}}R_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{pau}}=iR_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{pau}}$ and $\gamma_1=iL_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{pau}}R_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{pau}}$ with $L_{a}R_{b}$, and the two-dimensional chirality is their product, the cell $L_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{pau}}R_{\vec e_z}^{\mathrm{pau}}$, not the four-dimensional $\gamma_5$.

Here $L_a$ and $R_b$ are the left and right multiplications, $L_a\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}=a\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}$ and $R_b\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}=\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}} b$, and $c(M)$ is the coefficient matrix of $M$ in the basis of the sixteen products.

$$
\gamma_0=iL_{e_t}^{\mathrm{pau}}R_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{pau}},\quad \gamma_1=iL_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{pau}}R_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{pau}},\quad \gamma_2=iL_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{pau}}R_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{pau}},\quad \gamma_3=iL_{\vec e_z}^{\mathrm{pau}}R_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{pau}},\quad \gamma_5=-iL_{e_t}^{\mathrm{pau}}R_{\vec e_z}^{\mathrm{pau}} .
$$

The same in the axis-free display:

$$
\gamma_0=iL_{e_t}^{\mathrm{axf}}R_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{axf}},\quad \gamma_1=iL_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{axf}}R_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{axf}},\quad \gamma_2=iL_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{axf}}R_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{axf}},\quad \gamma_3=iL_{\vec e_z}^{\mathrm{axf}}R_{\vec e_y}^{\mathrm{axf}},\quad \gamma_5=-iL_{e_t}^{\mathrm{axf}}R_{\vec e_z}^{\mathrm{axf}} .
$$

In two dimensions only $\gamma_0$ and $\gamma_1$ are present, and their product is the chirality cell:
$$
\gamma_0\gamma_1=L_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{pau}}R_{\vec e_z}^{\mathrm{pau}},
\qquad
(\gamma_0\gamma_1)^2=I_4 .
$$

In the axis-free display:

$$
\gamma_0\gamma_1=L_{\vec e_x}^{\mathrm{axf}}R_{\vec e_z}^{\mathrm{axf}},
\qquad
(\gamma_0\gamma_1)^2=I_4 .
$$

The four-dimensional $\gamma_5$ is not this product; it is the volume cell times $i$, and its square is also $I_4$ because $i^2=-1$ turns the volume cell's $-L_{e_t}^{\mathrm{pau}}R_{e_t}^{\mathrm{pau}}$ into $+L_{e_t}^{\mathrm{pau}}R_{e_t}^{\mathrm{pau}}$.

A single product is a pure tensor; the products form the seven-dimensional cone $\operatorname{rank}c(M)\le1$, and a general element is a sum of at most four, with $\ell(M)=\operatorname{rank}c(M)$.

$\gamma_5$ is the reference-frame cell of the chirality $\Gamma$; the central grading $\chi=i\tilde{Ζ}_{\mathrm{ad}}$ — the difference of the two central idempotents of the complexified algebra, so $\chi^2=e_t$ — is a different object, represented by $\gamma_5$ on the Dirac module.

The dictionary is complex-linear; over $\mathbb{R}$ the products span $\mathrm{Cl}_{1,3}(\mathbb{R})\cong M_2(\mathbb{H})$ and the cells span $\mathrm{Cl}_{3,1}(\mathbb{R})\cong M_4(\mathbb{R})$.

**The standard gamma identities.** In the $\gamma$ reading, with $\epsilon_{0123}=+1$ and $\varepsilon=(1,-1,-1,-1)$,
$$
\{\gamma_\mu,\gamma_\nu\}=2\varepsilon_\mu\delta_{\mu\nu}I_4,\qquad
(\gamma_k)^2=-I_4,\qquad
\gamma_5^2=I_4,\qquad
\{\gamma_\mu,\gamma_5\}=0,
$$
$$
[\gamma_\mu,\gamma_\nu]=2\gamma_\mu\gamma_\nu\quad(\mu\ne\nu),\qquad
[\gamma_a,S_{cd}]=\varepsilon_a\delta_{ac}\gamma_d-\varepsilon_a\delta_{ad}\gamma_c,\qquad
S_{cd}=\tfrac14[\gamma_c,\gamma_d],
$$
$$
\mathrm{tr}(\gamma_\mu\gamma_\nu)=4\varepsilon_\mu\delta_{\mu\nu},\qquad
\mathrm{tr}(\gamma_5\gamma_\mu\gamma_\nu\gamma_\rho\gamma_\sigma)=-4i\,\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma},
$$
$$
\mathrm{tr}(\gamma_\mu\gamma_\nu\gamma_\rho\gamma_\sigma)
=4\bigl(\varepsilon_\mu\delta_{\mu\nu}\varepsilon_\rho\delta_{\rho\sigma}-\varepsilon_\mu\delta_{\mu\rho}\varepsilon_\nu\delta_{\nu\sigma}+\varepsilon_\mu\delta_{\mu\sigma}\varepsilon_\nu\delta_{\nu\rho}\bigr).
$$
The four contraction sums, with the index $\mu$ summed over the frame and the summed index raised by $\gamma^\mu=-\varepsilon_\mu\gamma_\mu$, are
$$
\sum_\mu\gamma^\mu\gamma_\mu=-4I_4,\qquad
\sum_\mu\gamma^\mu\gamma_\alpha\gamma_\mu=2\gamma_\alpha,\qquad
\sum_\mu\gamma^\mu\gamma_\alpha\gamma_\beta\gamma_\mu=-4\varepsilon_\alpha\delta_{\alpha\beta}I_4,\qquad
\sum_\mu\gamma^\mu A\gamma_\mu=(-1)^{n+1}(4-2n)A ,
$$
the general rule being $\sum_\mu\gamma^\mu A\gamma_\mu=(-1)^{n+1}(4-2n)A$ for $A$ a product of $n$ distinct generators. The metric sits in the raised index and nowhere else: with every index low the sums read $\sum_\mu\gamma_\mu\gamma_\mu=-2I_4$, $\sum_\mu\gamma_\mu\gamma_0\gamma_\mu=+4\gamma_0$, and the spacelike sums vanish, $\sum_\mu\gamma_\mu\gamma_k\gamma_\mu=0$.

**The display.** Every array printed in this section is given twice: in the Peirce display first, labelled $\mathrm{pau}$, whose arrays are the ones the literature prints, and in the axis-free display after it, labelled $\mathrm{axf}$, which is the display of the body and of the corpus. The two are carried into one another by $L_{e_\mu}^{\mathrm{axf}}=C_{\Pi}L_{e_\mu}^{\mathrm{pau}}C_{\Pi}^{-1}$, and a conjugation by $C_{\Pi}$ changes no product, no anticommutator, no trace and no determinant, so every identity of the section holds in both. An array is a statement about one display; a cell named in the running text keeps the Peirce label, and its axis-free array is the conjugate. The label names the array and not the operator.

## Summary

Bosonization in biquaternionic form is the standard two-dimensional equivalence, with the framework's naming of the two sides. The arena's two generator actions are $A_t:Q\mapsto iQ\vec e_x$ and $A_x:Q\mapsto i\vec e_xQ\vec e_y$; they square to $e_t$ and to $-e_t$ and anticommute, and their product in one order is the chirality $\Gamma:Q\mapsto\vec e_xQ\vec e_z$, an involution whose two values are the label: $\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}_L$ is the minus one and $\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}_R$ the plus one. The coefficient conjugation fixes the chirality, which is a product of units, and reverses the two generator actions, each of which carries one factor of $i$, so it preserves each value and the two values are a real splitting. In four dimensions the chirality carries the central $i$, the conjugation reverses it, and the values are exchanged. The dictionary is
$$
j_t=\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}*}\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}\bigr)\longleftrightarrow\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\frac{\partial\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial Q_x},
\qquad
j_x=\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}*}\vec e_x\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}\vec e_z\bigr)\longleftrightarrow\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\frac{\partial\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial Q_t},
$$
$$
\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}*}\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{spn}}\,i\vec e_x\bigr)\longleftrightarrow-\frac{1}{\pi\alpha}\cos\big(2\sqrt\pi\,\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}\big),
\qquad
\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu\frac{\partial j_{5\mu}}{\partial Q_\mu}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu\frac{\partial^2\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial Q_\mu^2},
$$
and the framework's contribution is to name the two sides: the fermion is an element of the algebra, read in one value for the count, and the boson is a central scalar, so that the equivalence is a correspondence between the algebra's operator algebra and the centre's exponentiated algebra. The counting $c_{\text{Dirac}}=2\times\tfrac12=1=c_{\text{boson}}$ matches the central charges, the framework's linear mass — the exchange of the two values, carried in the arena by the right factor $\vec e_y$ — bosonizes to the cosine, and the mass's central phase $\exp(i\theta)$ bosonizes to a shift of the boson field, the same central phase that weights the $\theta^{\mathrm{vac}}$ vacuum. The dictionary, the sine-Gordon equivalence, the Schwinger model, and the non-abelian case with its Wess–Zumino term are standard and transcribed.
