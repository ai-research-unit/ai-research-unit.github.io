Noether's Theorem in Biquaternionic Form

Introduction

Noether's theorem connects two things a physical theory supplies separately: its symmetries and its conservation laws. Every continuous symmetry of an action corresponds to a conserved quantity, and in a field theory to a conserved current. The theorem is not specific to any algebra, so writing it biquaternionically does not by itself produce a conservation law that was not already there. What the biquaternion algebra $\mathbb{B} = \mathbb{C}\otimes_\mathbb{R}\mathbb{H}$ fixes is the form the theorem takes: which subspace the conserved objects live in, how a symmetry acts on a biquaternion-valued field, and which biquaternion expressions are the currents.

The two ends of the theorem are both already in the read list. Relativistic Mechanics in Biquaternionic Form gives the free-particle action

$$ S = -mc\int\sqrt{-\,d\tilde R_\Phi\,d\tilde{R}_\Phi^{\natural}}, $$

whose integrand is built from the biquaternion norm of the displacement biquaternion, and it gives the conservation of the four-current in the framework form $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{J}) = 0$. This article joins the two ends: it derives the current from the symmetry of the action, for the two kinds of symmetry the framework contains.

Two kinds of symmetry. A relativistic action has

  • internal symmetries, which do not move the spacetime point — here the central phase $\tilde{Ξ}\mapsto \exp(i\alpha)\tilde{Ξ}$ of a complex biquaternion field, the symmetry whose localization is the gauge principle; and
  • spacetime symmetries, the Poincaré transformations — four translations $\tilde R_\Phi\mapsto\tilde R_\Phi+\tilde{R}_{\Phi,0}$ and six Lorentz transformations $\tilde R_\Phi\mapsto\tilde U\tilde R_\Phi\tilde U^{*}$. Translation invariance gives the conserved four-momentum; Lorentz invariance gives the conserved angular momentum.

The internal case produces a current biquaternion $\tilde{J}\in\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ with $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{J})=0$ on shell, the framework form of $\sum_\mu\partial J_\mu/\partial R_\mu = 0$. The spacetime case produces an energy–momentum current and an angular-momentum bivector.

What is established, and what is not. Established below, and recomputed: the central-phase current of the complex biquaternion scalar field,

$$ \tilde{J} = i\left[(\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{Ξ}^{*})\tilde{Ξ} - \tilde{Ξ}^{*}(\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{Ξ})\right], $$

lies in $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ and satisfies $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{J}) = i\,\mathrm{Sc}[(\Box\tilde{Ξ}^{*})\tilde{Ξ} - \tilde{Ξ}^{*}(\Box\tilde{Ξ})]$, which vanishes whenever the field obeys the Klein–Gordon equation, the sign of $\tilde{J}$ being the one for which the charge density of a positive-frequency wave is positive; the same current for the Dirac field, whose components are $j_\mu=\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}^*e_\mu\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\,b_\mu)$ with $b_t=e_t$ and $b_k=\vec e_z$, conserved at every mass, while the axial current is conserved only at $m=0$; the free-particle four-momentum $\tilde{P} = m\tilde{V}$ is the Noether charge of translation invariance, and the total four-momentum of an isolated system is conserved; and a translation acts on a plane wave by the central unit-modulus phase $\exp(i\,\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde P\tilde{R}_{\Phi,0}^{\natural}))$. What is standard, what is only transcribed, and what is interpretation is separated in its own section below.

Two defects in the read list, handled rather than repeated. First, the four-force of the parent is defined by the proper-time derivative, $\tilde{K}_{\mathrm{f}} = d\tilde{P}/d\tau$, so the sum $\sum_a\tilde{K}_{\mathrm{f},a}$ across bodies is not the rate of change of the total four-momentum unless all the Lorentz factors agree; Exercise: Four-Momentum Conservation in a Collision reports this, and the conservation law is stated correctly below. Second, the translation of a plane wave is by a central phase, $\exp(i\,\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde P\tilde{R}_{\Phi,0}^{\natural}))$, and the $i$ is not optional: the same scalar in a real exponential, $\exp(\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde P\tilde{R}_{\Phi,0}^{\natural}))$, is a dilation and not a phase at all.

Conventions. The units, the coefficient basis, the conjugations, the two sectors, the gradient and its quaternion conjugate, the d'Alembertian, the trace and the two displays are those of the read list and of the conventions files of the series; they are used here and not restated. One calibration is this article's own, and it belongs to the two coordinate charts rather than to the algebra: the gradient is taken with respect to the coefficients $Q_\mu$, with $Q_t=i c r'_t$, and the energy–momentum tensor of the later sections is written in the real coordinates $x_\mu=(c r'_{t},r_x,r_y,r_z)$, which are the same chart with $Q_t=ix_0$; the two are held apart where they are used. Throughout, $c$ is the speed of light in the medium and $c_0$ the vacuum value; $\vec v$ is reserved for particle and frame velocities.

The Dirac field, once. The Dirac content of the later sections is the element $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}=\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}+\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}$ of $\mathbb{B}$, whose two parts carry the two values: the value of $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}$ is $-1$, the value of $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}$ is $+1$, and only the zero element carries both. A product takes the value of its right factor, and left multiplication keeps the value, so the two parts are stable under multiplication on the left. The adjoint with which the currents are formed is the algebra's own conjugation, $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}^*$. The Action and What a Symmetry Is

The Particle

The free relativistic particle of rest mass $m$ has the action

$$ S[\tilde R_\Phi] = -mc\int\sqrt{-\,d\tilde R_\Phi\,d\tilde{R}_\Phi^{\natural}}, \qquad d\tilde R_\Phi = ic\,dr'_t\,e_t + d(r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z)\in\mathbb{B}_{\Phi} . $$

Because $d\tilde R_\Phi\,d\tilde{R}_\Phi^{\natural} = -c^2d{r'_{t}}^2 +dr_x^2+dr_y^2+dr_z^2 = -c^2d\tau^2$ with $\tau$ the proper time, the action is $S = -mc^2\int dr'_t/\gamma$, and the Lagrangian in the coordinate time $r'_t$ is

$$ \mathrm{Lag}(\vec v) = -mc^2\sqrt{1-\vec v^2/c^2} = -\frac{mc^2}{\gamma}, \qquad \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\vec v^2/c^2}} . $$

The Field

The relativistic scalar field of the framework is the complex biquaternion field $\tilde{Ξ} = \xi_{\mathrm{scl}}\,e_t \in \mathbb{B}_{C}$, with $\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}$ a complex scalar function. Its action is $S[\tilde{Ξ}] = \int \mathcal{L}\,d^4r$ with the real Lagrangian density

$$ \mathcal{L} = +\,\mathrm{Sc}\!\left[(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}^{*})(\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{Ξ})\right] + k_m^{2}\,\mathrm{Sc}\!\left[\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\tilde{Ξ}\right]. $$

Because $\mathrm{Sc}(e_\mu^{\natural}e_\nu)=\delta_{\mu\nu}$, the first term expands over the four coordinates with a single sign,

$$ \mathcal{L} = +\frac{\partial{\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}^{*}}{\partial R_t}\frac{\partial\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial R_t} +\sum_k\frac{\partial{\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}^{*}}{\partial R_k}\frac{\partial\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial R_k} +k_m^{2}\,{\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}^{*}\xi_{\mathrm{scl}} , $$

the uniform signs being what the $\natural$ conjugation supplies. The time term is nevertheless negative, because $\partial/\partial R_t=\partial/\partial i c r'_t$ acts on both factors and two applications of it give $-\frac{1}{c^2}\partial^2/\partial {r'_t}^2$; in the real coordinates $x_\mu=(c r'_{t},r_x,r_y,r_z)$ the same expression reads

$$ \mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{c^2}\left|\frac{\partial\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial r'_t}\right|^2 + |\nabla\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}|^2 + k_m^{2}|\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}|^2 . $$

This is the form used with the energy–momentum tensor below. The Euler–Lagrange equation of $\mathcal{L}$ is the biquaternion Klein–Gordon equation

$$ \left(\Box - k_m^{2}\right)\tilde{Ξ} = 0, $$

the overall sign of $\mathcal{L}$ being immaterial for the equation and fixed here by the positivity of the energy density. Both the action and the equation are those of the read list; $\mathcal{L}$ is exhibited only because a Noether current is read off a Lagrangian. Here $k_m=mc/\hbar$ is the mass wave number, the inverse of the mass length.

Symmetries of the Action and of the Equations

A one-parameter family $\tilde{Ξ}_\varepsilon$ with $\tilde{Ξ}_0 = \tilde{Ξ}$ is a symmetry of the action if $S[\tilde{Ξ}_\varepsilon] = S[\tilde{Ξ}]$ for every field configuration, and a symmetry of the equations of motion if it preserves the solution set. A symmetry of the action is the stronger notion, and it is the one that yields a conserved current by Noether's theorem.

The Theorem in the Two Forms the Framework Needs

For an internal symmetry, the theorem states that there is a current biquaternion $\tilde{J} = J_t\,e_t + \sum_k J_k\,\vec e_k$ whose divergence vanishes when the field obeys its equation:

$$ \delta S = 0 \ \text{for all fields} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathrm{Sc}\!\left(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{J}\right) = 0 \ \text{on shell}. $$

For a spacetime symmetry, the same construction applied to a transformation that moves the point $\tilde R_\Phi$ yields a current carrying one index per generator: for translations the energy–momentum current, for Lorentz transformations the angular-momentum current. The two forms differ only in what the symmetry variation acts on. The general identity behind both is that the divergence of the current is the field equation contracted with the symmetry variation; it is written out for the central phase in the next section and for translations after that.

The Current of the Central Phase

The Symmetry and the Current

Multiplication by a central phase,

$$ \tilde{Ξ}\ \longmapsto\ \exp(i\alpha)\tilde{Ξ}, \qquad \alpha\in\mathbb{R}, $$

leaves both terms of $\mathcal{L}$ unchanged, because $\exp(i\alpha)$ is central and $\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\mapsto \exp(-i\alpha)\tilde{Ξ}^{*}$: the two phase factors in $(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}^{*})(\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{Ξ})$ cancel, and $\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\tilde{Ξ}$ is invariant. The symmetry therefore holds for the massive field as well as the massless one, because the mass term depends on $\tilde{Ξ}$ only through the invariant $\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\tilde{Ξ}$ — a fact used again below.

For the infinitesimal variation $\delta\tilde{Ξ} = i\alpha\tilde{Ξ}$, $\delta\tilde{Ξ}^{*} = -i\alpha\tilde{Ξ}^{*}$, Noether's construction gives

$$ J_\nu = \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}/\partial R_\nu)}\,\delta\xi_{\mathrm{scl}} + \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial{\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}^{*}/\partial R_\nu)}\,\delta{\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}^{*} = i\alpha\left(\frac{\partial{\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}^{*}}{\partial R_\nu}\,\xi_{\mathrm{scl}} - \frac{\partial\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial R_\nu}\,{\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}^{*}\right), $$

the second equality from $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial(\partial\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}/\partial R_\nu) = +\partial{\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}^{*}/\partial R_\nu$ and its conjugate, which is what the Lagrangian above supplies. Setting $\alpha = 1$ and packaging the four components,

$$ \boxed{\ \tilde{J} = i\left[(\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{Ξ}^{*})\tilde{Ξ} - \tilde{Ξ}^{*}(\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{Ξ})\right] = \sum_\nu J_\nu e_\nu\ } \qquad (\tilde{Ξ} = \xi_{\mathrm{scl}}\,e_t). $$

The overall sign of a current is free — the symmetry with the opposite generator gives the opposite current — and it is fixed here by the charge density, as the next paragraph shows.

The Current Lies in the Material Sector

The temporal component in the $i c r'_t$ basis is $J_t = i\left(\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}\,\frac{\partial{\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}^{*}}{\partial R_t} - {\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}^{*}\frac{\partial\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial R_t}\right)$. Because $\partial/\partial R_t=\partial/\partial i c r'_t$ and the spatial derivatives are plain, the two kinds of component are recorded separately:

$$ J_t = -\frac{1}{c}\left({\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}^{*}\partial/\partial r'_t\,\xi_{\mathrm{scl}} - \xi_{\mathrm{scl}}\,\partial/\partial r'_t\,{\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}^{*}\right) = -\frac{2i}{c}\,\mathrm{Im}\!\left({\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}^{*}\frac{\partial\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial r'_t}\right), $$

which is purely imaginary, while the spatial components

$$ J_k = +2\,\mathrm{Im}\!\left({\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}^{*}\frac{\partial\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial R_k}\right) $$

are real, the $i$ of the current multiplying the $-2i$ of the Wronskian to $+2$. Hence $\tilde{J}\in\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$, with imaginary scalar part and real vector part, exactly as the four-current of relativistic mechanics. Writing $J_t = ic\rho$ recovers the parent's convention $\tilde{J} = ic\rho\,e_t + \vec j$, with

$$ \rho = \frac{i}{c^2}\left({\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}^{*}\partial/\partial r'_t\,\xi_{\mathrm{scl}} - \xi_{\mathrm{scl}}\,\partial/\partial r'_t\,{\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}^{*}\right), $$

which is real and positive on a positive-frequency wave $\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}\propto\exp(i(\vec k\cdot\vec r-\omega r'_t))$, where it equals $\frac{2\omega}{c^2}|\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}|^2$. This is the sign convention of The Klein–Gordon Equation in Biquaternionic Form, and it is what fixes the sign of $\tilde{J}$ above.

Conservation and the Identity Behind It

Forming the scalar part of $\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{J}$,

$$ \mathrm{Sc}\!\left(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{J}\right) = \sum_\mu\partial/\partial R_\mu\,J_\mu = \langle\tilde\nabla,\tilde J\rangle_{\natural} , $$

the sum being plain, because the signs of $\varepsilon$ are spent inside $\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}$: on the diagonal $\mathrm{Sc}(e_\mu^{\natural}e_\mu)=1$ for all four $\mu$. Using the definitions and the d'Alembertian,

$$ \boxed{\ \mathrm{Sc}\!\left(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{J}\right) = i\,\mathrm{Sc}\!\left[(\Box\tilde{Ξ}^{*})\tilde{Ξ} - \tilde{Ξ}^{*}(\Box\tilde{Ξ})\right]\ } $$

The cross terms of each $\mu$ enter with opposite signs and cancel, so only the second derivatives survive. This is the Noether identity in biquaternion form: the scalar divergence of the current is the Klein–Gordon operator applied to the field, contracted with the symmetry variation. On shell, $\Box\tilde{Ξ} = (k_m^{2})\tilde{Ξ}$ and the same for the conjugate, both terms become the same real multiple of $\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\tilde{Ξ}$ and cancel, so

$$ \mathrm{Sc}\!\left(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{J}\right) = 0 \quad\text{on shell}, $$

which is the continuity equation $\partial/\partial r'_t\rho + \mathrm{div}\,\vec j = 0$. The reason the time component needs no separate sign is the same one met above: $\partial/\partial R_t=\partial/\partial i c r'_t$ meets $J_t=ic\rho$, and the $ic$ of the variable cancels the $ic$ of the current, leaving the plain $\partial\rho/\partial r'_t$ of the continuity equation.

On What Case This Was Checked

A single plane wave is a trap: for $\tilde{Ξ} = \tilde{Ξ}_0\exp(i\,\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde P\tilde{R}_\Phi^{\natural}))$ the current is constant, so its divergence vanishes whether or not the field is on shell, and the plane wave that suggests the formula cannot test it. The conservation was therefore checked on three other cases:

  • a superposition of on-shell plane waves. With $c = \hbar = m = 1$ and the momenta $(|\vec k|,\omega) = (0,1)$, $(\tfrac34,\tfrac54)$, $(\tfrac{5}{12},\tfrac{13}{12})$ all satisfying $\omega^2 = \vec k^2 + 1$, and amplitudes $1, \tfrac12, \tfrac13$, the current has non-constant cross terms, and $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{J})$ cancels identically;
  • a standing wave, $\tilde{Ξ} = \sin(kx)\cos(\omega r'_t) + i\sin(kx)\sin(\omega r'_t)$ with $\omega^2 = c^2k^2 + c^2k_m^{2}$: this is on shell and is not a plane wave, and again $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{J})$ vanishes identically;
  • an off-shell field. A two-mode field with one mode off shell gives a divergence proportional to the field equation, explicitly nonzero. The identity above was also confirmed on a generic three-mode field, off shell: the difference $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{J}) - i\,\mathrm{Sc}[(\Box\tilde{Ξ}^{*})\tilde{Ξ} - \tilde{Ξ}^{*}(\Box\tilde{Ξ})]$ reduces to zero identically, and the divergence is $2\,\mathrm{Im}(\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\Box\tilde{Ξ})$, which is what the identity gives.

The last case is the honest statement of the theorem: the current is conserved whenever the field equation holds; off shell it need not be, and its divergence is the field equation.

Relation to the Current Already in the Corpus

The Klein–Gordon Equation in Biquaternionic Form posits the current with $\rho =\frac{i}{2\,c\,k_m}(\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\partial/\partial r'_t\tilde{Ξ} - \tilde{Ξ}\partial/\partial r'_t\tilde{Ξ}^{*})$ and $\vec j = -\frac{i c}{2\,k_m}(\tilde{Ξ}^{*}\nabla\tilde{Ξ} - \tilde{Ξ}\nabla\tilde{Ξ}^{*})$. Comparing component by component, that current equals $+\frac{c}{2\,k_m}\tilde{J}$: the Noether derivation reproduces the current the parent states, and fixes the normalization only up to the constant $\frac{c}{2\,k_m}$ that a current may always be multiplied by. What the derivation adds is why that combination is the conserved one, and that it is forced by the phase symmetry rather than chosen.

Relation to the Maxwell source. In the Maxwell article the source conservation is $\sqrt{\mu}\,\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{J}) = 0$ with $\tilde J = ic\rho\,e_t + \vec J$. Expanding, $\sqrt{\mu}\,\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{J}) = \sqrt{\mu}\,(\partial/\partial r'_t\rho + \mathrm{div}\,\vec J)$, so the Maxwell integrability condition is the same continuity equation as the conservation of $\tilde{J}$ above. The electric four-current of the material sector is the Noether current of the central phase; the gauge freedom localized in The Gauge Principle in Biquaternionic Form is the localization of the symmetry that produces it.

The Dirac Field: the Pair, the Two-Sided Form, and the Axial Current

The same phase is a symmetry of the massive Dirac field. For the Dirac field the dynamics is the chiral pair

$$ \begin{aligned} \tilde\nabla\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R} &= k_m\,\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L},\\[4pt] \tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L} &= k_m\,\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}, \end{aligned} $$

two equations, one per part, each first order and linear in the field, with $m$ central. They are the two lines of one dynamics, not two independent ones: eliminating one half gives the massive Klein–Gordon equation, and at $m=0$ the pair decouples into the two Weyl equations, $\tilde\nabla\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}=0$ and $\tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}=0$, one for each part. A central phase passes through both lines, since it passes through the central mass, so the current derived above is the conserved current of the Dirac field at every $m$, and $\langle\tilde\nabla,\tilde J\rangle_{\natural}=0$ on shell for it.

The two-sided form. Read on the whole algebra rather than on the two parts, the same dynamics is one equation in which the derivative acts on the left and the unit $\vec e_z$ on the right,

$$ \tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}} = -\,m\,i\,\bar{\tilde{Ξ}}_{\mathrm{sp}}\,\vec e_z , $$

where $\bar{\tilde{Ξ}}_{\mathrm{sp}}$ is the coefficient conjugate, taken on the four coefficients and leaving the units fixed. The mass is thus a right multiplication by $\vec e_z$ carried with the imaginary unit: the central mass of the pair becomes a product, with the unit displayed and the factor $i$ left in front, one real mass multiplying one unit and one $i$. This is the operator-theoretic content of the two-sidedness: the derivative acts on the left, the mass on the right, and the conjugation supplies the antilinear part, so the second field of the $\natural$-side equation is the conjugate of the first carried on the right. The one-sided pair is the definition; this is its form on the algebra, one line, with the unit $\vec e_z$ standing where the second part stood.

The currents on the algebra. With the algebra's conjugation as the adjoint, the central-phase current of the Dirac field has the components

$$ j_\mu = \mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}^*\,e_\mu\,\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\,b_\mu\bigr), \qquad b_t = e_t,\quad b_k = \vec e_z\ \ (k=x,y,z), $$

the unit of the coordinate inserted between the field and its adjoint: $j_t = \mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}^*\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}})$ is the Hermitian norm of the field, and $j_k = \mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}^*\vec e_k\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\vec e_z)$ is the component of the current along $\vec e_k$. The axial rotation assigns opposite phases to the two parts, so the mass term, which pairs them, is not invariant under it; the axial current therefore carries the unit $\vec e_z$ inside the product,

$$ j_{5\mu} = \mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}^*\,i\,e_\mu\,\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\,\vec e_z\vec e_{\tau(\mu)}\vec e_x\bigr), \qquad \tau(t)=x,\ \ \tau(k)=y\ \ (k=x,y,z), $$

which reduces on the two cases to

$$ j_{5t} = -\,i\,\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}^*\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\vec e_z\bigr), \qquad j_{5k} = i\,\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}^*\vec e_k\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\bigr). $$

The right multiplier of the general form is the product $\vec e_z\vec e_{\tau(\mu)}$ in that order, and it is not symmetric: writing the two units the other way round flips the sign of the current. The divergence is the plain sum of the four derivatives, since the $\natural$ in $\langle\tilde\nabla,\tilde J_5\rangle_{\natural}$ already carries the relative sign of the spatial derivatives; the mass is the obstruction:

$$ \langle\tilde\nabla,\tilde J_5\rangle_{\natural} = \sum_\mu\frac{\partial j_{5\mu}}{\partial R_\mu} = 2im\,\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}^*\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\vec e_y\bigr), $$

using $\vec e_z\vec e_x=\vec e_y$, and this vanishes only at $m=0$. The same statement without the units on show: the axial rotation multiplies each part by a phase opposite to the other's, and on the algebra it is a right multiplication,

$$ \tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\ \longmapsto\ \tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\,(\cos\alpha+\sin\alpha\,\vec e_z), $$

which multiplies $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}$ by $\exp(-i\alpha)$ and $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}$ by $\exp(+i\alpha)$, because right multiplication by $\vec e_z$ acts on the two parts as $-i$ and $+i$. That is why the axial current carries the unit $\vec e_z$ and not a separate object.

What the mass breaks, and what it does not. What the mass term breaks is the axial rotation, not the phase. The mass term is $-mi\bar{\tilde{Ξ}}_{\mathrm{sp}}\vec e_z$: one right factor $\vec e_z$ between the field and its conjugate. It is invariant under the central phase, which multiplies the field by a central scalar and leaves every unit alone, so both sides of the equation take the same phase and it is carried to itself; and it is not invariant under the axial rotation, which replaces that right factor by $\cos\alpha\,\vec e_z-\sin\alpha\,e_t$, since $\vec e_z\vec e_z=-e_t$. In the biquaternion reading the mass couples the two parts off the diagonal, so it breaks exactly the rotation that moves them against each other, and not the central $\mathrm{U}(1)$ that the algebra canonically carries. The previously recorded obstruction belonged to the retired antilinear single-field equation; the gauge principle article's corresponding section is revised on the same grounds.

Translation Invariance and the Conserved Four-Momentum

The Particle Charge

The action $S[\tilde R_\Phi]$ above depends on $\tilde R_\Phi$ only through $d\tilde R_\Phi$, so the constant shift

$$ \tilde R_\Phi\ \longmapsto\ \tilde R_\Phi + \tilde{R}_{\Phi,0}, \qquad \tilde{R}_{\Phi,0}\in\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}\ \text{constant}, $$

is a symmetry of the action. The Noether charges are the momentum conjugate to $r_x\,\vec e_x+r_y\,\vec e_y+r_z\,\vec e_z$ and the energy conjugate to $r'_t$; computing the first from the Lagrangian,

$$ \vec p = \frac{\partial \mathrm{Lag}}{\partial\vec v} = \gamma m\vec v, \qquad E = \vec p\cdot\vec v - \mathrm{Lag} = \gamma mc^2, $$

and combining them into the four-momentum,

$$ \boxed{\ \tilde{P} = m\tilde{V} = i\frac{E}{c}\,e_t + \vec p\ } \qquad (\tilde{V} = \gamma(ic\,e_t + \vec v)). $$

The mass-shell relation $\tilde{P}\tilde{P}^{\natural} = -m^{2}\,c^{2}$ follows, since $\tilde{P}\tilde{P}^{\natural}=-(E/c)^2+\vec p^{\,2}$ and $E^2=\vec p^{\,2}c^2+m^2c^4$. Translation invariance is thus the symmetry whose Noether charge is the four-momentum; the four-momentum is conserved for a free particle, $d\tilde{P}/d\tau = 0$, because $\mathrm{Lag}$ has no explicit $\tilde{Q}$-dependence. As an independent check, a particle with $m = c = 1$, $\vec p = (0.6,0,0)$ and $E = \sqrt{1.36}$ was boosted by the rotor $\tilde U = \cosh(\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}/2) + i\sinh(\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}/2)\vec e_x$ with $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}} = 1.2$, the conjugation being $\tilde U\tilde P\tilde U^{*}$: the result is $E' = 1.206$ and $p'_x = -0.674$, which are the component formulas $E'=\cosh\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}\,E-\sinh\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}\,p_x$ and $p'_x=\cosh\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}\,p_x-\sinh\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}\,E$, and the biquaternion norm is $-1$ in both frames, confirming that the Noether charge transforms as a four-vector.

The Conservation Law for a System, Stated Correctly

For an isolated system of bodies $a$ the conserved object is the total four-momentum, and the correct statement is

$$ \boxed{\;\tilde{P}_{\mathrm{tot}} = \sum_a \tilde{P}_a, \qquad \frac{d\tilde{P}_{\mathrm{tot}}}{dr'_t} = 0\;}, $$

a vanishing coordinate-time derivative. The parent defines the four-force by the proper-time derivative of a single body, $\tilde{K}_{\mathrm{f},a} = d\tilde{P}_a/d\tau_a$, and since $d\tau_a = dr'_t/\gamma_a$,

$$ \frac{d\tilde{P}_a}{dr'_t} = \frac{1}{\gamma_a}\tilde{K}_{\mathrm{f},a}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \sum_a \tilde{K}_{\mathrm{f},a} = \sum_a \gamma_a\frac{d\tilde{P}_a}{dr'_t}, $$

which equals $d\tilde{P}_{\mathrm{tot}}/dr'_t$ only when all the $\gamma_a$ are equal. The parent's symbol $\tilde{K}_{\mathrm{f}}$ is a per-body proper-time rate and cannot be summed across bodies. The conservation law in terms of the parent's four-forces is therefore

$$ \sum_a \frac{1}{\gamma_a}\tilde{K}_{\mathrm{f},a} = \frac{d\tilde{P}_{\mathrm{tot}}}{dr'_t} = 0, $$

or, in the integrated form that avoids the derivative altogether, $\sum_{\mathrm{in}}\tilde{P} = \sum_{\mathrm{out}}\tilde{P}$ for a collision. The exercise reports this defect; the corrected statement above is the one to use.

A numerical witness. Two bodies connected by a translation-invariant interaction exchange momentum, so at some instant $d\tilde{P}_2/dr'_t = -d\tilde{P}_1/dr'_t = i\,e_t + \vec e_x$, and their Lorentz factors differ, $\gamma_1 = 5/3$, $\gamma_2 = 5/4$. Then $d\tilde{P}_{\mathrm{tot}}/dr'_t = 0$, but

$$ \tilde{K}_{\mathrm{f},1} + \tilde{K}_{\mathrm{f},2} = (\gamma_2 - \gamma_1)\left(i\,e_t + \vec e_x\right) = -\frac{5}{12}\left(i\,e_t + \vec e_x\right) \neq 0 . $$

The naive sum of the parent's four-forces is not zero even though the total four-momentum is conserved. For the collision of Exercise: Four-Momentum Conservation in a Collision ($E_1 = 3mc^2$, equal masses, $\theta^* = 90^\circ$), the total four-momenta $\tilde{P}_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $\tilde{P}_{\mathrm{out}}$ were recomputed and agree component by component in the laboratory.

What Translation Does to a Plane Wave

A free field is a plane wave, and a translation acts on it by a central phase. With the series' phase convention $\tilde{Ξ} = \tilde{Ξ}_0\exp(i\,\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde P\tilde{R}_\Phi^{\natural}))$, whose phase is $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde P\tilde{R}_\Phi^{\natural}) = \vec k\cdot\vec r-\omega r'_t$, and with $\tilde P\in\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ and $\tilde R_\Phi\mapsto\tilde R_\Phi+\tilde{R}_{\Phi,0}$,

$$ \tilde{Ξ}\big|_{\tilde R_\Phi+\tilde{R}_{\Phi,0}} = \exp\!\bigl(i\,\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde P\tilde{R}_{\Phi,0}^{\natural})\bigr)\,\tilde{Ξ}\big|_{\tilde R_\Phi}, \qquad \tilde{Ξ}_0\ \text{constant}. $$

The multiplier is central and of unit modulus, because $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde P\tilde{R}_{\Phi,0}^{\natural}) = \langle\tilde P,\tilde{R}_{\Phi,0}\rangle_{\natural}$ is real: writing $\tilde P = i\frac{\omega}{c}e_t + \vec k$ and $\tilde{R}_{\Phi,0} = i R_{0t} e_t + \vec R_0$, with $R_{0t} = c r'_{t}_0$,

$$ \mathrm{Sc}\!\left(\tilde P\tilde{R}_{\Phi,0}^{\natural}\right) = -\frac{\omega R_{0t}}{c} + \vec k\cdot\vec R_0 = \vec k\cdot\vec R_0-\omega r'_{t,0} . $$

What makes it a phase is the $i$: the same scalar in a real exponential is a dilation. For $\tilde P = 2i\,e_t + 0.7\vec e_x - 0.3\vec e_y + 0.5\vec e_z$ and $\tilde{R}_{\Phi,0} = 0.4i\,e_t + 0.25\vec e_x - 0.35\vec e_y + 0.15\vec e_z$ (units $c = 1$), $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde P\tilde{R}_{\Phi,0}^{\natural}) = -0.445$ and the multiplier is $\exp(-0.445i)$, of modulus $1$, whereas $\exp(\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde P\tilde{R}_{\Phi,0}^{\natural})) = e^{-0.445}$ has modulus $0.641$. The $\natural$ inside the pairing must be the one the wave itself carries; the un-conjugated $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde P\tilde{R}_{\Phi,0}) = -\frac{\omega R_{0t}}{c}-\vec k\cdot\vec R_0$ belongs to the opposite sign convention for $\vec k$ in the phase, and mixing the two flips the sign of $\vec k\cdot\vec R_0$ alone. This central phase is the finite, field-space form of the translation subgroup; its infinitesimal generator is the derivation $\partial/\partial R_\mu$, and it is not a rotor, as The Poincaré Group and the Biquaternion Frame establishes.

The Energy–Momentum of the Field

The Canonical Tensor

The same translation symmetry applied to the field action gives the canonical energy–momentum, the Noether current of spacetime translations. For the complex scalar field it is

$$ \boxed{\;T_{\mu\nu} = \frac{\partial{\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}^*}{\partial x_\mu}\frac{\partial\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial x_\nu} + \frac{\partial\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial x_\mu}\frac{\partial{\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}^*}{\partial x_\nu} + \varepsilon_\mu\delta_{\mu\nu}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{scl},\xi}\;} $$

with the scalar Lagrangian of the same coordinates,

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{scl},\xi} = \sum_\mu\frac{\partial{\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}^*}{\partial R_\mu}\frac{\partial\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial R_\mu} + k_m^{2}|\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}|^2 = -\frac{1}{c^2}\left|\frac{\partial\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial r'_t}\right|^2 + |\nabla\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}|^2 + k_m^{2}|\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}|^2 . $$

Here $x_\mu=(c r'_{t},r_x,r_y,r_z)$ are the real coordinates, the chart of $Q_\mu$ with the $i$ of the time coefficient removed, $Q_t=ix_0$: $Q_0=i c r'_t$ carries the $i$, while $x_0=c r'_{t}$ does not. Both indices of $T$ are low, the first the row. The coefficient of $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{scl},\xi}$ is the plain form of the two units, $\mathrm{Sc}(e_\mu e_\nu)=\varepsilon_\mu\delta_{\mu\nu}$: plus on the time entry and minus on the three space entries. The derivative index of the conservation law carries one minus on the time direction and none on the three space directions, and that minus is the square of the $i$ in $Q_t=i c r'_t$, spent once and written out. Translation invariance gives

$$ -\frac{\partial T_{0\nu}}{\partial x_0}+\sum_k\frac{\partial T_{k\nu}}{\partial r_k} = 0 $$

on shell. Its time–time component is the energy density,

$$ T_{00} = \frac{1}{c^2}\left|\frac{\partial\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}{\partial r'_t}\right|^2 + |\nabla\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}|^2 + k_m^{2}|\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}|^2 > 0, $$

positive for every configuration because each term is a square. This was checked by recomputation on an on-shell superposition of two plane waves: the four divergences $-\partial T_{0\nu}/\partial x_0+\sum_k\partial T_{k\nu}/\partial r_k$ vanish to $10^{-10}$, the tensor is symmetric, and $T_{00}$ equals the expression above.

Why the Framework Does Not Write It as One Biquaternion

A symmetric rank-two tensor in four dimensions has ten independent components, while a biquaternion has four complex coefficients, that is eight real components, and $\tilde{W} = \tfrac12\tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{*}$ carries only four of them. So no single biquaternion can be $T_{\mu\nu}$; the framework writes the translation current as a bigeneral quaternionic bilinear, one basis element for each index. Exercise: The Electromagnetic Energy–Momentum Tensor constructs exactly this for the Maxwell field, $\Pi_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{F}\mathcal{E}_\mu\tilde{F}^{*}\mathcal{E}_\nu)$ with the first index the row, and shows that the four-component object is its time row. The framework's energy–momentum biquaternion is therefore not the whole Noether current of translations; it is the energy–flux part of it.

The Maxwell Case in the Corpus Form

For the electromagnetic field the four-component object is $\tilde{W} = \tfrac12\tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{*} = W\,e_t + \frac{i}{c}\vec S\in\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$, with $W$ the energy density and $\vec S = \vec E\times\vec H$ the Poynting vector. Its scalar divergence is the energy law,

$$ \mathrm{Sc}\!\left(\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{W}\right) = -\frac{i}{c}\left(\frac{\partial W}{\partial r'_t}+\mathrm{div}\,\vec S\right) = \frac{i}{c}\,\vec J\cdot\vec E , $$

the last equality being the Poynting theorem. The factor $i$ is uniform across the two terms, as a temporal component must be in the $i c r'_t$ convention, and dividing it out recovers $\partial W/\partial r'_t + \mathrm{div}\,\vec S + \vec J\cdot\vec E = 0$. The real form $\tilde W = W + \frac{1}{c}\vec S$ fails exactly this test: its scalar divergence carries the $i$ on the time term only, and on a free plane wave it equals $\frac{1-i}{c}\partial W/\partial r'_t$, which is not zero. What Exercise: The Electromagnetic Energy–Momentum Tensor isolates is that factor $i$ on the Poynting part, and the equation $\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{W} = -\tilde{\mathcal F}$, taken as a biquaternion identity with a power–force density on the right, is not what the corpus establishes for either form: what it verifies is the scalar statement above, not the vector one. Read as a Noether statement, $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{W}) = \frac{i}{c}\vec J\cdot\vec E$ says the translation current is conserved up to the work done on the charges — the source is present, so the field alone is not closed.

A Caution on the $i c r'_t$ Convention

The energy density is not $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{scl},\xi}$ and is not obtained from it by a flat sum: $T_{00}=\frac{1}{c^2}|\dot\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}|^2+|\nabla\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}|^2+k_m^{2}|\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}|^2$ while $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{scl},\xi}=-\frac{1}{c^2}|\dot\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}|^2+|\nabla\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}|^2+k_m^{2}|\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}|^2$, with the mass wave number $k_m=mc/\hbar$ and its reciprocal $k_m=mc/\hbar$. The gradient and $\Box$ live in the $Q_\mu$ frame, where $Q_0=i c r'_t$; the tensor lives in $x_\mu=(c r'_{t},r_x,r_y,r_z)$, where the time component is real and the minus is written on the time derivative. Mixing the two charts produces the wrong sign on the time derivative, and the mismatch is the same $1/i^2=-1$ that makes the time term of the uniform $Q$-frame sum of $\mathcal{L}$ negative once written in the real coordinates. This is the same caution the energy–momentum exercise records for the trace.

Lorentz Invariance and the Angular-Momentum Bivector

The Particle Bivector

Lorentz invariance is a spacetime symmetry like translation, and it has a Noether current too. For a free particle the conserved quantity is the antisymmetric pair

$$ L_{\mu\nu} = x_\mu p_\nu - x_\nu p_\mu, \qquad \frac{dL_{\mu\nu}}{d\tau} = \frac{dx_\mu}{d\tau}p_\nu - \frac{dx_\nu}{d\tau}p_\mu = \frac{p_\mu p_\nu-p_\nu p_\mu}{m} = 0, $$

using $dx_\mu/d\tau = p_\mu/m$ and $dp_\mu/d\tau = 0$ for a free particle; the conservation of the six independent components is the conservation of angular momentum and of the boost charges. In the biquaternion algebra the antisymmetric pair is a bivector, the same representation the field strength occupies: writing

$$ \boxed{\;\tilde L = \tfrac12\sum_{\mu,\nu}L_{\mu\nu}\,e^{\natural}_\mu e_\nu\;} $$

the angular momentum of a free particle is a single biquaternion. The tilde rather than an arrow is deliberate: the vector part of $\tilde L$ is $L_{0k}-\tfrac12\epsilon_{kij}L_{ij}$ along $\vec e_k$, which pairs the boost charges with $\vec r\times\vec p$ and is not the three-vector of orbital angular momentum. This is the same construction by which $\tilde{F} = \mathrm{Vect}(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{A}) = \tfrac12\sum_{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}e^{\natural}_\mu e_\nu$ packages the antisymmetric field strength, and the Lorentz generators themselves are bivectors.

The Rank-Three Field Current

For a field, the Noether current of Lorentz invariance is a rank-three object,

$$ L_{\mu\nu\rho} = x_\nu T_{\mu\rho} - x_\rho T_{\mu\nu} + S_{\mu\nu\rho}, $$

whose conservation

$$ -\frac{\partial L_{0\nu\rho}}{\partial x_0}+\sum_k\frac{\partial L_{k\nu\rho}}{\partial r_k} = 0 $$

expresses the symmetry of the energy–momentum tensor together with the conservation of spin. Being rank three, it is not a biquaternion and not even a single bilinear; the orbital and spin pieces separate, with the spin term vanishing for the classical scalar field. The particle bivector $\tilde L$ above is the finite-dimensional case where the rank-two object is the whole story.

Summary

Noether's theorem in biquaternionic form joins the two ends that Relativistic Mechanics in Biquaternionic Form states separately, the free-particle action and the conserved four-current, by deriving the second from the first. The theorem has two forms. For an internal symmetry, that of the central phase $\tilde{Ξ}\mapsto \exp(i\alpha)\tilde{Ξ}$ of the complex biquaternion scalar field, the derived current is

$$ \tilde{J} = i\left[(\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{Ξ}^{*})\tilde{Ξ} - \tilde{Ξ}^{*}(\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{Ξ})\right] = \sum_\nu J_\nu e_\nu, \qquad J_\nu = i\left(\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}\,\partial/\partial R_\nu\,{\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}^{*} - {\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}}^{*}\partial/\partial R_\nu\,\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}\right), $$

which lies in $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ and satisfies the Noether identity

$$ \mathrm{Sc}\!\left(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\tilde{J}\right) = i\,\mathrm{Sc}\!\left[(\Box\tilde{Ξ}^{*})\tilde{Ξ} - \tilde{Ξ}^{*}(\Box\tilde{Ξ})\right], $$

zero whenever the field is on shell. This is the framework form of $\langle\tilde\nabla,\tilde J\rangle_{\natural} = 0$; it reproduces the current the Klein–Gordon article posits, up to the conventional constant $c/k_m/2$, with $\rho>0$ on a positive-frequency wave; and its expansion at the Maxwell source is the charge-conservation condition $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{\nabla}^{\natural}\sqrt{\mu}\,\tilde{J}) = 0$.

The same phase acts on the Dirac field, whose dynamics is the chiral pair $\tilde\nabla\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}=k_m\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}$, $\tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}=k_m\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}$, equivalently the one equation on the algebra $\tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}=-mi\bar{\tilde{Ξ}}_{\mathrm{sp}}\vec e_z$. The vector current has the components $j_\mu=\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}^*e_\mu\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\,b_\mu)$, with $b_t=e_t$ and $b_k=\vec e_z$, conserved at every $m$; the axial current carries the unit $\vec e_z$ inside the product and obeys $\sum_\mu\partial j_{5\mu}/\partial R_\mu = 2im\,\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}^*\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}\vec e_y)$, vanishing only at $m=0$. The mass therefore breaks the axial rotation and not the phase.

For a spacetime symmetry, translation invariance gives the four-momentum

$$ \tilde{P} = m\tilde{V} = i\frac{E}{c}e_t + \vec p, \qquad E = \gamma mc^2,\quad \vec p = \gamma m\vec v, $$

as the Noether charge, conserved for a free particle. The conservation law for a system is the vanishing of the coordinate-time derivative of the total, $d\tilde{P}_{\mathrm{tot}}/dr'_t = 0$; the parent's proper-time four-force cannot be summed across bodies, and the correct form is $\sum_a\tilde{K}_{\mathrm{f},a}/\gamma_a = 0$, or the integrated $\sum_{\mathrm{in}}\tilde{P} = \sum_{\mathrm{out}}\tilde{P}$. A translation acts on a plane wave by the central unit-modulus phase $\exp(i\,\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde P\tilde{R}_{\Phi,0}^{\natural}))$, with $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde P\tilde{R}_{\Phi,0}^{\natural}) = \vec k\cdot\vec R_0-\omega r'_{t,0}$.

The field translation current is the canonical energy–momentum, $-\partial T_{0\nu}/\partial x_0+\sum_k\partial T_{k\nu}/\partial r_k = 0$ on shell in the real coordinates, with positive energy density $T_{00} = \frac{1}{c^2}|\partial\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}/\partial r'_t|^2 + |\nabla\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}|^2 + k_m^{2}|\xi_{\mathrm{scl}}|^2$. Rank two cannot be one biquaternion, so the framework packages it bilinearly; the Maxwell object $\tilde{W} = \tfrac12\tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{*}$ is its time row, and $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{W}) = \frac{i}{c}\vec J\cdot\vec E$ is the energy law it carries. Lorentz invariance gives the angular-momentum bivector $\tilde L = \tfrac12\sum L_{\mu\nu}\,e^{\natural}_\mu e_\nu$, with the rank-three field current reduced to the rank-two particle bivector only when the spin term vanishes. The theorem is standard; the algebra supplies the home of each conserved object and the compact form of its divergence, and it supplies no conservation law the symmetries did not already give.
