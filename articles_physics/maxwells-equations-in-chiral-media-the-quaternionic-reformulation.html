Maxwell's Equations in Chiral Media — The Quaternionic Reformulation

Introduction

A chiral medium responds to an electric excitation with a magnetic polarisation and to a magnetic excitation with an electric one. This magnetoelectric coupling is what makes such a medium optically active: the two circular polarisations propagate with different wavenumbers, so the plane of a linearly polarised wave rotates as the wave advances. The effect is not exotic. A multitude of organic molecules, among them the DNA molecule, are chiral at some frequencies, the pupil of the eye is a chiral medium, and chiral materials are now manufactured and used in engineering [Kravchenko and Oviedo 2003].

The companion article Electromagnetism in Media — The Local Complex Structure at Work treats a medium through its two ordinary parameters, the permittivity $\epsilon$ and the permeability $\mu$, and leaves the constitutive relations $\mathbf{D}=\epsilon\mathbf{E}$, $\mathbf{B}=\mu\mathbf{H}$ untouched. A chiral medium needs a third parameter. This article records the quaternionic reformulation of the time-harmonic Maxwell system for a chiral medium given by V. V. Kravchenko and H. Oviedo [2003], and the applications they draw from it: integral representations for the fields, a complete solution of the extendability problem, the electromagnetic energy balance, and solutions in an inhomogeneous, slowly changing (stratified) chiral medium.

The reformulation is a diagonalisation. Two complex quaternionic combinations of the fields are formed, one for each circular polarisation, and each satisfies a first-order quaternionic equation of Beltrami type whose constant is one of the two chiral wavenumbers. The value of writing the equations quaternionically rather than as the ordinary vector Beltrami equations $\mathrm{rot}\,\Phi=\alpha_2\Phi$ and $\mathrm{rot}\,\Psi=-\alpha_1\Psi$ is that the quaternionic equations carry the whole spatial function theory — the Borel–Pompeiu formula, the Cauchy integral formula and the Plemelj–Sokhotski formulas — and the authors use exactly that theory to obtain the integral representations and the extendability theorem.

A word on the word. In this series chiral usually names a property of a fermion: the inequivalence of the left- and right-handed components of a spinor, the subject of Chiral Fermions in the Biquaternion Framework. Here it names a property of a material medium — an electric–magnetic cross-response. The two senses are unrelated, and the word is disambiguated here once and not again.

Conventions. The biquaternion algebra is $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$ with basis $e_0=1,e_1,e_2,e_3$, $e_k^2=-e_0$, and scalar imaginary $i$ commuting with the units. The fields are time-harmonic, taken in the $e^{-i\omega t}$ convention, so that $\partial_t\to-i\omega$ and the four Maxwell equations become algebraic in $\omega$. In this article the only differential operator that appears is the spatial Moisil–Theodoresco operator

$$ D_3=e_1\partial_1+e_2\partial_2+e_3\partial_3, \qquad D_3^2=-\Delta, \qquad \Delta=\partial_1^2+\partial_2^2+\partial_3^2, $$

with complex coefficients; it is the operator written $D_3$ in Quaternion Regular Functions and it is the spatial part of the four-dimensional biquaternionic gradient $\tilde\nabla=e_0\partial_{ict}+D_3$. The source writes the quaternion units $i_1,i_2,i_3$ and the operator $D$; the translation to $e_k$ and $D_3$ is used throughout. The speed of light in the medium is $c=1/\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}$ and the medium wavenumber is $k=\omega\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}=\omega/c$, as in Electromagnetism in Media. Bold letters are three-vectors and the scalar product is $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$.

Chiral Media and the Drude–Born–Fedorov Relations

The defining feature of a chiral medium is that the electric displacement and the magnetic induction each respond to both fields. The constitutive relations used here are the Drude–Born–Fedorov relations

$$ \mathbf{D}=\epsilon\bigl(\mathbf{E}+\beta\,\mathrm{rot}\,\mathbf{E}\bigr), \qquad \mathbf{B}=\mu\bigl(\mathbf{H}+\beta\,\mathrm{rot}\,\mathbf{H}\bigr), $$

where $\epsilon$ and $\mu$ are the ordinary permittivity and permeability and $\beta$ is the chirality measure of the medium, the Pasteur parameter. The parameter $\beta$ has the dimension of a length; the combination $k\beta$ that will appear below is dimensionless. Two limiting cases bracket the class: $\beta=0$ is the ordinary isotropic medium of the companion article, and the relations then return $\mathbf{D}=\epsilon\mathbf{E}$, $\mathbf{B}=\mu\mathbf{H}$.

Three consequences are worth stating, because they are what a reader who has not met a chiral medium needs.

  • Simultaneous polarisation. The relations say that an electric excitation polarises the medium magnetically as well as electrically, and conversely. This is the magnetoelectric coupling.
  • Optical activity. Because the two circular polarisations are affected oppositely — the factor $\beta\,\mathrm{rot}$ is first order in the wave vector and flips sign with the handedness of the polarisation — they acquire different effective wavenumbers. A linearly polarised wave, which is the sum of the two circular ones, therefore has its plane of polarisation rotated along the path. This is circular birefringence, and it is the observable that identifies an optically active medium.
  • A continuity equation. The field equations imply a conserved charge. In the time-harmonic convention the conservation law is $\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{j}-i\omega\rho=0$, and taking the divergence of the second field equation gives $\mathrm{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}=-(i/k)\,\mathrm{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{j}}$, while $\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{E}=\rho/\epsilon$ gives $\mathrm{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}=\rho/(\epsilon\sqrt{\mu})$. Together,

$$ \mathrm{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}=-\frac{i}{k}\,\mathrm{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{j}}=\frac{\rho}{\epsilon\sqrt{\mu}}, \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{j}}=\frac{\mathbf{j}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}} . $$

It is this identity that converts the source terms of the reformulation into the compact form $(18)$ below. The paper writes it as $\rho/\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}=-(i/k)\,\mathrm{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{j}}$, that is, it prints $\rho/\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}$ for the scalar $\mathrm{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}=\rho/(\epsilon\sqrt{\mu})$; the two differ by $\sqrt{\epsilon}$, a normalisation convention in the printed charge that does not enter the reformulation or the sourceless system.

The physical examples — DNA, the pupil of the eye, organic molecules, manufactured chiral metamaterials — fix the meaning of the class. They also warn against a purely formal reading: a chiral medium is not an abstract perturbation of an isotropic one but a distinct and experimentally rich class, whose linear response is the one described by the two relations above.

The Reformulation: Diagonalising the Chiral Maxwell System

The normalised system

Write the fields in the dimensionless normalisation

$$ \tilde{\mathbf{E}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\,\mathbf{E}, \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{H}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\,\mathbf{H}, \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{j}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\,\mathbf{j}, $$

which balances the electric and magnetic halves in the same way as the field-strength biquaternion $\tilde F=i\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\mathbf{E}-\sqrt{\mu}\,\mathbf{H}$ of The Field-Strength Biquaternion and Its Invariants. Substituting the Drude–Born–Fedorov relations in the time-harmonic Maxwell equations and using $k=\omega\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}$ gives the chiral system

$$ \mathrm{rot}\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}=ik\bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}+\beta\,\mathrm{rot}\,\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\bigr), \qquad \mathrm{rot}\,\tilde{\mathbf{H}}=-ik\bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{E}}+\beta\,\mathrm{rot}\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\bigr)+\tilde{\mathbf{j}} . \tag{12} $$

At $\beta=0$ this is the ordinary pair $\mathrm{rot}\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}=ik\tilde{\mathbf{H}}$, $\mathrm{rot}\,\tilde{\mathbf{H}}=-ik\tilde{\mathbf{E}}+\tilde{\mathbf{j}}$, so the chiral system is the ordinary one with the rotation terms shifted by $\beta\,\mathrm{rot}$.

The diagonalising combinations

Introduce the two purely vectorial biquaternions

$$ \Phi=\tilde{\mathbf{E}}+i\tilde{\mathbf{H}}, \qquad \Psi=\tilde{\mathbf{E}}-i\tilde{\mathbf{H}}, \tag{14} $$

inverse to $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}=\tfrac12(\Phi+\Psi)$, $\tilde{\mathbf{H}}=\tfrac12(\Phi-\Psi)/i$. For real fields the two are complex conjugates, $\Psi=\Phi^{*}$; they are the left- and right-handed halves of the field in the sense of circular polarisation, not the chiral halves of a spinor of the companion article on chiral fermions. Applying $D_3=\sum_ke_k\partial_k$ and using $D_3f=-\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{f}+\mathrm{rot}\,\mathbf{f}$ on a pure vector, the cross terms close and the equations $(12)$ become

$$ \Bigl(D_3-\frac{k}{1-k\beta}\Bigr)\Phi=\frac{i}{k}\bigl(\alpha_2\,\tilde{\mathbf{j}}+\mathrm{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{j}}\bigr), \qquad \Bigl(D_3+\frac{k}{1+k\beta}\Bigr)\Psi=-\frac{i}{k}\bigl(\alpha_1\,\tilde{\mathbf{j}}-\mathrm{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{j}}\bigr), \tag{18} $$

with

$$ \alpha_1=\frac{k}{1+k\beta}, \qquad \alpha_2=\frac{k}{1-k\beta}. \tag{17} $$

The derivation is a two-line computation. From $(12)$, $\mathrm{rot}\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}=ik\tilde{\mathbf{H}}+ik\beta\,\mathrm{rot}\,\tilde{\mathbf{H}}$ and $\mathrm{rot}\,\tilde{\mathbf{H}}=-ik\tilde{\mathbf{E}}-ik\beta\,\mathrm{rot}\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}+\tilde{\mathbf{j}}$, so

$$ D_3\Phi=-\mathrm{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}+\mathrm{rot}\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}+i\,\mathrm{rot}\,\tilde{\mathbf{H}} =k\Phi+k\beta\bigl(\mathrm{rot}\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}+i\,\mathrm{rot}\,\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\bigr)+i\tilde{\mathbf{j}}-\mathrm{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}, $$

and the bracket is $D_3\Phi+\mathrm{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$. Collecting the $D_3\Phi$ terms gives $(D_3-\alpha_2)\Phi=i\tilde{\mathbf{j}}/(1-k\beta)-\mathrm{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$, and the continuity identity of the preceding section, $\mathrm{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}=-(i/k)\,\mathrm{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{j}}$, turns the right-hand side into the first of equations $(18)$. The second follows by the same computation with $i\to-i$, which exchanges $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ and reverses the signs.

The sourceless system

In the absence of charges and currents, $\rho=0$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{j}}=0$, and $(18)$ reduces to the diagonal system

$$ \bigl(D_3-\alpha_2\bigr)\Phi=0, \qquad \bigl(D_3+\alpha_1\bigr)\Psi=0 . \tag{20} $$

Each equation is first order, scalar-shifted, and involves only one of the two combinations. Written in vectors, $(20)$ is the pair of Beltrami equations $\mathrm{rot}\,\Phi-\alpha_2\Phi=0$ and $\mathrm{rot}\,\Psi+\alpha_1\Psi=0$; purely vectorial solutions of $(D_3+\alpha)\mathbf{f}=0$ are the Beltrami fields, also called force-free fields. The reformulation is the procedure the authors call the Bohren transformation [Lakhtakia, Varadan and Varadan 1989]. Their point is not the vector form, which is classical, but that the quaternionic form $(20)$ is an equation of a type whose solutions carry a complete function theory, and that the results of the next sections are not reachable within three-dimensional vector calculus.

A bridge to the field-strength biquaternion. The combination $\Phi$ is, up to a constant, the object the rest of the series calls the field strength. Let $\mathbf E,\mathbf H$ be the physical fields, $\tilde{\mathbf E}=\mathbf E/\sqrt{\mu}$ and $\tilde{\mathbf H}=\mathbf H/\sqrt{\epsilon}$ the normalised ones entering $\Phi$ (the source's §4 convention), and $\tilde F=i\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\mathbf{E}-\sqrt{\mu}\,\mathbf{H}$ the field strength of the rest of the series (the source's §7 convention). With $c=1/\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}$,

$$ \Phi=\tilde{\mathbf{E}}+i\tilde{\mathbf{H}}=-ic\,\tilde F=ic\,\tilde F^{\natural}, $$

the two expressions agreeing because $\tilde F$ is a pure vector and $\tilde F^{\natural}=-\tilde F$ on the vectors. The chiral system is therefore the statement that $\Phi$ obeys a single first-order quaternionic equation with a scalar shift; the companion combination $\Psi$ obeys the shifted equation of the opposite shift. That is the precise sense in which the reformulation is a diagonalisation of the Maxwell operator by a complex structure already present in the algebra.

Two Wavenumbers and Optical Activity

At $\beta=0$ the two constants $(17)$ coincide, $\alpha_1=\alpha_2=k$, the two equations $(20)$ are the same equation, and the diagonalisation reduces to the ordinary electromagnetic split of the field into its two circularly polarised halves. At $\beta\neq0$ they separate, and the separation is the physics. The three combinations worth recording are

$$ \alpha_1\alpha_2=\frac{k^2}{1-k^2\beta^2}, \qquad \alpha_2-\alpha_1=\frac{2k^2\beta}{1-k^2\beta^2}, \qquad \alpha_1+\alpha_2=\frac{2k}{1-k^2\beta^2}. $$

The difference $\alpha_2-\alpha_1$ is the optical activity. A plane wave of either combination has the Beltrami form: an amplitude perpendicular to the wave vector with a circular polarisation. For the two amplitudes $\Phi_0=\Psi_0=\hat{\mathbf{e}}_1+i\hat{\mathbf{e}}_2$, with $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_1,\hat{\mathbf{e}}_2,\hat{\mathbf{k}}$ a right-handed triad, the combinations

$$ \Phi=\Phi_0\,e^{\,i\alpha_2\hat{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathbf{x}}, \qquad \Psi=\Psi_0\,e^{-i\alpha_1\hat{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathbf{x}}, $$

satisfy the two Beltrami equations and reconstruct the fields through $(14)$. They are the two circular polarisations, and they carry the two different wavenumbers. An incident linearly polarised wave is their superposition; over a distance $L$ the phases of the two components differ by $(\alpha_2-\alpha_1)L$, and a linear polarisation whose two circular components differ in phase by $\delta$ has its plane rotated by $\delta/2$, so the plane of polarisation has rotated by

$$ \Delta\theta=\tfrac12(\alpha_2-\alpha_1)L=\frac{k^2\beta}{1-k^2\beta^2}\,L \;\xrightarrow{\;\beta\ \text{small}\;}\; k^2\beta\,L, $$

the standard law of optical activity. This is the quantity a measurement of the rotation angle determines. It also fixes the domain of the linearised Drude–Born–Fedorov description: the denominators $1-k^2\beta^2$ show that the description is a low-chirality expansion, valid for $k|\beta|<1$.

The Quaternionic Analysis Behind the Reformulation

Equations $(20)$ are equations of the form $D_{3\pm\alpha}u=0$ with the scalar shift

$$ D_{3\pm\alpha}=D_3\pm\alpha, \qquad \Delta+\alpha^2=-(D_3+\alpha)(D_3-\alpha), $$

the factorisation being the same computation as $\Box=\tilde\nabla\tilde\nabla^{\natural}$ one dimension lower. The family $D_{3\pm\alpha}$ is the classical Helmholtz–Dirac family of Clifford analysis — the splitting, the shifted kernel and the radiation condition are set up in Clifford Analysis, and this article only uses them — and its four standard facts, which the authors use and attribute to Kravchenko and Shapiro [1996], are the following.

The kernel. With $\Theta_\alpha$ the fundamental solution of the Helmholtz operator, $(\Delta+\alpha^2)\Theta_\alpha=\delta$, chosen as

$$ \Theta_\alpha(x)=-\frac{e^{\,i\alpha|x|}}{4\pi|x|} \qquad(\operatorname{Im}\alpha\ge 0), $$

the two distributions

$$ \mathcal K_{\pm\alpha}=-(D_3\mp\alpha)\Theta_\alpha $$

are fundamental solutions of $D_{3\pm\alpha}$, that is $D_{3\pm\alpha}\mathcal K_{\pm\alpha}=\delta$. The choice $\operatorname{Im}\alpha\ge0$ selects the outgoing kernel. The principal symbol of $\Delta+\alpha^2$ is $-\lvert\xi\rvert^2$, non-zero for $\xi\neq0$, so the operator is elliptic and the shift by $\alpha$ is a lower-order perturbation; the Helmholtz kernel replaces the classical spatial Cauchy kernel $-\mathbf{x}/(4\pi\lvert\mathbf{x}\rvert^3)$ of the unshifted $D_3$, which is the three-dimensional analogue of the four-dimensional Cauchy kernel $\bar x/\lvert x\rvert^4$ treated in Quaternion Regular Functions and Biquaternion Regular Functions.

The four theorems. On a bounded domain $\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^3$ with closed piecewise smooth (Liapunov) boundary $\Gamma$, and with $T_\alpha$, $K_\alpha$, $S_\alpha$ the Teodorescu transform, the Cauchy-type operator and the operator of singular integration built from the kernel $\mathcal K_\alpha$,

$$ T_\alpha f(x)=\int_\Omega \mathcal K_\alpha(x-y)f(y)\,d\Omega_y, \qquad K_\alpha f(x)=-\int_\Gamma \mathcal K_\alpha(x-y)\,\vec n(y)\,f(y)\,d\Gamma_y, $$

$$ S_\alpha f(x)=-2\int_\Gamma \mathcal K_\alpha(x-y)\,\vec n(y)\,f(y)\,d\Gamma_y, \qquad P_\alpha=\tfrac12(I+S_\alpha), \qquad Q_\alpha=I-P_\alpha, $$

one has: the Borel–Pompeiu formula $K_{\pm\alpha}f+T_{\pm\alpha}D_{3\pm\alpha}f=f$ in $\Omega$; the Cauchy integral formula $f=K_{\pm\alpha}f$ in $\Omega$ for $f$ in $\ker D_{3\pm\alpha}$; the Plemelj–Sokhotski formulas $\lim_{\Omega\ni x\to y\in\Gamma}K_{\pm\alpha}f(x)=P_{\pm\alpha}f(y)$ and $\lim_{\Omega^c\ni x\to y}K_{\pm\alpha}f(x)=-Q_{\pm\alpha}f(y)$; and the involutiveness $S_{\pm\alpha}^2f=f$. These are the spatial generalisations of the complex $T$-operator, the Cauchy-type operator and the operator of singular integration, and they are the four tools the applications use.

Integral Representations and the Extendability Problem

Integral representations. Applying the Borel–Pompeiu formula to $\Phi$ and $\Psi$ and returning to the fields through $(14)$ gives an integral representation for the pair $(\tilde{\mathbf{E}},\tilde{\mathbf{H}})$ inside $\Omega$ in terms of the boundary values of $\Phi$ and $\Psi$ and the volume sources. In vectors, the vector parts of the resulting formulas are the Stratton–Chu formulas for a chiral medium; the scalar parts are a Gauss-type identity, and are identities when the fields obey $(12)$. Thus the representation reconstructs the fields inside the domain from their values on the boundary — the same role the retarded-potential representation plays in Exercise: The Retarded Potentials and the Green's Function, with the outgoing chiral kernel in place of the retarded one. It is equation $(21)$ of the source. The survey states the structural point plainly: in this form the Stratton–Chu formulas are a Cauchy integral formula for the electromagnetic field, a convolution with the fundamental solution of the Maxwell operator, and the classical vector form only hides that; the biquaternionic representation makes the meaning transparent. That is the survey's justification for the whole programme, and it is the reason the same kernel is then used as a computational basis rather than merely written down.

The extendability problem. The question the authors pose is the boundary-value problem for the chiral system: given two Hölder-continuous vectors $\tilde{\mathbf{e}},\tilde{\mathbf{h}}$ on the boundary $\Gamma$ of a bounded domain $\Omega$, does there exist a pair $(\tilde{\mathbf{E}},\tilde{\mathbf{H}})$ satisfying the chiral Maxwell equations $(12)$ in $\Omega$ and coinciding with $\tilde{\mathbf{e}},\tilde{\mathbf{h}}$ on $\Gamma$? Through $(14)$ the problem splits into the two boundary-value problems

$$ D_{3-\alpha_2}\Phi=0\ \text{in }\Omega,\quad \Phi|_\Gamma=\tilde{\mathbf{e}}+i\tilde{\mathbf{h}}, \qquad D_{3+\alpha_1}\Psi=0\ \text{in }\Omega,\quad \Psi|_\Gamma=\tilde{\mathbf{e}}-i\tilde{\mathbf{h}}, $$

and each of these has the solvability criterion $Q_\alpha v=T_\alpha g$ on $\Gamma$ for the inhomogeneous problem $D_\alpha f=g$, $f|_\Gamma=v$ (the Theorem 6 of the paper). Combining the two criteria through $(14)$ gives the authors' complete solution of the extendability problem: the pair $(\tilde{\mathbf{e}},\tilde{\mathbf{h}})$ extends to a solution of $(12)$ in $\Omega$ if and only if two integral conditions on $\Gamma$ are satisfied, and then the solution is given by the Stratton–Chu representation. The two conditions are exactly the two boundary-value criteria, one for each circular polarisation.

The structural point is in the authors' Remark 11. The vector parts of the two integral conditions are the classical, and well known, necessary condition that $\tilde{\mathbf{e}}$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}$ be boundary values of the corresponding Stratton–Chu integrals; that condition is not sufficient. The extra information that makes it sufficient is carried by the scalar parts of the same conditions. In three-dimensional vector calculus it is not easy to see why scalar information should be needed for a vector boundary-value problem; in quaternionic terms it is immediate, because the quaternionic Cauchy formula is a single formula for a four-component object and the scalar and vector parts are its two halves. This is the article's clearest instance of the framework earning its keep: the reformulation does not merely restate a classical problem, it exhibits the part of the boundary data that the vector form hides.

Unbounded domains. The same result holds for unbounded $\Omega$ provided the fields satisfy the radiation condition $(1\pm i\,\hat{\mathbf{x}})\cdot f(x)=o(1/|x|)$ at infinity, the dot being the quaternionic product by the element $1\pm i\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ of the algebra. In the electromagnetic variables that radiation condition is the Silver–Müller condition, so the extendability theorem for the exterior problem is a theorem about the physically admissible outgoing fields. Those two first-order conditions the primary paper attributes to McIntosh and Mitrea and to Kravchenko and Castillo, and it derives from them, through the decomposition of the Helmholtz null-set, the single condition of the second-order problem: a solution $u$ of the quaternionic Helmholtz equation $\Delta u+\alpha^2u=0$ that is outgoing satisfies

$$ i\alpha\,u(x)+\hat{\mathbf{x}}\cdot Du(x)=o\!\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right), \qquad |x|\to\infty, $$

uniformly in direction. Its scalar part is the classical Sommerfeld condition and its vector part is the vector radiation condition of Colton and Kress, so one quaternionic equality carries both, and the two first-order conditions are recovered from it by asking that $u$ lie in one of the two shifted kernels. This is the radiation condition of the ambient space of the boundary-value problem rather than of its two halves.

The zero divisor inside the radiation condition. Both forms above are built on the element $1\pm i\hat{\mathbf{x}}$, and that element is a zero divisor of the algebra. With $\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{x}/|\mathbf{x}|$ a unit vector one has $\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{2}=-e_0$, hence $(i\hat{\mathbf{x}})^{2}=e_0$ because $i$ is central, and therefore

$$ N(1\pm i\hat{\mathbf{x}})=1+i^{2}=0,\qquad (1+i\hat{\mathbf{x}})(1-i\hat{\mathbf{x}})=0,\qquad (1+i\hat{\mathbf{x}})^{2}=2\,(1+i\hat{\mathbf{x}}). $$

It is a zero divisor of the non-pure kind: the scalar part is $1$, so it is not nilpotent, but $P_{\pm}=\tfrac12(1\pm i\hat{\mathbf{x}})$ are idempotents with $P_{\pm}^{2}=P_{\pm}$, $P_{+}P_{-}=0$ and $P_{+}+P_{-}=e_0$, so the unit directions carry a sphere of complementary idempotent pairs and $1-i\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ is at once a right and a left divisor of $1+i\hat{\mathbf{x}}$. This matters for the condition and not only for the algebra: a condition of the form $k\,f+\hat{\mathbf{x}}fk=o(1/|x|)$ invites the step of dividing out the factor to conclude that $f$ itself decays, and that step is undefined when the factor is a zero divisor, so no decay rate follows from the condition alone. The primary paper records exactly this. For a scalar parameter the condition "can be written without the term $\hat{\mathbf{x}}/|x|^{2}$", which "apparently gives a faster decay", and the author's verdict is that this is not true, "because $1+i\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ is a zero divisor". Where the wave vector is itself isotropic — a zero divisor in $\mathbb{C}^{3}$ — the paper therefore adds an independent decay hypothesis to the condition, requiring $f=o(1/|x|)$ of the field and $u^{s}=o(1)$ of the scattered field in the scattering problem. The algebra the caveat rests on is in Biquaternion Zero Divisors and Zero Divisors as a Physical Locus in Biquaternionic Form; the sphere of idempotents $P_{\pm}$ is the one of Biquaternion Idempotents and Projections.

The authors state the extendability result as complete under the stated hypotheses — a Liapunov boundary and Hölder data. Read as a statement about the classical chiral boundary-value problem, it is the scattering-theoretic question of Athanasiadis, Martin and Stratis [1999], here solved by quaternionic means.

A Numerical Method from the Fundamental Solutions

The kernels assembled above are not only a language for the boundary-value problem; they are a computational basis for it. A survey by Khmelnytskaya and Kravchenko reports a method that solves the exterior chiral scattering problem by collocation on these very kernels, and reports its measured convergence. It is the one place in the corpus's sources where the biquaternionic apparatus is shown computing rather than describing.

The problem. Let $\Gamma$ be the boundary of a perfectly conducting body and $\Omega^-$ the exterior domain. Find fields satisfying the time-harmonic chiral system in $\Omega^-$, the outgoing radiation condition at infinity (the Silver–Müller condition of the previous section), and the boundary condition

$$ \tilde{\mathbf E}(\mathbf x)\times\vec n(\mathbf x)=\mathbf f(\mathbf x), \qquad \mathbf x\in\Gamma , $$

with $\mathbf f$ a prescribed tangential field; this is the source's equation $(14)$. The source writes it for the unnormalised fields of its §3, in which the diagonalising combinations are $\vec\varphi=\mathbf E+i\mathbf H$ and $\vec\psi=\mathbf E-i\mathbf H$ with the same two wavenumbers $\alpha_1,\alpha_2$; the normalised fields $\tilde{\mathbf E},\tilde{\mathbf H}$ of this article are used below, and the two conventions describe the same equations.

The basis. The approximation space is spanned by the fundamental solutions of the two shifted operators, evaluated at source points placed on an auxiliary surface. For the exterior problem the sources lie on a closed surface $\Gamma^-$ enclosed by $\Gamma$, the points $\{y_j^-\}$ being dense on it, and the two families are

$$ \mathcal K_{\alpha_1,j}=\mathcal K_{\alpha_1}(\mathbf x-y_j^-), \qquad \mathcal K_{-\alpha_2,j}=\mathcal K_{-\alpha_2}(\mathbf x-y_j^-), $$

the source's systems $(11)$; for the interior problem the sources lie on a surface $\Gamma^+$ enclosing the scatterer and the same construction uses $(10)$. That a dense set of sources suffices is the content of the source's two completeness theorems (Theorems 5 and 6): for boundary data in $H^s(\Gamma)$, $s>1$, that are extendable into the relevant domain, and provided $\alpha_1^2,\alpha_2^2$ are not Dirichlet eigenvalues of the auxiliary domain, the data are approximated to arbitrary precision in $H^{s-1}(\Gamma)$ by right-linear combinations

$$ \tilde{\mathbf e}_N=\tfrac12\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal K_{\alpha_1,j}\,a_j+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal K_{-\alpha_2,j}\,b_j\Bigr), \qquad \tilde{\mathbf h}_N=\tfrac{1}{2i}\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal K_{\alpha_1,j}\,a_j-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal K_{-\alpha_2,j}\,b_j\Bigr), $$

with $a_j,b_j\in\mathbb B$ constant. The completeness theorem is the extension to the exterior problem of the extendability characterisation of the previous section: the same operators $P_\alpha,Q_\alpha$ that decide whether data extend also decide whether the basis can represent them.

The collocation scheme. Two properties of the basis fix the algebra of the method. First, a $\mathcal K_{\pm\alpha}$ is a full biquaternion, not a vector: it has scalar part $\pm\alpha\,\Theta_\alpha$ and vector part $-\mathrm{grad}\,\Theta_\alpha$. A right-linear combination of them with generic coefficients is therefore not purely vectorial, whereas the fields are; the source's Remark 7 adds the two scalar-part conditions

$$ \mathrm{Sc}\Bigl(\sum_{j}\mathcal K_{\alpha_1,j}\,a_j+\sum_{j}\mathcal K_{-\alpha_2,j}\,b_j\Bigr)=0, \qquad \mathrm{Sc}\Bigl(\sum_{j}\mathcal K_{\alpha_1,j}\,a_j-\sum_{j}\mathcal K_{-\alpha_2,j}\,b_j\Bigr)=0 $$

as two further equations at each collocation point. Second, the unknowns are the $2N$ biquaternions $a_j,b_j$, that is $8N$ complex numbers; each collocation point supplies the two complex tangential components of the boundary condition plus the two scalar conditions, four complex equations in all, so $2N$ points close the system, which is linear in the coefficients. Collocating the boundary condition rather than the field is justified by a stability estimate of the source: the field in any closed interior subset $\Omega^-_r$ of $\Omega^-$ is controlled by the boundary residual,

$$ \|\tilde{\mathbf E}\|_{\infty,\Omega^-_r}+\|\tilde{\mathbf H}\|_{\infty,\Omega^-_r} \le C\,\|\vec n\times\tilde{\mathbf E}\|_{L^2(\Gamma)} . $$

The impedance variant. The survey notes that the same basis serves other boundary-value problems of the Maxwell system, notably the impedance problem, whose boundary condition $\vec E\times\vec n-Z_{\mathrm s}\,[(\vec H\times\vec n)\times\vec n]=\vec f$ (the survey's $Z_{\mathrm s}$ is written $\xi$) replaces the perfect-conductor condition $\tilde{\mathbf E}\times\vec n=\mathbf f$ of the problem above. It changes only the coefficient matrix of the collocation system, not the basis or the counting argument. The bracket is the tangential part of $\vec H$ up to sign, $[(\vec H\times\vec n)\times\vec n]=(\vec H\cdot\vec n)\vec n-\vec H$, and it is tangential to $\Gamma$ for any $\vec H$, so the added term is invisible to the normal component of the condition and $Z_{\mathrm s}=0$ returns the perfect-conductor case; that is why the modification is a coefficient change and nothing else.

The reported test. Why this basis rather than a matrix one. The survey states three advantages of a vector fundamental solution over the usual matrix fundamental solutions of the Maxwell system: it has four components rather than sixteen; it carries the physical meaning of the field of a point source; and its singularity is of lower order. Those three properties are what keep the collocation system small and its conditioning tolerable, and the table above is the evidence the survey offers for the method working.

The completeness, and the classical route it rests on. The survey states the completeness theorems; the primary paper proves them, and the proof shows what the method stands on. It begins from a theorem of Kupradze [1967]: for a closed surface $\Gamma$ of class $C^2$, the system $\{\Theta_\alpha(x-y_n)\}$ of Helmholtz fundamental solutions whose sources are dense on a surface is complete in $L^2(\Gamma)$, and, provided $\alpha^2$ is not a Dirichlet eigenvalue of the enclosed domain, the same holds for the sources on an interior surface. That is the classical foundation of the method of fundamental solutions in the scalar case. The contribution of the primary paper is to replace $L^2(\Gamma)$ by the Sobolev scale $H^s(\Gamma)$ that the boundary-value problem actually uses, in three steps: completeness of the same scalar system in $L^2(\Omega)\cap\ker(\Delta+\alpha^2)$; then in $H^s(\Omega)\cap\ker(\Delta+\alpha^2)$; and then in $H^s(\Gamma)$, the boundary being the trace space of those solutions. The step from the domain to the boundary is carried by the decomposition of the Helmholtz null-set into the two shifted kernels — the proposition recorded in Biquaternion Regular Functions, there written with the opposite sign convention — together with two operators, the extension $\Lambda$ of the Dirichlet problem and the trace $\gamma$. The composite boundary operators $\tilde Q_{\pm\alpha}=Q_{\pm\alpha}\gamma\Pi_{\pm\alpha}\Lambda$ satisfy $u=\tilde Q_\alpha u+\tilde Q_{-\alpha}u$ for every $u\in H^s(\Gamma)$ with $s>1$, and coincide with $Q_{\pm\alpha}$ on the range of $Q_{\pm\alpha}$, so a boundary function already in $\operatorname{im}Q_\alpha(H^s(\Gamma))$ is represented by the $\alpha$-hyperholomorphic kernels alone and the $-\alpha$ part drops out; the completeness of the systems $\{\mathcal K_{3\pm\alpha}(x-y_n)\}$ in $\operatorname{im}Q_{\pm\alpha}(H^s(\Gamma))$, in the norm of $H^{s-1}(\Gamma)$, follows from the completeness of the scalar system. The lift from the scalar system to the quaternionic kernels uses nothing beyond the decomposition: the approximants are $\frac{1}{2\alpha}\sum_n\mathcal K_{3\alpha,n}a_n$, so the complete system of "quaternionic fundamental solutions" is the system of shifted Cauchy kernels evaluated at the source points. The kernels of the integral representations and the basis of the numerical method are the same functions, and the method is the integral theory used at the points where the kernel is already known.

The obstruction the primary paper names. For the unshifted case $\alpha=0$ the analogous completeness is not new: it is in Gürlebeck and Sprößig [1997, p. 284], for harmonic functions. What the primary paper records about it is a statement about the algebra rather than about the method, and it is the remark of the paper most worth carrying. That classical proof does not transfer to complex-quaternion-valued functions, because it requires an $L^2$ space "which would correspond to the complex quaternionic multiplication" — an inner product on the algebra under which the multiplication is well behaved — and there is none, the algebra's own norm $N(a)=\sum_\mu a_\mu^2$ being isotropic. The remedy the paper takes is to run the $L^2$ argument where an inner product does exist, on the scalar Helmholtz operator with its ordinary $L^2(\Omega;\mathbb C)$ pairing, and to bring the quaternionic multiplication in afterwards through the algebraic decomposition, so that it never has to be positive definite. The corpus's account of the absent positivity is in Biquaternion Zero Divisors and Zero Divisors as a Physical Locus in Biquaternionic Form; the remark is the first concrete cost of it recorded in this literature — a classical proof that works for real harmonic functions and fails for complex-quaternionic ones for want of an inner product, not for want of an argument.

Electromagnetic Energy Balance in a Chiral Medium

The quaternionic Stokes theorem, Theorem 1 of the paper's preliminaries, gives the energy balance. It is a corollary, not a separate computation: the balance equation follows from the Stokes identity applied to the pair $\tilde{\mathbf{E}},\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^*$, using the two differential identities that the chiral system implies,

$$ D_3\tilde{\mathbf{E}}=-\tilde\rho+\frac{k^2\beta\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}+ik\,\tilde{\mathbf{H}}+ik\beta\,\tilde{\mathbf{j}}}{1-k^2\beta^2}, \qquad D_3\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^*=\frac{ik^*\tilde{\mathbf{E}}^*+k^{*2}\beta^*\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^*+\tilde{\mathbf{j}}^*}{1-k^{*2}\beta^{*2}}, \tag{26,27} $$

which are the chiral generalisations of the ordinary relations $D_3\mathbf{E}=\mathrm{rot}\,\mathbf{E}$, $D_3\mathbf{H}=\mathrm{rot}\,\mathbf{H}$ of the sourceless case, the scalar term $-\tilde\rho$ of the first identity being the paper's symbol for $-\mathrm{div}\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$ as fixed above. The scalar part of the Stokes identity is the energy balance

$$ -\int_\Gamma\bigl\langle[\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{*}],\vec n\bigr\rangle\,d\Gamma =\int_\Omega\frac{ik^*}{1-k^{*2}\beta^{*2}}\bigl\langle\tilde{\mathbf{E}},\tilde{\mathbf{E}}^{*}\bigr\rangle\,dx -\int_\Omega\frac{ik}{1-k^2\beta^2}\bigl\langle\tilde{\mathbf{H}},\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{*}\bigr\rangle\,dx +\int_\Omega\frac{1}{1-k^{*2}\beta^{*2}}\bigl\langle\tilde{\mathbf{E}},\tilde{\mathbf{j}}^{*}\bigr\rangle\,dx -\int_\Omega\frac{ik\beta}{1-k^2\beta^2}\bigl\langle\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{*},\tilde{\mathbf{j}}\bigr\rangle\,dx . \tag{24} $$

The left-hand side is the flux of the complex Poynting vector through the boundary, written in the quaternionic pairing $\langle[\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{*}],\vec n\rangle$; the right-hand side is the stored and dissipated energy of the two circular polarisations, weighted by the chiral denominators $1-k^2\beta^2$ and its conjugate $1-k^{*2}\beta^{*2}$. Those weights are the effect of the coupling: the two polarisations do not enter the balance symmetrically. The vector part of the same Stokes identity is a further identity of the field, equation $(25)$ of the source, a vector equality the authors call useful in the same way. The two differential identities $(26,27)$ are the load-bearing step, and they are what makes the balance a corollary of Stokes rather than an independent derivation.

Inhomogeneous and Stratified Chiral Media

The last application relaxes the homogeneity. Now all three medium characteristics are continuously differentiable functions of position, $\epsilon=\epsilon(\mathbf{x})$, $\mu=\mu(\mathbf{x})$, $\beta=\beta(\mathbf{x})$, and the chiral system becomes

$$ \mathrm{rot}\,\tilde{\mathbf{H}}=-i\omega\epsilon(\mathbf{x})\bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{E}}+\beta(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{rot}\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\bigr)+\tilde{\mathbf{j}}, \qquad \mathrm{rot}\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}=i\omega\mu(\mathbf{x})\bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}+\beta(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{rot}\,\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\bigr), \tag{29} $$

with the normalisation now applied locally, $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}=\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\mathbf{E}$, $\tilde{\mathbf{H}}=\sqrt{\mu}\,\mathbf{H}$ — the source's §7 normalisation, which differs from the §4 normalisation used above by the common factor $\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}=1/c$, so the two sections' tildes mean different things. The reformulation of the preceding sections does not survive unchanged, because the medium parameters no longer commute with the derivatives; but there is a regime in which it does.

Slowly changing media. Put $\tilde\epsilon=\mathrm{grad}\sqrt{\epsilon}/\sqrt{\epsilon}$ and $\tilde\mu=\mathrm{grad}\sqrt{\mu}/\sqrt{\mu}$; these are the dimensionless vectors of §7, and $\tilde\epsilon/k$, $\tilde\mu/k$ are dimensionless too. In equation $(31)$ of the source the medium variation enters only through the multiplication operators $M_{\tilde\epsilon/k}$ and $M_{\tilde\mu/k}$. If $|\tilde\epsilon|,|\tilde\mu|\ll|k|$ those two terms are negligible, and the diagonalisation survives: the two combinations

$$ \tilde\Phi=\tilde{\mathbf{E}}+i\tilde{\mathbf{H}}, \qquad \tilde\Psi=\tilde{\mathbf{E}}-i\tilde{\mathbf{H}} \tag{30} $$

satisfy, to leading order,

$$ \bigl(D_3-\alpha_2\bigr)\tilde\Phi=0, \qquad \bigl(D_3+\alpha_1\bigr)\tilde\Psi=0, \tag{32} $$

the same pair as the homogeneous problem $(20)$. The slowly changing chiral medium is therefore solvable by the same equations and the same kernel as the homogeneous one; only the normalisation of the fields has followed the medium. This is the geometric-optics regime of the medium, and the point of contact with the WKB material of the series.

The device for variable coefficients. The paper solves the variable-coefficient equation $(D_3+\alpha(\mathbf{x}))u=0$, with $\alpha$ a scalar function, by a zero-divisor device. Let $\varphi$ be a solution of the eikonal equation $(\nabla\varphi)^2=\alpha^2$, and put $\eta=e^{\varphi}$. Then

$$ D_3+\alpha=\eta\bigl(D_3+Q_+\bigr)\eta^{-1}, \qquad Q_\pm=\alpha\pm\nabla\varphi, \qquad Q_+Q_-=0, $$

the last being the zero-divisor identity $\alpha^2-(\nabla\varphi)^2=0$ read in the algebra. Because $Q_+$ is a zero divisor, the shifted operator admits a factorisation, and solutions are built from $\eta$ and functions in the kernel of $D_3$. For a stratified medium, $\alpha=\alpha(x_1)$ depending on one coordinate only, the two solutions of the eikonal equation are $\varphi=\pm i\Theta$ with $\Theta'=\alpha$, and the corresponding solutions of $(D_3+\alpha(x_1))u=0$ are

$$ u_1(x)=e^{\,i\Theta(x_1)}\Bigl[H_1(x_2,x_3)(1-ie_1)A_1+H_2(x_2,x_3)e_2(1+ie_1)A_2\Bigr], \tag{43} $$

$$ u_2(x)=e^{-i\Theta(x_1)}\Bigl[G_1(x_2,x_3)(1+ie_1)B_1+G_2(x_2,x_3)e_2(1-ie_1)B_2\Bigr], \tag{44} $$

where $H_1,H_2,G_1,G_2$ are analytic functions of the complex variable $z=x_3+e_1x_2$ — that is, their parts satisfy the Cauchy–Riemann conditions $\partial_2h_0+\partial_3h_1=0$, $\partial_3h_0-\partial_2h_1=0$ — and $A_i,B_i$ are arbitrary constant biquaternions. The exponential $e^{i\Theta}$ carries the phase accumulated along the stratification, and the analytic functions carry the transverse structure. Feeding them into the reconstruction $(14)$ gives explicit solutions of the chiral system for a stratified chiral medium; the case of general slowly changing $\epsilon,\mu,\beta$ is approximated by this one. The shift carried by each exponential is fixed below.

A sign bookkeeping point. Proposition 14 records that $(43,44)$ solve $(D_3+\alpha(x_1))u=0$ with $\Theta$ an antiderivative of $\alpha$. The source then states the reduced system as $(32)$ and gives the solutions of it as $(46,47)$, with $\Theta_1,\Theta_2$ antiderivatives of $\alpha_1,\alpha_2$ respectively (equation $(45)$; the field formula following Proposition 15 uses the exponent $e^{\,i\int\alpha_2}$). Recomputation on the printed forms shows that the two statements are related by one global sign: the printed $(46,47)$ with $\Theta_2'=\alpha_2$, $\Theta_1'=\alpha_1$ satisfy $(D_3+\alpha_2)\tilde\Phi=0$ and $(D_3-\alpha_1)\tilde\Psi=0$, whereas $(32)$ reads $(D_3-\alpha_2)\tilde\Phi=0$ and $(D_3+\alpha_1)\tilde\Psi=0$. The two agree if $\Theta_1,\Theta_2$ are read as antiderivatives of $-\alpha_1,-\alpha_2$, equivalently if the signs of the two exponentials are exchanged. The verified assignment, valid for a constant or a variable $\alpha(x_1)$, is the one recorded here: the exponential $e^{\,i\Theta}$ on the first term accompanies the operator $D_3+\alpha$ whose shift is the derivative of $\Theta$. The pairing rule behind that assignment is the pair of eigenvalue identities

$$ e_1(1-ie_1)=+i(1-ie_1), \qquad e_1(1+ie_1)=-i(1+ie_1), $$

which fix, for each idempotent factor, whether $e^{\,i\Theta}$ or $e^{-i\Theta}$ belongs to the kernel of $D_3+\Theta'$. The discrepancy is a sign convention in one line of the source and does not affect the existence or the form of the solutions.

The one equation behind six systems. The equations of this section are instances of a single first-order problem, and it is worth recording the list, because the systems otherwise live in four different parts of the menu. Each of the following reduces to

$$ D_3f+f\vec\alpha = 0, \qquad D_3 = e_1\partial_1+e_2\partial_2+e_3\partial_3, \qquad \vec\alpha = \alpha_1e_1+\alpha_2e_2+\alpha_3e_3 , \tag{45} $$

with the coefficient on the right: the Dirac equation with a scalar potential, with an electric potential, and with a pseudoscalar potential; the force-free magnetic fields of magnetohydrodynamics, $\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{B} = 0$ and $\mathrm{rot}\,\mathbf{B} = \nu\mathbf{B}$ with a non-constant scalar factor $\nu$, which is $(D_3-\nu)\mathbf{B} = 0$ and hence a Beltrami field — the same form as the Beltrami pair $(20)$ above, with $\nu$ in the place of the chiral wavenumber; the Maxwell system of a slowly changing medium, that is the pair $(32)$; and the static Maxwell system. The Dirac members carry the potentials and the dictionary in The Dirac Equation in Biquaternionic Form; the force-free member is the magnetohydrodynamic reading of the equations of this section, and the slowly changing member is $(32)$ itself. The source of the list is Kravchenko and Kravchenko (2003), who also observe that of the six, only the static Maxwell member forces $\vec\alpha$ to be a gradient — for it the permittivity must factorise, $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_1(x_1)\varepsilon_2(x_2)\varepsilon_3(x_3)$ with $\alpha_k = \partial_k\varepsilon_k/(2\varepsilon_k)$ — while in the other five the coefficient is a general vector-valued function. That distinction is what the corpus's Riccati material covers, and what it does not: the equation with a vectorial coefficient is reduced to four scalar Schrödinger equations, not one, and the reduction, its four potentials and its exact Jacobian condition are in Quaternion Regular Functions.

What Is Not Claimed

The reformulation and the diagonalisation $(18)$–$(20)$ are recomputed and exact. The energy balance $(24)$ is the authors' identity; the two differential identities $(26,27)$ on which it rests are recomputed and exact, and the boundary-integral identity itself is recorded as attributed rather than re-derived. The extendability theorem is recorded as the authors' result under its stated hypotheses — a Liapunov boundary and Hölder data — and the reading of the two integral conditions as the classical Stratton–Chu condition plus a quaternionic scalar completion is the authors' Remark 11. The numerical method of the section A Numerical Method from the Fundamental Solutions is recorded from the primary paper, together with its completeness theorems, its collocation scheme and the classical Kupradze theorem they rest on: the completeness proof is summarised here and not reproduced, and the two convergence tables are quoted as their own reported data — the survey's ellipsoid run and the primary paper's sphere run. Neither computation is repeated.

Two restrictions bound the scope. The chirality parameter is taken real and the medium lossless; the absorbing case, in which $\beta$ is complex and the two circular polarisations are absorbed differently — circular dichroism — is not treated, in parallel with the absorbing case declined in Electromagnetism in Media. And the explicit solutions $(43,44)$ are one-dimensional in the medium variation: $\alpha$ depends on the single coordinate $x_1$, so the construction describes a stratified rather than a fully inhomogeneous medium, the general slowly changing case being reached only to leading order.

Summary

A chiral medium is an isotropic medium with a magnetoelectric response, described by the Drude–Born–Fedorov relations with the third parameter $\beta$. Its time-harmonic Maxwell system is diagonalised by the two combinations $\Phi=\tilde{\mathbf{E}}+i\tilde{\mathbf{H}}$ and $\Psi=\tilde{\mathbf{E}}-i\tilde{\mathbf{H}}$, each of which satisfies a first-order equation of Beltrami type with one of the two chiral wavenumbers

$$ \alpha_1=\frac{k}{1+k\beta}, \qquad \alpha_2=\frac{k}{1-k\beta}, \qquad \alpha_1\alpha_2=\frac{k^2}{1-k^2\beta^2}, $$

reducing to the ordinary wavenumber $k$ when $\beta=0$. The two wavenumbers are the two circular polarisations; their difference $2k^2\beta/(1-k^2\beta^2)$ is the optical activity, and the rotation of the plane of polarisation is half of it per unit length. The combination $\Phi$ is, up to $-ic$, the field-strength biquaternion $\tilde F$ of the rest of the series, so the diagonalisation is by a complex structure already present in the algebra.

Because the diagonal equations are of the Helmholtz–Dirac type $D_{3\pm\alpha}u=0$, they carry the whole spatial function theory — the Helmholtz kernel $\mathcal K_{\pm\alpha}=-(D_3\mp\alpha)\Theta_\alpha$, the Borel–Pompeiu formula, the Cauchy integral formula and the Plemelj–Sokhotski formulas — and the authors use it for two results: an integral representation of the fields of Stratton–Chu type, and a complete solution of the extendability problem, in which the scalar parts of the integral conditions supply the information that the classical vector condition lacks. The energy balance follows as a corollary of the quaternionic Stokes theorem, with the chiral weights $1-k^2\beta^2$ and its conjugate, and the two differential identities $(26,27)$ as its load-bearing step. For an inhomogeneous but slowly changing chiral medium the same diagonalisation survives to leading order, and the zero-divisor device solves the variable-coefficient equation explicitly for a stratified medium, in terms of exponentials of the accumulated phase and analytic transverse functions.

The same kernel is a computational basis and not only a language. A collocation method solves the exterior chiral scattering problem with right-linear combinations of the fundamental solutions of $D_{3\pm\alpha}$, using the completeness of those systems and the extendability characterisation as its foundation, and imposing the scalar parts of the trial functions to keep them purely vectorial. The same basis carries over to other boundary-value problems of the system, the impedance condition among them, with a change only in the coefficient matrix of the collocation system. The completeness is the primary paper's theorem, and it rests on Kupradze's classical result that the Helmholtz fundamental solutions with sources dense on a surface are complete in $L^2(\Gamma)$: the primary paper's contribution is to lift that to the Sobolev scale $H^s(\Gamma)$ used by the boundary-value problem, the algebraic splitting of the Helmholtz null-set into the two shifted kernels carrying the passage from the scalar system to the quaternionic one, so that the complete system of fundamental solutions is the system of shifted Cauchy kernels evaluated at the source points. Two tests are on record: an ellipsoid, whose reported errors for a magnetic dipole fall from $0.441\times10^{-3}$ at ten sources to $0.522\times10^{-7}$ at thirty-five, and a unit sphere, whose errors fall from $0.802\times10^{-2}$ at three sources to $0.278\times10^{-6}$ at thirty-five but are not monotone at twenty-five. The figures are recorded, not reproduced.

The two things a reader should carry away are the two wavenumbers and the fact that the third parameter $\beta$ is the one the framework's complex structure was already prepared to absorb: the same split of the field into two complex halves that in vacuum separates the electric and magnetic parts separates here the two circular polarisations.
