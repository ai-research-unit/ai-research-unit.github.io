The Data Boundary: Which Articles May Print a Measured Strength, and Why

Introduction

The rule. This category keeps one coupling statement, and it is in The One Coupling That Returns the Standard Model. The rule that follows is easy to state and hard to apply: no article outside that one may carry a value, a boundary condition, a running, a matching, or a ratio called exact.

The difficulty. A third of the category is Standard Model physics transcribed, and transcribed physics arrives with numbers. A rule that forbade those numbers would forbid the corpus's own subject matter. So the rule needs a boundary, and a boundary written only in prose cannot be checked.

This article states that boundary. It carries no strength value of its own: a page that quoted the numbers it classifies would be the first entry in its own table.

It defines the three categories by a test, lists the two articles that make a claim, lists the sixteen that print a measured strength with the quantity each prints, and states what the boundary does not license. A permission without its abuses spelled out is a loophole.

The Three Categories, Defined by One Test

The test is a single question: does the sentence survive the deletion of every strength value in it?

category test what it may print where
structural survives symbols, generators, multiplicities, charges, anomaly identities everywhere
claim does not survive the value, the boundary condition, the running, the matching the coupling section only
data survives only because a measurement supplies the value the measured number, marked as measured, with its source the articles whose subject is the comparison

The middle row. A structural statement is checked by the algebra — a trace identity catches an error in it. A claim is checked by nothing in the algebra: it is imposed, so an error in it is invisible until an experiment, and it moves when the scale moves. That is why it is kept in one place.

The third row. A data article does not assert that the model produces the number. It asserts that the world contains the number, and then asks what follows from it. The two-route disagreement in the electroweak sector is the cleanest example: two routes to the boson masses, one from the measured strengths and one from the measured masses, and they differ at tree level. That difference is a fact about the world, not a claim about this model, and refusing to print it would hide the most informative number in the sector.

The category holds one hundred and fifty-two articles: two claims, sixteen data, and the remaining one hundred and thirty-four structural. They state a group, a carrier, a multiplet list, a charge, an anomaly identity, a boson count, a dimension budget or a diagram, with every strength a symbol and no value attached. They are not listed here because the list would be the category.

The Five Tests a New Article Must Pass

An article may print a strength value only if all five hold. If one fails, the value belongs in the coupling article instead.

  1. The subject is the measurement. The article's argument is about what the world contains, not about what the algebra produces. A value printed in an article whose subject is the algebra is a claim in disguise.
  2. The value is marked as measured, with a scheme and a scale, or with the statement that neither applies.
  3. Nothing in the article predicts it. If the article's own machinery produces the number, the number is a claim and the article is in the wrong category.
  4. The value is used, not displayed. A value that enters a comparison is data; a value that decorates a paragraph is a leak.
  5. Its removal would break the article's argument. This is the test that decides. If the argument survives without the number, the number goes.

What the Boundary Does Not License

Four abuses are worth naming.

First, relabelling. A measured value may not be used as a prediction by relabelling. Writing a measured angle in an article about the model's own sector, and calling it "a standard input", does not make it data. The fourth test decides: does the value enter a comparison, or does it settle a question the article could not otherwise settle? In the second case it is a claim, and it is in the wrong place.

Second, the smuggled equality. An equality may not be smuggled in as a definition. The relation between the electric charge, the weak strength and the angle is a definition, and it carries no scale: the angle is the ratio. A definition of that kind is structural and may stand anywhere. A numerical value for it is data. An assertion that the model produces the value is a claim. The three are different sentences that look alike.

Third, the computed coefficient. It is not data. The one-loop coefficients of the strong and electroweak groups are computed from colour and isospin data, and they may be stated anywhere, with their values, because no measurement enters them. The gluon article and the framework's QCD article quote a coefficient and stay structural: their coefficient is a count of diagrams.

Fourth, the model's own sector. The boundary may not be widened to cover it. "The Standard Model is data" is true. "The fourth colour's couplings are the Standard Model's" is not, and it is the smuggling route the coupling rule closes. The two sectors share a low-energy limit; they do not share a coupling statement.

Summary

Two articles claim, sixteen print measured strengths as data, and the rest carry symbols.

The test is one question, and it is mechanical: does the sentence survive the deletion of every value in it? If yes it is structural, if no it is a claim, and if only a measurement makes it true it is data.

The permission has four edges drawn, and the sharpest is the fourth: the low-energy limit is shared with the Standard Model; a coupling statement is not.
