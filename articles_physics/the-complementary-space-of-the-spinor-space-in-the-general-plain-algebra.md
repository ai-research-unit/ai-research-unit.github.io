# __The Complementary Space of the Spinor Space in the General Plain Algebra__

## Introduction

The companion article *The Spinor Space as a Module of the General Plain Algebra* reads the first minimal left ideal $S=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_1$ of the biquaternion algebra as a module of the plain product. This article reads the **other half of the algebra**. The left regular module splits,

$$
\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_1\oplus\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2=S\oplus\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2,
$$

and the second summand, the minimal left ideal generated by $\tilde\Pi_2=\tfrac12(e_0-ie_3)$, is the **complementary space** of the spinor space. It is the subject of this article.

The complement is a left module of the general plain algebra (GPA), isomorphic to $S$, reached from $S$ by a genuine module isomorphism — right multiplication by the nilpotent $\tilde\Upsilon_1$ — and by the coefficient conjugation $\bar{\cdot}$, which is an anti-linear real structure of the ring. The complement's **physical interpretation** is the GPA interpretation: it is the second copy of the state space on which a **right** multiplication can act, and the corpus's chirality-off-diagonal mass is exactly such a right multiplication. What the complement is *not* is equally important here. It is not the orthogonal complement of $S$ for the algebra's own form $B$: the module split and the $B$-orthogonal split are different decompositions of $\mathbb{B}$, and that failure is the sharpest difference between the GPA interpretation and the Hilbert interpretation of the same subspace. The Hilbert complement of $S$ is the subject of the sibling article *The Complementary Space of the Spinor Space in the General Plain Sesqualgebra*, where the two splits do coincide.

The conventions are those of the companion article. The algebra is $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$, with basis $e_0=1,e_1,e_2,e_3$, $e_k^2=-e_0$ and $e_1e_2=e_3$, and central scalar imaginary $i$; the conjugations are $\tilde Q^{\natural}$, $\tilde Q^{*}$ and $\bar{\tilde Q}$, and $\operatorname{Sc}$ is the scalar part. The plain product is written by juxtaposition; the symbol $\star$ belongs to the sibling article.

## The Complement as a Left Ideal

### The Idempotent and Its Complement

With

$$
\tilde\Pi_1=\tfrac12(e_0+ie_3),\qquad \tilde\Pi_2=\tfrac12(e_0-ie_3),
$$

the two idempotents are orthogonal and complete in the plain product,

$$
\tilde\Pi_1^2=\tilde\Pi_1,\qquad \tilde\Pi_2^2=\tilde\Pi_2,\qquad \tilde\Pi_1\tilde\Pi_2=\tilde\Pi_2\tilde\Pi_1=0,\qquad \tilde\Pi_1+\tilde\Pi_2=e_0 .
$$

The relation $\tilde\Pi_2=e_0-\tilde\Pi_1$ is the algebraic form of the word *complementary*: the second idempotent is the complement of the first in the algebra's own unit. Both are primitive, each generates a minimal left ideal, and the two resolve the identity (*The Biquaternion Vacuum as a Minimal Idempotent*).

### The Two Summands

The complementary space is the second ideal,

$$
\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_2,\tilde\Upsilon_1\},\qquad \tilde\Upsilon_1=\tfrac12(ie_1-e_2),
$$

of complex dimension two, generated by $\tilde\Pi_2$ and closed under left multiplication. The two ideals are the two columns of the matrix model $\mathsf{M}_2$ with $\mathsf{M}_2(e_k)=-i\sigma_k$, in which $\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Upsilon_2,\tilde\Upsilon_1,\tilde\Pi_2$ are the matrix units $E_{11},E_{21},E_{12},E_{22}$:

$$
S=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_1\leftrightarrow\text{first column } \mathbb{C}\{E_{11},E_{21}\},\qquad
\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2\leftrightarrow\text{second column } \mathbb{C}\{E_{12},E_{22}\}.
$$

Each column is one **diagonal** Peirce corner plus one **off-diagonal** corner, so each ideal's basis holds exactly one idempotent and one nilpotent; the complement's basis $(\tilde\Pi_2,\tilde\Upsilon_1)$ is the mirror case of the spinor space's $(\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Upsilon_2)$, and the reason is §*Why the Basis Is One Idempotent and One Nilpotent* of the companion article. The multiplication is the table of the companion article.

### The Left Action on the Complement

On the complement's own basis $(\tilde\Pi_2,\tilde\Upsilon_1)$ the left multiplications by the four basis elements are

$$
L_{\tilde\Pi_1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
L_{\tilde\Pi_2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
L_{\tilde\Upsilon_1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
L_{\tilde\Upsilon_2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},
$$

so the complement is closed under every left multiplication, as a left ideal must be. The pattern is the mirror of the spinor space: on the basis $(\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Upsilon_2)$ of $S$ the matrices are $\operatorname{diag}(1,0),\operatorname{diag}(0,1),E_{12},E_{21}$, so the left-action matrices on the complement are those on $S$ under the interchange of generators $\tilde\Pi_1\leftrightarrow\tilde\Pi_2$ and $\tilde\Upsilon_1\leftrightarrow\tilde\Upsilon_2$. That relabelling is a statement about the two actions and not a map between the modules; the module isomorphism between them is given below.

## The Module Complement

### The Peirce Summand

The module complement of $S$ is immediate. The Peirce decomposition of $\mathbb{B}$ with respect to the pair $(\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Pi_2)$ cuts the algebra into four one-dimensional complex corners,

$$
\tilde\Pi_1\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_1=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_1,\qquad
\tilde\Pi_1\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Upsilon_1,\qquad
\tilde\Pi_2\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_1=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Upsilon_2,\qquad
\tilde\Pi_2\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_2,
$$

and produces the direct sum of left modules

$$
\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_1\oplus\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2=S\oplus\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2 .
$$

The complement of $S$ in this sense is $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2$, the only proper direct summand. It is minimal, simple, and isomorphic to $S$; by the uniqueness of the simple module of $\mathbb{B}$ there is no other left module for it to be.

### The Isomorphism Is Right Multiplication, Not a Relabelling

The module isomorphism $S\to\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2$ is realised concretely by **right multiplication by the nilpotent** $\tilde\Upsilon_1$:

$$
\tilde\Pi_1\tilde\Upsilon_1=\tilde\Upsilon_1,\qquad \tilde\Upsilon_2\tilde\Upsilon_1=\tilde\Pi_2,
$$

so

$$
S\,\tilde\Upsilon_1=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Upsilon_1,\tilde\Pi_2\}=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2 .
$$

Because it is a **right** multiplication it commutes with the left action, $\tilde Q(x\tilde\Upsilon_1)=(\tilde Qx)\tilde\Upsilon_1$, so it is a $\mathbb{B}$-linear map $S\to\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2$; being nonzero between simple modules of the same dimension it is an isomorphism. Symmetrically $\tilde\Upsilon_2$ carries the complement back to $S$: $\tilde\Pi_2\tilde\Upsilon_2=\tilde\Upsilon_2$ and $\tilde\Upsilon_1\tilde\Upsilon_2=\tilde\Pi_1$.

A caution belongs here. The naive "basis swap" $\tilde\Pi_1\leftrightarrow\tilde\Pi_2$, $\tilde\Upsilon_2\leftrightarrow\tilde\Upsilon_1$ is **not** a module map, because it does not commute with the left action: $\tilde\Pi_2\tilde\Pi_1=0$ while $\tilde\Pi_2\tilde\Pi_2=\tilde\Pi_2$. The module isomorphism is right multiplication by a nilpotent, which is linear over the algebra on the left and mixes the two idempotents.

## The Algebra's Bilinear Form Does Not See the Split

### The Form Is Not Orthogonal to the Module Split

The companion article showed that the algebra's own symmetric form

$$
B(\tilde P,\tilde Q)=\operatorname{Sc}(\tilde P\tilde Q)
$$

is degenerate on the carrier, $B_S=\operatorname{diag}(\tfrac12,0)$ on $(\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Upsilon_2)$, with radical $\mathbb{C}\tilde\Upsilon_2$. The same degeneracy makes the module split non-orthogonal. The two nilpotents are not $B$-orthogonal to each other:

$$
B(\tilde\Upsilon_2,\tilde\Upsilon_1)=\operatorname{Sc}(\tilde\Upsilon_2\tilde\Upsilon_1)=\operatorname{Sc}(\tilde\Pi_2)=\tfrac12,
$$

so an element of the complement, $\tilde\Upsilon_1$, pairs with an element of the carrier, $\tilde\Upsilon_2$, and the module decomposition $\mathbb{B}=S\oplus\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2$ is **not** an orthogonal decomposition for $B$.

### The $B$-Orthogonal Complement Is a Different Subspace

The $B$-orthogonal complement of $S$ is defined by

$$
S^{\perp_B}=\{v\in\mathbb{B}:B(u,v)=0\ \text{for all}\ u\in S\}.
$$

It is computed on the four matrix units. The conditions $B(\tilde\Pi_1,v)=0$ and $B(\tilde\Upsilon_2,v)=0$ read, on the coordinates $v=v_1\tilde\Pi_1+v_2\tilde\Upsilon_2+v_3\tilde\Upsilon_1+v_4\tilde\Pi_2$, as $v_1=0$ and $v_3=0$, so

$$
S^{\perp_B}=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Upsilon_2,\tilde\Pi_2\},
$$

of complex dimension two. It is **not** the module complement $\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_2,\tilde\Upsilon_1\}$: the two subspaces share the idempotent $\tilde\Pi_2$ and differ in their nilpotent, $\tilde\Upsilon_2$ against $\tilde\Upsilon_1$. Equivalently, $S\cap S^{\perp_B}=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Upsilon_2=\operatorname{rad}B_S$, the radical of the restricted form, so the sum $S+S^{\perp_B}$ is only three-dimensional:

$$
S+S^{\perp_B}=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Upsilon_2,\tilde\Pi_2\}.
$$

The two decompositions of $\mathbb{B}$ — the module decomposition and the $B$-orthogonal decomposition — therefore differ, and the difference is exactly the nilpotent direction that the algebra's form cannot see. The module complement and the $B$-orthogonal complement of the spinor space are different subspaces; the module complement is the one that is a module.

Symmetrically the complement $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2$ has its own degenerate restriction $B_{\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2}=\operatorname{diag}(\tfrac12,0)$ on $(\tilde\Pi_2,\tilde\Upsilon_1)$, with radical $\mathbb{C}\tilde\Upsilon_1$, so each of the two columns is a degenerate two-dimensional subspace of the four-dimensional form.

## What the Complement Is Not

### Not the Dual

The complement is a **left** ideal. The dual of $S$ — the space of the bras — is the **right** ideal reached by the Hermitian conjugation ${}^{*}$:

$$
\tilde\Pi_1^{*}=\tilde\Pi_1,\qquad \tilde\Upsilon_2^{*}=\tilde\Upsilon_1,
\qquad
S^{*}=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Upsilon_1\}=\tilde\Pi_1\mathbb{B},
$$

the first **row** of the matrix model, $\mathbb{C}\{E_{11},E_{12}\}$. The dual of the spinor space is therefore not its complement, and the two operations — the Hermitian conjugation ${}^{*}$, which reverses the arrows and produces the dual, and the coefficient conjugation $\bar{\cdot}$, which produces the complement — act differently on the same space. This is the algebraic form of the standard caution that a bra is not an antiparticle. Because ${}^{*}$ is the Hermitian conjugation of the general plain sesqualgebra, the full dual-versus-conjugate discussion belongs to the sibling article; here it is stated only to separate the two subspaces, and the complement is reached not by ${}^{*}$ but by the maps of the next section.

### Reached by the Coefficient Conjugation

The map that carries $S$ onto the complement is the **coefficient conjugation** $\bar{\cdot}$:

$$
\overline{\tilde\Pi_1}=\tilde\Pi_2,\qquad \overline{\tilde\Upsilon_2}=-\tilde\Upsilon_1,
\qquad
\bar S=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_2,\tilde\Upsilon_1\}=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2 .
$$

The conjugation fixes the four basis elements $e_\mu$ and negates the scalar imaginary $i$; it is an anti-linear involution of $\mathbb{B}$ and a real structure in the sense that it is not complex-linear. It exchanges the two idempotents and the two ideals, so it is the ring-level operation that the word *complementary* names. It is not an operation of the plain product, and it is not the metric's conjugation either; it is a real structure of the algebra, and the complement is naturally read as a **conjugate** sector.

## The Doubling and the Vacuum

### The Second Copy of the State Space

The complement is isomorphic to $S$ as a left module and is a different subspace of $\mathbb{B}$. The algebra therefore offers **two copies of the same state space**, $\mathbb{B}=S\oplus\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2$, and an operator acting on the right can relate them. Both copies carry the same defining representation of the double cover, so the doubling is a copy and not a second representation: at the module level the two chiral halves are $S$ and its conjugate module $\bar S$, which are non-isomorphic, whereas the two ideals are isomorphic. Where the corpus calls the two ideals the two chiralities — the spectral-triple and chirality-off-diagonal-mass conventions — it is using a second, different convention for the word (*The Spectral Triple of the Biquaternion Algebra*, *Bosonization in Biquaternionic Form*). The two uses coexist; a reader should check which is meant.

### The Other Vacuum

The choice of idempotent selects the module, and it is a choice: the vacuum idempotent $\tilde\Pi_1$ generates $S$, and the complementary idempotent $\tilde\Pi_2$ generates $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2$ (*The Biquaternion Vacuum as a Minimal Idempotent*). The module is unique but the vacuum is not, so the complement is what the other choice of vacuum produces.

### The Sectors Cut Across the Two Ideals

The complement is not a sector. Each of the two ideals meets each of the sectors $\mathbb{M}_\pm$ in exactly one real dimension:

$$
S\cap\mathbb{M}_+=\mathbb{R}\{\tilde\Pi_1\},\qquad S\cap\mathbb{M}_-=\mathbb{R}\{i\tilde\Pi_1\},\qquad
\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2\cap\mathbb{M}_+=\mathbb{R}\{\tilde\Pi_2\},\qquad \mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2\cap\mathbb{M}_-=\mathbb{R}\{i\tilde\Pi_2\}.
$$

The material–informational split therefore cuts **across** both ideals rather than aligning with them: neither ideal is material and neither is informational, and each carries one real direction of each sector. The complement is a second copy of the state space and not a second sector.

## Worked Verification

The claims are recomputed on the basis $(\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Upsilon_2,\tilde\Upsilon_1,\tilde\Pi_2)$ and on the matrix units $E_{ij}$.

- **The idempotents and the complement.** $\tilde\Pi_1^2=\tilde\Pi_1$, $\tilde\Pi_2^2=\tilde\Pi_2$, $\tilde\Pi_1\tilde\Pi_2=\tilde\Pi_2\tilde\Pi_1=0$, $\tilde\Pi_1+\tilde\Pi_2=e_0$, hence $\tilde\Pi_2=e_0-\tilde\Pi_1$.
- **The complement is a left ideal.** Every left multiplication maps $\{\tilde\Pi_2,\tilde\Upsilon_1\}$ into $\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_2,\tilde\Upsilon_1\}$, with the matrices of §*The Left Action on the Complement*.
- **The module isomorphism.** $\tilde\Pi_1\tilde\Upsilon_1=\tilde\Upsilon_1$ and $\tilde\Upsilon_2\tilde\Upsilon_1=\tilde\Pi_2$, so $S\tilde\Upsilon_1=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2$; and $\tilde\Pi_2\tilde\Upsilon_2=\tilde\Upsilon_2$, $\tilde\Upsilon_1\tilde\Upsilon_2=\tilde\Pi_1$, so $(\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2)\tilde\Upsilon_2=S$.
- **The algebra's form does not separate the two ideals.** $B(\tilde\Upsilon_2,\tilde\Upsilon_1)=B(\tilde\Upsilon_1,\tilde\Upsilon_2)=\tfrac12$, while $B(\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Pi_2)=B(\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Upsilon_1)=B(\tilde\Upsilon_2,\tilde\Pi_2)=0$.
- **The $B$-orthogonal complement.** $S^{\perp_B}=\{v_1=v_3=0\}=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Upsilon_2,\tilde\Pi_2\}$, of dimension two, and $S\cap S^{\perp_B}=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Upsilon_2=\operatorname{rad}B_S$.
- **The conjugate reaches it, the adjoint does not.** $\overline{\tilde\Pi_1}=\tilde\Pi_2$ and $\overline{\tilde\Upsilon_2}=-\tilde\Upsilon_1$, so $\bar S=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2$; while $\tilde\Pi_1^{*}=\tilde\Pi_1$ and $\tilde\Upsilon_2^{*}=\tilde\Upsilon_1$, so $S^{*}=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Upsilon_1\}=\tilde\Pi_1\mathbb{B}\neq\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2$.
- **The sector intersections.** With $\mathbb{M}_\pm$ the fixed spaces of $\pm{}^{*}$, the four intersections above each have real dimension one.

## Boundaries

- **Cited rather than re-derived.** The idempotents, the Peirce decomposition and the ideals are *Ideals and Peirce Decomposition of the General Plain Algebra* and *The Biquaternion Vacuum as a Minimal Idempotent*; the module theory is *Modules over the General Plain Algebra of Biquaternions*; the algebra's form is *The Ordinary Product and the Material Sector*; the two structures of the carrier are the companion article *The Spinor Space as a Module of the General Plain Algebra*; the mass and the spectral triple are *Bosonization in Biquaternionic Form* and *The Spectral Triple of the Biquaternion Algebra*.
- **Not claimed.** The article identifies no physical degree of freedom with the complement; it does not choose between the occupation, conjugate and chirality interpretations of the doubling; and it states the module complement of the spinor space and the failure of the algebra's form to see it, not a new structure of the algebra.
- **Narrower than the general vacuum frontier.** Only the pair $(\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Pi_2)$, the two coordinate columns, is treated. The general idempotent $\tilde\Pi(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu})=\tfrac12(e_0+i\hat{\boldsymbol\mu})$ generates a minimal left ideal for every unit vector $\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}$, and its complement is a different subspace; the family is the projective line of *Modules over the General Plain Algebra of Biquaternions*.
- **The Hilbert complement is elsewhere.** The orthogonal complement of $S$ for the positive definite sesquilinear form, and the coincidence of that complement with the module complement, are the subject of the sibling article *The Complementary Space of the Spinor Space in the General Plain Sesqualgebra*. The present article's point is precisely that for the algebra's own bilinear form no such coincidence holds.

## Physical Readings

- **The state space is doubled, and the complement is the second copy.** The interpretation is that the algebra offers two identical state spaces rather than one, so any operator that acts on the right can mix them, and the framework's two-component spinor structure is this doubling. Boundary: a counting statement about the algebra; it adds no second particle by itself.
- **The mass couples the state space to its complement.** The interpretation is that the chirality-off-diagonal mass is a right multiplication, and a right multiplication is exactly what moves a state from $S$ to $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2$, so the mass's other component is the complement. Boundary: the article gives the algebraic mechanism of the coupling and no value or spectrum of the mass; the number belongs to the dynamical articles.
- **The module split and the form's split are different.** The interpretation is that the algebra's own form does not separate the two ideals — the nilpotent is invisible to it — so the orthogonal interpretation of the doubling requires the metric of the sibling article and not the composition form of this one. Boundary: a statement about the two forms on the same algebra.
- **The second vacuum is the complement.** The interpretation is that the two one-mode ideals are the two choices of vacuum idempotent, so the complement is what the other vacuum generates, and no algebraic fact prefers one vacuum. Boundary: the module is unique; only the vacuum is a choice.
- **The doubling is not a chirality in the module sense.** The interpretation is that the complement is isomorphic to $S$, so at the module level the doubling is a copy and not a chirality; where the corpus calls the two ideals the two chiralities it is using a second convention. Boundary: a convention warning, not a new result.

## Summary

The complementary space of the spinor space $S=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_1$ is the second minimal left ideal

$$
\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Pi_2,\tilde\Upsilon_1\},\qquad \tilde\Pi_2=e_0-\tilde\Pi_1,
$$

the second column of the matrix model. It is a left module of the general plain algebra, isomorphic to $S$ through the $\mathbb{B}$-linear map of right multiplication by the nilpotent $\tilde\Upsilon_1$, $S\tilde\Upsilon_1=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2$; the naive relabelling of the two idempotents and the two nilpotents is not a module map. It is reached also by the anti-linear coefficient conjugation $\bar{\cdot}$, which is a real structure of the ring and not an operation of the plain product.

The compatibility of the complement with the algebra's own symmetric form $B(\tilde P,\tilde Q)=\operatorname{Sc}(\tilde P\tilde Q)$ is a failure, and it is the article's sharp point. Because $B(\tilde\Upsilon_2,\tilde\Upsilon_1)=\tfrac12$, the module decomposition $\mathbb{B}=S\oplus\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2$ is not an orthogonal decomposition; the $B$-orthogonal complement of $S$ is the different subspace $S^{\perp_B}=\mathbb{C}\{\tilde\Upsilon_2,\tilde\Pi_2\}$, and $S+S^{\perp_B}$ is only three-dimensional, with $S\cap S^{\perp_B}=\operatorname{rad}B_S=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Upsilon_2$. The nilpotent direction is exactly what the algebra's form cannot see. The orthogonal complement of $S$, and its coincidence with the module complement, require the positive definite sesquilinear form and are the sibling article's subject.

Physically the complement is the **second copy of the state space**, $\mathbb{B}=S\oplus\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_2$, which an operator acting on the right can mix with the first; it is where the chirality-off-diagonal mass, itself a right multiplication, takes a state. It is not the dual, which is the right ideal $\tilde\Pi_1\mathbb{B}$ reached by the Hermitian conjugation ${}^{*}$, and it is not a second representation: it carries the same defining representation of the double cover. Neither ideal is contained in a sector — each meets $\mathbb{M}_+$ and $\mathbb{M}_-$ in one real dimension — so the ideal split and the sector split are independent.
