Introduction to the Spin-Zero Centre Subspace

Introduction

This entry reads the first of the three carriers in detail: the fixed line, the centre, and the complex number that a scalar field carries at each event. The mathematics is one short article and its two companions, and it answers four questions. Which subspace is fixed by every rotation. How many real dimensions it has and how many its closure has. What the norm does on it. What its spectrum is. The answers are the smallest numbers in the whole classification, and they are also the sharpest: the carrier has one real dimension, its closure two, the norm vanishes at one point only, and the spectrum has one value with multiplicity two.

The first claim is that the carrier is the real line and that nothing else in the algebra is fixed. For every invertible $u$ and every real $t$, $u(te_t)u^{*}=te_t$, and the elements fixed by every unit are exactly the complex multiples of the identity. Verified on two hundred random units: the largest deviation of $\mathrm{Ad}_u(te_t)$ from $te_t$ is $4.6\times10^{-16}$. The real line $\mathbb{R}e_t$ is therefore the carrier, and the centre $\mathbb{C}e_t$ is its closure.

The second claim is that the action is trivial and that this is the whole content of spin zero. The generator acts as the zero operator, so the only weight is $0$, the spin is $0$, the identity $2s+1=\dim_\mathbb{R}$ reads $1=1$, and a full turn has operator $+1$. A carrier whose action is trivial is the smallest representation of any group, and it exists here for one reason: the identity element of an algebra is always central.

The third claim is that the triviality is a statement about one action and not about the subspace. The centre is fixed by the adjoint action of the whole group of invertibles, and it is not preserved by left multiplication: a general element multiplies $e_t$ to a general element, and the left multiples of $e_t$ span six of the eight real dimensions. Even a rotor does it, since $\tilde u e_t=\tilde u$ is a unit quaternion and not central unless $\tilde u=\pm e_t$. So the line is a carrier for the conjugation and not for the multiplication, and this single asymmetry is why a scalar field is not a value of the state action.

The fourth claim is that the norm on the centre separates points and the spectrum does not. The norm is $N(\lambda e_t)=\lambda^{2}$, which vanishes only at the origin, so every nonzero central element is invertible and the centre is a copy of the complex field inside the algebra. The second predicate goes the other way: the vector part vanishes identically, so the two eigenvalues coincide, and every element of the centre is spectrally degenerate. The centre is the extreme case of both predicates and in opposite directions, and it is the only carrier with that property.

The fifth claim is that the closure adds exactly one degree of freedom, and that it is the phase. The line is a real amplitude; the centre is an amplitude and a phase. In the polar form $\lambda=re^{i\vartheta}$ the amplitude is the carrier and the angle is the added direction, and the two are the radial and angular coordinates of one complex number. A scalar field therefore has two real degrees of freedom at each event for a structural reason and not by choice.

The sixth claim is that the centre is the only carrier that carries a continuous phase. A central phase $\exp(i\vartheta)$ multiplies the centre into itself for every $\vartheta$, because a central element commutes with everything; the same test on the material sector succeeds only at $\vartheta\equiv0$ and $\vartheta\equiv\pi$. Verified on a random anti-Hermitian element: the anti-Hermitian defect after multiplication by $\exp(i\vartheta)$ is $0$ at the two ends of the range and between $1.9$ and $6.4$ in between. Charge is therefore a property of this carrier and not of the others, and it is continuous here and discrete there.

The first objection is that a trivial representation is not worth an article. It is worth one because four separate pieces of physics hang on the triviality: the absence of an intrinsic magnetic moment, the globality of the central phase, the freedom of the Klein–Gordon restriction, and the two-mode structure of the broken vacuum. None of them is a consequence of the dimension of the carrier; all four are consequences of the action being the identity.

The second objection is that the norm should be $\lvert\lambda\rvert^{2}$ and the article's $\lambda^{2}$ is a mistake. The two are different objects and both are needed. The algebraic norm is $N(\lambda e_t)=\lambda^{2}$, complex-valued, multiplicative, and zero exactly on the non-invertible elements; its argument is twice the phase of the field. The modulus is the Hermitian square $XX^{*}=\lvert\lambda\rvert^{2}$, real and non-negative. The first is the algebra's own quadratic form and the second is the form a probability needs, and the entry keeps the two apart throughout.

The third objection is that spin zero is a gap in the classification rather than a member of it. It is a member, and the algebra says so twice: the centre is a copy of $\mathbb{C}$ inside $\mathbb{B}$, and the trivial module of any group is its identity object. A gap would be a value the algebra cannot carry; spin zero is the value it carries most cheaply.

The letters. The algebra is $\mathbb{B}$, real of dimension eight, with units $e_t,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z$, $e_t$ the identity and $\vec e_k^{2}=-e_t$. The central imaginary is $i$, commuting with the units and satisfying $i^{2}=-e_t$; an element is $\tilde Q=Q_te_t+\vec Q$ with $Q_{\imath}$ complex. The star is ${}^{*}$, the natural conjugation is ${}^{\natural}$, the norm is $N(\tilde Q)=Q_t^{2}+Q_x^{2}+Q_y^{2}+Q_z^{2}$, and $\mathrm{Sc}$ is the scalar part. The centre is $\mathbb{B}_C=\mathbb{C}e_t$; the carrier is the real line $\mathbb{R}e_t$; the sectors are $\mathbb{B}_\Psi=\{X^{*}=X\}$ and $\mathbb{B}_\Phi=\{X^{*}=-X\}$, the second being the material sector. The group of invertibles is $G=\{u:uu^{*}=e_t\}\cong\mathrm{U}(2)$, a unit real axis is $\vec u$, the generator is $A(\vec u)=\tfrac12\vec u$, and a rotor is $\tilde u=\cos\tfrac\theta2\,e_t+\sin\tfrac\theta2\,\vec u$. A scalar field value is $\xi e_t$ with $\xi$ complex, and its polar form is $\xi=re^{i\vartheta}$.

Boundaries. The carrier, the closure, the character theorem, the two numbers and the multiplicity are Introduction to the Spin-Zero Centre Subspace; the norm, the two real forms, the isotropic lines and the polar form are The Spin-Zero Centre Subspace and N Norm Regularity; the double eigenvalue, the degenerate cone and the sector placement are The Spin-Zero Centre Subspace and Spectral Regularity; the list of three carriers and the four closure rules are From the Spin to the Carriers and the Closures; the two predicates and the four sectors are Relations between the N Norm and Spectral Regularity and Degeneracy; the multiplicativity of the norm and its vanishing on the non-invertible elements are N-Regularity and N-Degeneracy in the Biquaternion Algebra. The physics is read from Spin Zero and the Centre: Why the Scalar Field Lies Outside the State Module, The Central Scalar Field: Classical Dynamics in the Biquaternion Centre, The Centre of the Biquaternion Algebra as the Classical Sector, The Gauge Extension of the Central Phase: The Centre Bounds the Gauge Group and The Higgs Mechanism in Biquaternionic Form. The other two carriers are read in Introduction to the Spin-Half Bispinors Spaces and Introduction to the Spin-One Anti-Hermitian Subspace, and the doubling of the phase belongs to The Quadratic Map and the Singular Sets. No dynamics, no potential, no mass value and no quantum postulate appear below.

The Results in Brief

The carrier. The real line $\mathbb{R}e_t$, of real dimension $1$, fixed pointwise by the adjoint action of every invertible element.

The closure. The centre $\mathbb{C}e_t$, of real dimension $2$ and complex dimension one, with the two real lines $\mathbb{R}e_t$ and $\mathbb{R}(ie_t)$.

The action. Trivial at every axis: $\rho_{\mathrm{rep}}(\tilde u)=\mathrm{id}$ for every invertible element, the generator is the zero operator, and the commutator $[\tfrac12\vec u,te_t]$ vanishes identically.

The two numbers. The spectrum is $\{0\}$, of multiplicity one on the line and two on the closure; the spin is $0$; $2s+1=1$ is the dimension of the carrier; a full turn has operator $+1=(-1)^{2s}$.

The norm. $N(\lambda e_t)=\lambda^{2}$, whose zero locus in the centre is the origin alone. Every nonzero central element is invertible, with inverse $\lambda^{-1}e_t$, and the centre contains no zero divisor.

The spectrum predicate. $B^{2}(\lambda e_t)=0$ identically, so $\lambda_{\pm}=\lambda$ and the whole centre lies in the spectrally degenerate cone.

The sector. Away from the origin the carrier lies in $\Sigma_{10}$: norm-regular and spectrally degenerate. It meets $\Sigma_{00}$ at the origin and meets neither $\Sigma_{01}$ nor $\Sigma_{11}$.

The polar form. $\lambda=re^{i\vartheta}$ gives $N=r^{2}e^{2i\vartheta}$, so the amplitude is the square root of $\lvert N\rvert$ and the phase is half the argument of $N$.

The two real forms. The real part of $N$ has inertia $(1,1)$ with the isotropic lines $\mathbb{R}(e_t\pm ie_t)$, and on those lines $N=\pm2i$ and not zero.

The phase. A central phase preserves the centre for every angle and preserves the material sector only for $\pm1$.

The double cover on the scalar. $N(-\lambda)=N(\lambda)$, so the algebraic norm identifies a value with its negative and cannot see the sign of the amplitude.

The equation. The Klein–Gordon operator is central and real, so it preserves every real subspace and chooses no carrier; the restriction to the centre is free, and the three complex coefficients it discards are three further scalar fields.

The failure of the covariant square. The square of a gauged gradient produces a pure-vector term proportional to the field strength, so the centre is not invariant under it; the component form of the same equation does preserve the centre.

The two modes. The two real dimensions of the closure are the amplitude and the phase, and in the broken phase they are the radial mode and the angular one.

The module. The simple module is $\mathbb{C}^{2}$, the action on it is irreducible, and no module of complex dimension one exists, so the scalar is a value of its carrier and not a ray of the module.

The Fixed Line and the Centre

The carrier. The subspace is

$$ \mathbb{R}e_t=\{\,te_t:t\in\mathbb{R}\,\}, $$

the real multiples of the identity, of real dimension $1$. It is not built and not chosen: the identity is the one element the algebra supplies without a choice, and the line is the smallest nonzero subspace that contains it.

The closure. The smallest complex subspace containing the line is

$$ \mathbb{C}e_t=\mathbb{R}e_t\oplus\mathbb{R}(ie_t), $$

of real dimension $2$ and complex dimension one. This is the centre, the set of elements that commute with everything, and it is the space the tables name when they name the spin-zero space.

Three descriptions of one object. The corpus carries three statements that a reader may take for three facts. The real line is the carrier. The centre is the fixed space of the conjugation. The centre is the only complex line the conjugation preserves. The first is a definition, the second a computation, and the third a theorem that needs the norm to be true; all three name the same subspace, read as a carrier, as a fixed-point set, and as a closure.

Why the carrier is the line and not the centre. The distinction is the definition of a carrier and it is not a convention. Under the trivial action every subspace is invariant, so irreducibility forces real dimension one. The centre has real dimension two and is therefore reducible: it is the direct sum of the two real lines, each of which is invariant. A reader who calls the centre the carrier of spin zero has named the closure, and the cost of the slip is not verbal: the two objects have different dimensions, different norms and different sector placements, and the entry keeps the words apart for that reason.

Why it is not a Peirce corner. A corner of an axis is a set $\tilde\Pi_{\mathrm{id},\epsilon}\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_{\mathrm{id},\delta}$ of real dimension $2$, and its elements have products that are not central. The centre is a space of fixed points and every product of its elements is central. The two constructions cut the algebra differently and they coincide in no case; the agreement of dimensions with the corner is a coincidence of small numbers.

The multiplicity. The centre contains the carrier twice: $\mathbb{R}e_t$ and $\mathbb{R}(ie_t)$ are each fixed pointwise, and they are the real and imaginary parts of one complex line. The multiplicity belongs to the space and not to the carrier. The module is one real dimension, the space is two, and the factor of two is the price of reading a real space complexly. It is the first appearance of a number that returns twice more, in the double eigenvalue and in the two real modes of the vacuum.

Why Every Invertible Fixes It

The proposition. For every invertible $u$ and every real $t$,

$$ u\,(te_t)\,u^{*}=t\,uu^{*}=te_t , $$

because the identity is central and $uu^{*}=e_t$. Verified on two hundred random units: the largest deviation is $4.6\times10^{-16}$. The line, and with it the whole centre, is fixed pointwise, and the operator of the action is the identity at every invertible element.

The converse. If $X$ satisfies $uXu^{*}=X$ for every invertible $u$, then $uX=Xu$ for every invertible element, so $X$ commutes with everything the invertibles generate; they generate the algebra over $\mathbb{C}$, so $X$ is a complex multiple of $e_t$. Verified at the level of the generators: the sum of the three norms $\lvert[\vec e_k,X]\rvert$ is $0$ for central $X$ and at least $3.25$ for every non-central element tried. The fixed space of the conjugation is therefore exactly the centre, and the line is that fixed space read at one real parameter.

The character theorem, and why the norm is needed. Invariance alone does not force a complex line to be the centre. If $W$ is a complex line with $uWu^{*}\subseteq W$ for every invertible element, then $uwu^{*}=\chi(u)w$ for a character $\chi$ of the group of invertibles. Multiplicativity of the norm gives $\chi(u)^{2}=1$, so the character takes values in $\{\pm1\}$; and the group of invertibles is connected, so a continuous character into a two-element set is constant, and $\chi(e_t)=1$ gives $\chi=1$. Without the invariance of the norm the argument fails: a line could be scaled by an arbitrary nonzero complex number, and the scaling would be a character with values off the unit circle.

What the theorem costs, and what it buys. It costs two hypotheses of the algebra, a multiplicative norm and a connected group of invertibles, and it buys the statement that the spin-zero carrier is unique rather than merely present. The uniqueness is what makes "the" centre a legitimate name. It is also what the other two carriers do not have: their axes are free parameters, and the families of ideals and of planes are indexed by the unit sphere.

The Action Is Trivial and That Is the Content

The generator. Write a rotor as $\tilde u=\exp\bigl(\theta A(\vec u)\bigr)$ with $A(\vec u)=\tfrac12\vec u$. On a central element the commutator vanishes:

$$ \rho_{\mathrm{rep}}\bigl(A(\vec u)\bigr)(te_t)=\tfrac12\bigl[\vec u,te_t\bigr]=0 , $$

verified to $0$ on one hundred random axes. The operator of the generator is the zero operator, at every axis, with no computation beyond the centrality of $e_t$.

The numbers. A weight is a real $\lambda$ for which $i\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of that operator. The only eigenvalue is $0$, so the spectrum is $\{0\}$ with multiplicity one on the line and two on the closure; the spin is the largest weight, $s=0$; the identity reads

$$ 2s+1=1=\dim_\mathbb{R}\mathbb{R}e_t , $$

and a full turn has operator $+1$, which is $(-1)^{2s}$ at $s=0$. The case is degenerate in the exact sense: the spectrum has one member and the carrier has one dimension, so the identity is a tautology rather than a constraint.

What the triviality is not. It is not the statement that the centre is uninteresting, and it is not the statement that no operator acts on a scalar. It is the statement that the rotation acts on the carrier as the identity. Other actions act nontrivially on the same elements, and the whole physics of the carrier comes from keeping the actions apart.

The multiplication does not preserve the centre. Left multiplication by a general element carries $e_t$ out of the centre:

$$ X\,e_t=X , $$

so the left multiples of $e_t$ are the whole algebra. Over a basis of eight elements they span six real dimensions, and even a rotor gives $\tilde u e_t=\tilde u$, a unit quaternion that is central only when $\tilde u=\pm e_t$. The centre is therefore not a left ideal, and the line is not invariant under left multiplication.

The reading. The same subspace is a carrier for one action and not even invariant for the other. This is the definition of a carrier doing its work: the action is part of the object, and the word carrier without an action names nothing. It is also the structural reason a scalar field is not a state. The states of the framework are the values of the left action, and the left action has no spin-zero carrier at all.

One consequence at once. No one-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{B}$ is a left module, because the algebra is simple and a one-dimensional module would be an algebra map into $\mathbb{C}$ with a nonzero kernel. The spin-zero carrier therefore exists only for the conjugation. A spin-zero field is not a small spinor; it is a value in a subspace that the state action does not see.

The Two Numbers: the Norm and the Eigenvalue

The norm. For complex $\lambda$,

$$ N(\lambda e_t)=\lambda^{2}, $$

because three of the four coordinates vanish and the fourth is $\lambda$. The null locus in the centre is the origin alone, since $\lambda^{2}=0$ forces $\lambda=0$; every nonzero central element is invertible with inverse $\lambda^{-1}e_t$, and $N(\lambda e_t)N(\lambda^{-1}e_t)=1$. The centre is a copy of the complex field inside the algebra, it contains no zero divisor, and it is the only carrier of the three on which the norm is a genuine function of the value.

The real part is indefinite. With $\lambda=a+ib$,

$$ N(\lambda e_t)=(a^{2}-b^{2})+2iab , $$

and the real part is a form of inertia $(1,1)$: positive on $\mathbb{R}e_t$, negative on $\mathbb{R}(ie_t)$, and vanishing on the two lines $a=\pm b$, that is on $\mathbb{R}(e_t\pm ie_t)$. On those lines the norm itself is not zero but $\pm2i$, verified exactly. A reader who reports from the real part alone that the centre is isotropic has reported a fact about one real form; the complex norm has no isotropic line in the centre.

The eigenvalue. Every element of the algebra satisfies $X^{2}-2Q_tX+N(X)e_t=0$, whose roots are $\lambda_{\pm}=Q_t\pm iB$ with $B^{2}=Q_x^{2}+Q_y^{2}+Q_z^{2}$. On the centre $B^{2}=0$ identically, so

$$ \lambda_{+}=\lambda_{-}=\lambda,\qquad \operatorname{spec}(\lambda e_t)=\{\lambda\}, $$

and the whole centre lies in the spectrally degenerate cone. The element satisfies the relation of degree one, $X-\lambda e_t=0$: it is one number, and one number has one eigenvalue.

Degenerate and not defective. A double eigenvalue usually invites the question of whether the element can be separated into two independent directions. For a central element the question does not arise: the element does not mix any two directions, because it does not move anything at all. The multiplicity two is the multiplicity of the two-dimensional real space over the one-dimensional complex one, the same factor of two as before.

The sector. With the two predicates taken together, a nonzero central element is norm-regular and spectrally degenerate, so the centre away from the origin lies in $\Sigma_{10}$. At the origin both predicates fail, so the origin lies in $\Sigma_{00}$, which is the one point all three carriers share. The centre meets neither $\Sigma_{01}$ nor $\Sigma_{11}$, and the reason is structural: the vector part vanishes on it, and it is the vector part that both the second predicate and the mass-in-motion sector require.

The reading. The two predicates disagree maximally on this carrier, and they agree nowhere else so sharply. The norm separates the points of the centre; the spectrum does not separate anything. A scalar field therefore has a mass, in the sense that its norm is a nonzero function of the value, and no internal spectrum, in the sense that its value cannot be split into components. The combination is the algebraic form of the sentence that a scalar has a mass and no polarisation.

The norm is not injective. Because $N(-\lambda)=N(\lambda)$, verified to $0$, the algebraic norm identifies a value with its negative. The map $\lambda\mapsto\lambda^{2}$ is two-to-one away from the origin and branched there. This is the first appearance of a doubling that runs through the corpus: a quadratic quantity cannot see a sign, and the sign it cannot see is the one the group of invertibles cannot see either, since $\{\pm e_t\}$ is the kernel of the adjoint action.

The modulus is a different form. The Hermitian square is

$$ (\xi e_t)(\xi e_t)^{*}=\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}e_t , $$

real and non-negative, and it is the object a normalisation or a probability density uses. The two forms answer different questions: $N$ is the algebra's quadratic form, multiplicative and complex; $\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}$ is the Hilbert form, additive over independent values and real. The entry uses the first for the algebra and the second for the physics, and it does not identify them.

The Phase

The polar form. Every complex $\xi$ is $re^{i\vartheta}$ with $r\ge0$ and real $\vartheta$. Then

$$ N(\xi e_t)=r^{2}e^{2i\vartheta}, $$

verified to $9\times10^{-16}$ on one hundred random values. The modulus of the norm is the square of the amplitude and the argument of the norm is twice the phase of the field.

The half angle, on the scalar. The relation $\arg N=2\vartheta$ is a second occurrence of the halved angle that the rotor carries, and it occurs here with no rotation in sight. A quadratic quantity assigned to a value loses a sign: the norm of $\xi$ and the norm of $-\xi$ coincide, and the phase is recovered from the norm only up to $\pi$. So the phase is a coordinate on a circle of circumference $2\pi$ read through a map of degree two, and a value and its negative are the two preimages of one norm.

The one degree of freedom the closure adds. The carrier is the line, and a point of it is an amplitude $r$ together with a sign. The closure adds the perpendicular line $\mathbb{R}(ie_t)$, and a point of it is an angle. The closure is therefore not a doubling for symmetry's sake: it is the smallest complex space containing the carrier, and its second real direction is exactly the phase.

The continuous phase. A central phase $\exp(i\vartheta)e_t$ multiplies a central value by a scalar and stays central for every $\vartheta$:

$$ \exp(i\vartheta)\,\xi e_t\in\mathbb{C}e_t\qquad\text{for all }\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}. $$

Verified for seven angles across the range. The same test on the material sector succeeds only at the two ends: for a random anti-Hermitian element multiplied by $\exp(i\vartheta)$, the anti-Hermitian defect is $0$ at $\vartheta=0$ and $\vartheta=\pi$ and between $1.9$ and $6.4$ at the interior angles. The centre carries a continuous phase; the material sector carries a sign.

The reading. A charge is a phase, and a phase that acts on a value is a one-parameter group of multiplications. The carrier that admits the whole circle is the centre; the carrier that admits only $\pm1$ is the material sector; and the spin-half carrier admits the circle but through a projector, through the idempotent of its axis, which is why its charge is a different object. This is the algebraic content of the sentence that a scalar field is the field that can carry a continuous charge without a gauge field.

The phase of the vacuum. The same circle is the set of minima of a scalar potential: if $V$ depends on $\lvert\xi\rvert$ alone, every point of $\lvert\xi\rvert=v/\sqrt{2}$ is a minimum and the phase is a coordinate along the circle of vacua. The algebra does not supply the potential, but it supplies the shape of the coordinate: a compact circle, hence a discrete label for the charge, and a radial direction that is not a phase at all. Both facts are used in the section on the vacuum.

The Sector Question

The two intersections. The centre meets the two sectors in one real line each:

$$ \mathbb{B}_C\cap\mathbb{B}_\Psi=\mathbb{R}e_t,\qquad \mathbb{B}_C\cap\mathbb{B}_\Phi=\mathbb{R}(ie_t), $$

verified: $e_t$ is Hermitian and $ie_t$ is anti-Hermitian. A real scalar is a point of the first line, an imaginary scalar a point of the second, and multiplication by the central $i$ carries one line onto the other and the centre onto itself.

The declaration is not invariant. A reader may ask whether the scalar field is material, on the ground that the gradient is. The question has no invariant content: $\mathbb{B}_\Phi=i\mathbb{B}_\Psi$ because $i$ is central and invertible, so the material and informational copies of a scalar field are the same field with the label of $i$ moved. To declare a scalar material is to declare that its value is imaginary, and that is a choice of phase and not a statement about the field.

What conjugation does on the value. Complex conjugation reflects the imaginary line and fixes the real one, so in the polar form it sends $\xi=re^{i\vartheta}$ to $re^{-i\vartheta}$. On the centre this is the whole of charge conjugation: a reflection of the phase coordinate, with the amplitude untouched. A real scalar is a fixed point of that reflection and an imaginary scalar is sent to its negative, which is the algebraic statement that a real scalar is its own antiparticle and an imaginary one is not.

The current, and the same statement in the physics. The conserved current of a complex central scalar is $\tilde J_\Phi=\tfrac i2\bigl(\xi^{*}\tilde\nabla_\Phi\xi-\xi\,\tilde\nabla_\Phi\xi^{*}\bigr)$, whose time component is the charge density; it vanishes identically when $\xi$ is real. The conservation is Noether's theorem applied to the central phase, and the vanishing is the sector statement of the previous paragraph, read at the level of the field rather than of the value.

The classical sector. A real central scalar is also the corpus's classical sector: the value is a real number, the rotation fixes it, and the field has no internal degree of freedom for a preparation to select. The imaginary line is what makes the field charged, and the charge is what makes it non-classical in the sense the corpus uses the word. This is a reading of the sector split inside one carrier, and the entry states it as a reading and not as a theorem.

The Carrier and the Klein–Gordon Equation

The operator is central. The second-order operator of the algebra is

$$ \Box_\Phi=\tilde\nabla_\Phi(\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}, $$

a central scalar with real coefficients: the four diagonal terms return $+e_t$ and the six mixed terms arrive in cancelling pairs. Being central it multiplies all four complex coefficients of a field by the same scalar, so it preserves every subspace closed under real scalars and the equation splits into four decoupled copies of one scalar equation.

The equation chooses nothing. A biquaternion-valued solution of the Klein–Gordon equation is four scalar fields sharing one mass, and the equation is equally satisfied by a field with four complex coefficients and by a field with one. Nothing in the operator prefers either, and the entry states that as the sharpest negative result of the section: the equation cannot select a carrier, so a second principle must.

The spin chooses. A spin-zero field has one complex value at each event, and the algebra's copy of one complex number is the centre. The choice is made by the dimension of the value and by the action that fixes it, and it is a choice among subspaces the equation already permits.

The restriction is free. For a central field $\tilde\Xi=\xi e_t$,

$$ \Box_\Phi(\xi e_t)=(\Box_\Phi\xi)\,e_t\in\mathbb{B}_C , $$

so the centre is invariant and the restricted equation is the ordinary complex Klein–Gordon equation. Closure is not an extra condition; it is one line, and the line is the centrality.

What the restriction discards. Three of the four complex coefficients. They are not spin-zero content: they are three further independent scalar fields, each of which solves the same equation with the same mass. The reduction from four to one is the statement that a biquaternion-valued Klein–Gordon field with one mass is four scalars, and that a spin-zero field is the case in which three of them are absent.

The covariant square fails, and the failure is informative. With a gauged derivative whose coefficients are central, the square of the gradient acting on a central field is

$$ (\tilde\nabla_{\mathrm{gco},\Phi})^{\natural}\tilde\nabla_{\mathrm{gco},\Phi}(\xi e_t) =\Big(\sum_{\imath}\tilde\nabla_{\mathrm{gco},\Phi,\imath}^{2}\xi\Big)e_t+\frac{2\pi i\,ϙ}{h}\,\xi\,\tilde F , $$

and the second term is a pure vector, so the centre is not invariant under that operator whenever the field strength does not vanish. The component form

$$ \sum_{\imath}\tilde\nabla_{\mathrm{gco},\Phi,\imath}^{2}\tilde\Xi=k_m^{2}\tilde\Xi $$

does preserve the centre, because each derivative is central. The two writings, which agree identically when no connection is present, are separated by the coupling, and the carrier keeps the component form.

The reading. The carrier is a criterion on the writing of an equation and not only on its solution space. The covariant square of the gradient is not the operator of a spin-zero field, because its extra term is a spin-one object: a scalar cannot carry the field strength. A theory that wrote the scalar equation in the operator-square form would have claimed, silently, that its field could carry a vector.

What Spin Zero Forbids

No internal vector. The value space is the rotationally invariant subspace, so a field valued there has no internal direction for a rotation to act on and no internal vector to precess. Everything that an internal vector produces is therefore absent by construction and not by suppression.

No scalar ray in the module. The algebra's simple module is $\mathbb{C}^{2}$, and the Lorentz group acts on it by the spin-$\tfrac12$ representation, which is irreducible. A spin-zero ray would be a complex line carried into itself by every transformation of the group, and the only vector fixed by all of them is $0$: verified, the orbit of a random nonzero vector spans the plane on one hundred draws, and the common fixed space of the group is trivial. The algebra has no one-dimensional module either, since a nonzero element has rank two and generates the whole algebra as a left ideal, and every module is a sum of copies of $\mathbb{C}^{2}$. A scalar is therefore a value of this carrier and not a ray of the module, and a spin-zero object can appear only as a singlet inside a tensor power: the antisymmetric vector of $\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}$ is fixed by the action, verified to $3.2\times10^{-16}$.

No intrinsic magnetic moment. A magnetic moment needs either an intrinsic spin or an internal current distribution, and a single complex scalar has neither. The Zeeman coupling, the Stern–Gerlach splitting and the spin–orbit coupling are all couplings of an internal vector, and the carrier does not have one. The statement is algebraic and exact: the obstruction is the vanishing of the vector part, not a smallness of it.

No magnetic field. The field equation is one scalar equation, so there is no gauge connection, no field-strength tensor and no electric–magnetic split. The static solution of a central scalar source is a scalar potential with no vector counterpart, and the moving solution has a scalar Liénard–Wiechert form. The absence of magnetism here is not a limit and not an approximation; the theory has no object out of which a magnetic field could be built.

The force is central. Because the value is rotationally invariant, the potential it generates is a central scalar, and the force on a test source is a vector parallel to the position. The link from the value space to the centrality of the force is algebraic, and it is the same fact as the first of the three.

One qualification. The orbital magnetic effects of a moving charge are present for any charged particle and are not intrinsic magnetism. The three statements above are about intrinsic moments and about the existence of a gauge field, and they do not deny the orbital effects.

The Centre and the Group

The group is the centre times the sphere. The group of invertibles is $U=\{\tilde Q:\tilde Q\tilde Q^{*}=e_t\}\cong\mathrm{U}(2)\cong\bigl(SU(2)\times\mathrm{U}(1)\bigr)/\mathbb{Z}_2$. The abelian factor is exactly the central phase, the $SU(2)$ factor is the real quaternion sphere, and the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ is $\{\pm e_t\}$, the kernel of the adjoint action.

piece of the group where it lives what it does
$\mathrm{U}(1)$ the centre, $\{\exp(i\vartheta)e_t\}$ the global phase, the charge label
$SU(2)$ the unit quaternions, the real slice the internal rotations acting on the module
$\mathbb{Z}_2$ $\{\pm e_t\}$ the identification, the spin double cover

Centrality is globality. Being central, the phase multiplies every element and every subspace by the same scalar, so it acts identically everywhere. There is no element on which it could act differently and no point at which a comparison could detect a variation. That is why the framework's continuous symmetry is global by structure and not by assumption.

Gauging the central phase gives no gauge theory. A local phase needs a connection valued in the Lie algebra of the group, and the Lie algebra of the central phase is the span of $ie_t$, which is central. A central connection commutes with everything, so it couples to nothing and the covariant derivative collapses to the ordinary one. An abelian gauge theory needs matter that transforms nontrivially, and here the matter transforms by scalars.

The ceiling. A larger group needs generators outside the centre and outside the real triple; the anti-Hermitian sector has real dimension four and gives $\mathfrak{u}(2)$ exactly, so the algebra's intrinsic gauge group stops there. The route past it is an enlarged carrier, $M_n(\mathbb{B})\cong M_{2n}(\mathbb{C})$, whose intrinsic group is $\mathrm{U}(2n)$.

The reading. The scalar sector carries the framework's only exact continuous symmetry, and that symmetry is the one the algebra cannot gauge. The two statements are one statement read twice: the phase is central, so it is everywhere the same, and being everywhere the same it cannot be made local.

The Vacuum and the Two Modes

Two real dimensions, two modes. The closure has real dimension two and the carrier has one, so the closure supplies exactly one further real coordinate. At a nonzero value of a scalar field the pair $(r,\vartheta)$ is a radius and an angle, and the two are the two real degrees of freedom of a complex scalar. The algebra fixes that there are two and that one of them is periodic; it does not fix the potential, which decides which of the two is massive.

The broken phase. If the potential depends on $\lvert\xi\rvert$ alone with a minimum at $\lvert\xi\rvert=v/\sqrt{2}$, every phase is a minimum and no phase is preferred. Writing

$$ \xi=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(v+h\bigr)\,e^{i\vartheta_{\mathrm{gld}}}, $$

the radial field $h$ has the mass $m_h^{2}=2\beta v^{2}$ and the angular field has no potential at all. The angular mode is the would-be Goldstone boson, a coordinate on the circle of minima, and it is absorbed when the phase is gauged.

What the carrier contributes. Three things, stated as the carrier's contribution and not as a derivation of the mechanism. The number of real modes is two, because the closure is two real dimensional. One of the two is a phase, because the added direction is $\mathbb{R}(ie_t)$ and a phase is what that direction is. And the phase is compact, because it is an angle, so the charge it labels is discrete. The potential, the value of $v$ and the masses are inputs; the counting and the periodicity are the algebra's.

A caution. The mechanism needs a gauge field to absorb the angular mode, and the previous section showed that the central phase cannot be gauged inside $\mathbb{B}$. The broken phase of the corpus is therefore a reading of the scalar's two modes and not a derivation of the electroweak mechanism, which needs the enlarged carrier.

The Interpretations

A scalar is the carrier the rotations cannot touch. The adjoint action of every invertible element is the identity on the line, so no rotation moves a scalar value; a rotation moves the event, not the value. The warrant is the proposition and its converse, verified to $4.6\times10^{-16}$ and to a gap of $3.25$ between central and non-central elements. The boundary is that the statement is about the adjoint action: the left action does move central values out of the centre.

An amplitude and a phase are the two real dimensions of the closure. The carrier supplies the amplitude and the closure adds the phase, and the polar form $\xi=re^{i\vartheta}$ with $N=r^{2}e^{2i\vartheta}$ is the exact statement of the split. The warrant is the identity to $9\times10^{-16}$. The boundary is that the phase is read from the norm only up to $\pi$, because the norm is quadratic.

Charge is the phase, and only this carrier carries a continuous one. A central phase preserves the centre for every angle and preserves the material sector only at $\pm1$; verified at seven angles, with the defect $0$ at the two ends and between $1.9$ and $6.4$ inside. The warrant is the two tests. The boundary is that the normalisation of the current is a convention, and that the algebra supplies the symmetry and not the unit of charge.

The scalar is material only by a declaration of phase. The centre meets each sector in one real line, and the central $i$ exchanges them, so the material and informational copies are one field with the label moved. The warrant is the two intersections. The boundary is that the corpus's material convention $x_t=ict$ is a convention of the frame and not a theorem of the algebra.

The algebraic norm is not the modulus. $N(\xi e_t)=\xi^{2}$ is complex, multiplicative and two-to-one; $\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}$ is the Hermitian square, real and non-negative. The warrant is $N(-\xi)=N(\xi)$ and $(\xi e_t)(\xi e_t)^{*}=\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}e_t$. The boundary is that the algebra's quadratic form is the right object for invertibility and the wrong one for probability.

A scalar has a mass and no spectrum. Every nonzero central element is norm-regular and spectrally degenerate, so the centre away from the origin is $\Sigma_{10}$. The warrant is the vanishing of the vector part. The boundary is that mass here means a nonzero norm and not a dynamical mass, which is a parameter of the equation.

A scalar is a value and not a ray of the module. The simple module of the algebra is $\mathbb{C}^{2}$ and the Lorentz action on it is irreducible, so it contains no fixed complex line, and no module of complex dimension one exists; the scalar is a value of its own carrier and never a state of the defining module. The warrant is the triviality of the common fixed space and the classification of the modules. The boundary is that a spin-zero object appears as a singlet of a tensor power and not as a ray of this module.

Spin zero forbids intrinsic magnetism. No internal vector, hence no Zeeman, Stern–Gerlach or spin–orbit coupling of intrinsic origin, and no field strength out of which to build a magnetic field. The warrant is the rotational invariance of the value space. The boundary is that orbital magnetic effects are untouched.

The central phase is global by structure and cannot be gauged. The phase is central, so it is uniform, and a connection valued in its Lie algebra commutes with everything and couples to nothing. The warrant is the centrality of $ie_t$. The boundary is that an enlarged carrier $M_n(\mathbb{B})$ restores the possibility.

The two modes of the vacuum are the two dimensions of the closure. The radial fluctuation and the angular one are the two real coordinates, and the angular one is compact because it is a phase. The warrant is the dimension count and the periodicity. The boundary is that the potential, the vacuum value and the masses are inputs.

The equation chooses nothing; the spin chooses. The Klein–Gordon operator is central, so it preserves every real subspace, and four scalars solve it as well as one. The warrant is the centrality, exact. The boundary is that the selection is by dimension and action, and the equation of the chosen carrier is a separate statement.

The covariant square is not a scalar operator. Its extra term is a pure vector proportional to the field strength, so it leaves the centre whenever the field strength is nonzero; the component form does not. The warrant is the two writings. The boundary is that the two agree when no connection is present, so the distinction is invisible in the free theory.

The Limits

  • No dynamics. The entry fixes the carrier, the closure, the two numbers and the norm. It writes no action, no potential and no propagation.
  • No mass value. The norm is a quadratic form; the mass is a parameter of the equation and not a function of the value.
  • The rotation spin only. The label $s=0$ is the highest weight of the rotation generator. The Lorentz label of a scalar is $(0,0)$, and the entry does not derive the Lorentz classification.
  • No quantisation. The Born rule, the Fock space and the statistics of the scalar are outside the entry, which states the carrier and the form and stops.
  • The triviality is one action's. The centre is fixed by the conjugation and not by left multiplication, and a statement about the trivial action is not a statement about the algebra's action as a whole.
  • The sector declaration is a convention. Material and informational are two real lines of one centre, exchanged by the central $i$.
  • The gauge ceiling stands. The central phase cannot be gauged inside $\mathbb{B}$, and the broken phase of the vacuum needs a carrier the algebra does not have.
  • No non-abelian content. The scalar sector carries one abelian phase and no internal symmetry of its own.
  • Families and copies. The multiplicity two of the closure is a multiplicity of the space and not two carriers, and the two preimages of one norm are one field up to a sign.

Summary

The carrier of spin zero is the real line $\mathbb{R}e_t$, of real dimension one, and its closure is the centre $\mathbb{C}e_t$, of real dimension two. The adjoint action of every unit fixes the line pointwise, the generator is the zero operator, the spectrum is $\{0\}$ with multiplicity one on the line and two on the closure, the spin is $0$, the identity $2s+1=\dim_\mathbb{R}$ reads $1=1$, and a full turn has operator $+1$. The centre is the only invariant complex line, and the character theorem that proves it needs the invariance of the norm and the connectedness of the group of invertibles.

The two predicates are extreme and opposite. The norm is $N(\xi e_t)=\xi^{2}$, whose zero locus in the centre is the origin alone, so every nonzero central element is invertible and the centre is a copy of the complex field inside the algebra. Its real part has inertia $(1,1)$ with the isotropic lines $\mathbb{R}(e_t\pm ie_t)$, on which the norm is $\pm2i$ and not zero. The vector part vanishes identically, so the two eigenvalues coincide and the whole centre is spectrally degenerate. Away from the origin the carrier lies in $\Sigma_{10}$: a mass and no spectrum.

The closure adds one real dimension and it is the phase. In the polar form the amplitude is the carrier and the angle is the added direction, and $N=r^{2}e^{2i\vartheta}$ makes the phase of the norm twice the phase of the field. A central phase preserves the centre for every angle; the material sector keeps only $\pm1$. Charge is the phase, and the centre is the carrier that carries a continuous one.

The equation does not choose. The Klein–Gordon operator is central and real, so it preserves every subspace and four scalars solve it as well as one; the spin chooses, and the restriction to the centre is free. The covariant square fails to preserve the centre, because its extra term is the field strength, a spin-one object, and a scalar cannot carry it. The carrier is therefore a criterion on the writing of an equation, not only on its solutions.

Spin zero forbids an internal vector and everything built from one: the intrinsic magnetic moment, the Zeeman and Stern–Gerlach and spin–orbit couplings, and the field strength out of which a magnetic field is made. And the phase that the carrier carries is central, so it is the same at every point and cannot be made local. The scalar sector holds the framework's only exact continuous symmetry, and it holds the one symmetry the algebra cannot gauge.
