Biquaternion Universe

Biquaternion Harmonic Analysis

Introduction

This article introduces harmonic analysis on the biquaternion algebra $\mathbb{B}$. It follows the special functions article, which defined the exponential, the logarithm, and the polar form of a biquaternion, and it uses the division theory article, which characterized the zero divisors and the roots of $-1$.

The treatment is purely mathematical. The goal is to define the biquaternion Fourier transform, establish its basic properties, show how it decomposes into ordinary complex Fourier transforms, and identify the points where the biquaternion structure creates genuinely new phenomena.

The key structural fact is that the Fourier kernel is a biquaternion exponential, and the exponential is defined by a root of $-1$. The roots of $-1$ in $\mathbb{B}$ come in two families: the degenerate roots (the scalar imaginary $i$, and the unit pure real quaternions), and the non-trivial roots (the elements of the form $b\mu + d\nu i$ with $\mu \perp \nu$ and $b^2 - d^2 = 1$). The choice of root determines the nature of the transform. The degenerate roots give the ordinary complex or quaternion Fourier transform; the non-trivial roots give a genuinely biquaternionic transform.

The Fourier Kernel

The exponential as kernel

The kernel of the biquaternion Fourier transform is the biquaternion exponential

$$ \exp\left(-2\pi \mu \frac{nu}{N}\right), $$

where $N$ is the number of samples, $n, u \in \{0, 1, \ldots, N-1\}$ are the sample index and the frequency index, and $\mu$ is a biquaternion root of $-1$, i.e., $\mu^2 = -e_0$.

Because $\mu^2 = -e_0$, the exponential takes the closed form derived in the special functions article:

$$ \exp\left(-2\pi \mu \frac{nu}{N}\right) = \cos\left(2\pi \frac{nu}{N}\right) e_0 - \sin\left(2\pi \frac{nu}{N}\right) \mu, $$

where the sine and cosine are the ordinary real (or complex) trigonometric functions applied to the real (or complex) argument $2\pi nu/N$. The kernel is therefore a unit biquaternion, in the sense that its norm form is $e_0$.

The choice of root

The choice of the root $\mu$ is not neutral. There are two cases.

Degenerate root. If $\mu$ is a degenerate root, then:

  • For $\mu = i$ (the scalar imaginary), the kernel reduces to the ordinary complex exponential, and the transform is the ordinary complex Fourier transform applied to each coefficient.
  • For $\mu = \mu_{\mathbb{R}}$ (a unit pure real quaternion), the kernel is a real quaternion exponential, and the transform is the ordinary quaternion Fourier transform.

Non-trivial root. If $\mu$ is a non-trivial root, of the form

$$ \mu = b \mu_{\mathbb{R}} + d \nu_{\mathbb{R}} i, \qquad \mu_{\mathbb{R}} \perp \nu_{\mathbb{R}}, \quad b^2 - d^2 = 1, $$

then the kernel is a genuinely biquaternionic exponential. It has both a real quaternion part and an imaginary quaternion part, and the transform is not reducible to an ordinary complex or quaternion Fourier transform of the coefficients.

The non-trivial roots exist because the biquaternion algebra is not a division algebra: the equation $q^2 = -e_0$ has solutions beyond the degenerate ones. This is the first place where the biquaternion structure enters the harmonic analysis in an essential way.

The Fourier Transform Pair

Definition

Let $f : \{0, 1, \ldots, N-1\} \to \mathbb{B}$ be a biquaternion-valued sequence. The discrete biquaternion Fourier transform of $f$ is the sequence $F : \{0, 1, \ldots, N-1\} \to \mathbb{B}$ defined by

$$ F[u] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \exp\left(-2\pi \mu \frac{nu}{N}\right) f[n], \qquad u \in \{0, 1, \ldots, N-1\}. $$

The inverse transform is

$$ f[n] = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{u=0}^{N-1} \exp\left(2\pi \mu \frac{nu}{N}\right) F[u], \qquad n \in \{0, 1, \ldots, N-1\}. $$

The placement of the kernel on the left of $f[n]$ is a choice. An alternative transform has the kernel on the right, $F[u] = \sum_n f[n] \exp(-2\pi \mu nu/N)$. The two transforms are closely related and have similar properties.

Invertibility

The invertibility of the transform depends on the properties of the kernel and of the signal.

The kernel has unit norm. For every $n$ and $u$, the exponential $\exp(-2\pi \mu nu/N)$ satisfies

$$ N\left(\exp\left(-2\pi \mu \frac{nu}{N}\right)\right) = e_0, $$

because it is of the form $\cos\theta \, e_0 + \sin\theta \, \mu$ with $\mu^2 = -e_0$, and the norm form of such an element is $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$. So the kernel is always invertible, regardless of the choice of root and the values of $n, u$.

The signal may have vanishing norm. If a sample $f[n]$ has vanishing norm form, $N(f[n]) = 0$, then $f[n]$ is a zero divisor, and it does not contribute to the transform in a way that can be recovered by the inverse. Concretely, if $f[n]$ is a zero divisor, then multiplying it by the kernel (which is invertible) gives another zero divisor, and the sum over $n$ may or may not vanish, depending on the cancellations. In general, samples with vanishing norm form are not recoverable from the transform.

The transform is invertible on signals of non-vanishing norm. If every sample $f[n]$ has non-vanishing norm form, then the transform is invertible: the inverse formula reproduces the original signal. This is the analogue of the invertibility of the ordinary discrete Fourier transform, and it is the case of practical interest.

Factorization into Four Complex Fourier Transforms

The idea

The biquaternion Fourier transform can be computed using ordinary complex Fourier transforms. This is a consequence of the matrix representation $\mathbb{B} \cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$: a biquaternion is a $2 \times 2$ complex matrix, and the transform of a matrix-valued signal is the matrix of the transforms of its entries.

More explicitly, the transform can be factorized into four complex Fourier transforms, one for each of the four complex components of the biquaternion signal, after a change of basis adapted to the root $\mu$.

The change of basis

Let $\mu$ be the root of $-1$ used in the kernel. Choose two other unit pure biquaternions $\nu$ and $\xi$ such that

$$ \mu \perp \nu, \qquad \nu \perp \xi, \qquad \xi \perp \mu, \qquad \mu \nu = \xi. $$

The four elements $e_0, \mu, \nu, \xi$ form a complex orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{B}$. Any biquaternion can be written in this basis as

$$ \tilde{Q} = Q_0 e_0 + Q_\mu \mu + Q_\nu \nu + Q_\xi \xi, $$

with $Q_0, Q_\mu, Q_\nu, Q_\xi \in \mathbb{C}$.

The factorization

Writing the signal in this basis, and using the closed form of the exponential, the transform separates into four pieces:

$$ F[u] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \left( \cos\theta \, e_0 - \sin\theta \, \mu \right) \left( Q_0[n] e_0 + Q_\mu[n] \mu + Q_\nu[n] \nu + Q_\xi[n] \xi \right), $$

where $\theta = 2\pi nu/N$. Expanding the product and collecting terms by basis element, we obtain four sums:

$$ F[u] = F_0[u] e_0 + F_\mu[u] \mu + F_\nu[u] \nu + F_\xi[u] \xi, $$

where each $F_\bullet[u]$ is a complex linear combination of the form

$$ F_\bullet[u] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \exp\left(\pm 2\pi i \frac{nu}{N}\right) G_\bullet[n], $$

with $G_\bullet[n]$ a complex linear combination of the four complex coefficients of $f[n]$ in the new basis, and the sign depending on the term.

The complex Fourier transforms

The four sums are isomorphic to ordinary complex Fourier transforms. Concretely, if we replace the root $\mu$ by the scalar imaginary $i$ in the kernel, the four sums become ordinary complex Fourier transforms of the complex functions $G_\bullet[n]$. So the biquaternion Fourier transform can be computed by:

  1. Changing the basis of each sample $f[n]$ from $\{e_0, e_1, e_2, e_3\}$ to $\{e_0, \mu, \nu, \xi\}$.
  2. Applying the ordinary complex Fourier transform to the four complex components.
  3. Reassembling the four transforms into a biquaternion by the inverse change of basis.

The result is a fast algorithm for the biquaternion Fourier transform, using existing complex FFT libraries.

Why this matters

The factorization is a computational result, but it also has a structural meaning. It shows that the biquaternion Fourier transform is not a fundamentally new operation: it is the ordinary complex Fourier transform applied to the entries of the matrix representation, dressed in biquaternion language. The non-trivial content is the choice of root and the basis change that it induces. Everything else is ordinary harmonic analysis.

The Convolution Theorem

Definition of convolution

Let $f, g : \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{B}$ be two biquaternion-valued sequences, extended to $\mathbb{Z}$ by $f[n] = g[n] = 0$ outside the support. The convolution of $f$ and $g$ is

$$ (f * g)[n] = \sum_{m \in \mathbb{Z}} f[m] g[n - m]. $$

The convolution is not commutative in general, because the biquaternion product is not commutative. The order of the factors matters.

The convolution theorem

The convolution theorem for the biquaternion Fourier transform takes the form

$$ \mathcal{F}[f * g] = \mathcal{F}[f] \cdot \mathcal{F}[g], $$

where $\mathcal{F}$ denotes the biquaternion Fourier transform and the product on the right is the biquaternion product. The statement is not the same as in the complex case: the product on the right is the biquaternion product, and the order of the factors must be preserved.

For the transform with kernel on the left, the convolution theorem reads

$$ \mathcal{F}[f * g][u] = F[u] \cdot G[u], $$

and for the transform with kernel on the right, the theorem reads

$$ \mathcal{F}[f * g][u] = G[u] \cdot F[u]. $$

The two statements differ, and the order must be chosen to match the placement of the kernel.

Consequences

The convolution theorem implies that convolution in the signal domain becomes biquaternion multiplication in the frequency domain. This is the basis for filtering: a biquaternion-valued filter can be applied by pointwise multiplication in the frequency domain.

It also implies that the convolution is associative (because the biquaternion product is associative), distributive over addition, but not commutative in general. The non-commutativity is a genuinely biquaternionic feature of the harmonic analysis.

The Continuous Transform

Definition

For a function $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{B}$ that is integrable, the continuous biquaternion Fourier transform is

$$ F(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \exp\left(-2\pi \mu \omega t\right) f(t) \, dt, \qquad \omega \in \mathbb{R}, $$

where $\mu$ is a biquaternion root of $-1$. The inverse transform is

$$ f(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \exp\left(2\pi \mu \omega t\right) F(\omega) \, d\omega. $$

The convergence and invertibility of the continuous transform follow the same pattern as the discrete case: the kernel is invertible, but the integral may not converge if $f$ has samples of vanishing norm in a measure-theoretic sense.

Relationship to the discrete transform

The continuous transform is the limit of the discrete transform as $N \to \infty$ and the sampling interval goes to zero. The two transforms have the same structural properties: the choice of root, the factorization into complex transforms, the convolution theorem, and the vanishing-norm issue.

Higher-Dimensional Transforms

Two-dimensional transform

For a biquaternion-valued image $f : \mathbb{Z}^2 \to \mathbb{B}$, the two-dimensional biquaternion Fourier transform is

$$ F[u, v] = \sum_{m=0}^{M-1} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \exp\left(-2\pi \mu \frac{mu}{M}\right) f[m, n] \exp\left(-2\pi \mu \frac{nv}{N}\right). $$

The two kernels can be placed on the left and right of $f$, or both on the same side. The two-dimensional transform factors into four two-dimensional complex transforms, just as in the one-dimensional case.

Higher dimensions

The generalization to higher dimensions is straightforward. The transform of a biquaternion-valued function on $\mathbb{Z}^d$ is defined by $d$ kernels, and it factors into $2^d$ complex transforms (or $4$ in the two-dimensional case, more generally $2^d$ complex transforms per biquaternion component, which gives $4 \cdot 2^d$ scalars).

The Vanishing-Norm Issue

Samples of vanishing norm

A sample $f[n]$ with $N(f[n]) = 0$ is a zero divisor. Such samples have the property that they cannot be recovered from the transform: they are annihilated by the kernel (which is invertible) in a way that loses information.

More precisely, if $f[n]$ is a zero divisor, then there exists a nonzero biquaternion $\tilde{Z}$ with $f[n] \circ \tilde{Z} = 0$ or $\tilde{Z} \circ f[n] = 0$. The transform kernel is invertible, so multiplying $f[n]$ by the kernel gives another zero divisor, and the sum over $n$ may or may not be recoverable.

Consequences for the transform

  • The transform is not invertible on signals containing zero-divisor samples. The inverse transform cannot recover the vanished information.
  • The transform is invertible on signals of non-vanishing norm. If every sample has non-vanishing norm form, the transform is invertible.

Structural interpretation

The vanishing-norm issue is a genuinely biquaternionic feature. It does not arise in the complex or quaternion Fourier transforms, because the complex and quaternion algebras are division algebras: every nonzero element has an inverse. The biquaternion algebra is not a division algebra, and this is reflected in the harmonic analysis.

The issue can be avoided by restricting the signals to a subspace where the norm form is nonzero, such as the quaternion subspace $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$. On $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$, every nonzero sample is invertible, and the transform is invertible on all signals. On the anti-Hermitian subspace $\mathbb{M}_-$, the samples with vanishing norm lie on a cone, and the transform is invertible only for signals whose samples lie off this cone.

Open Questions

  1. Wavelets. Can a biquaternion wavelet transform be defined, and what are its properties? The wavelet kernel is more general than the Fourier kernel, and the biquaternion structure may introduce new phenomena.

  2. Time-frequency analysis. Can a biquaternion time-frequency distribution (Wigner, spectrogram, etc.) be defined, and what are its properties?

  3. The continuous transform on the full algebra. What is the natural domain of definition of the continuous biquaternion Fourier transform, given the zero-divisor issue?

  4. Non-commutative signal processing. What are the implications of the non-commutativity of the biquaternion product for signal processing? Convolution is not commutative, and this changes the standard filtering theory.

  5. Application to physics. The biquaternion Fourier transform may be relevant to the analysis of biquaternion-valued fields in the complexified-spacetime program, where the Fourier transform is the standard tool for analyzing field equations.
