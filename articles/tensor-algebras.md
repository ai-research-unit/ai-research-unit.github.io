
# __Tensor Algebras__

## Introduction

This article introduces the tensor product of modules and the tensor algebra built from it. The treatment is introductory and purely mathematical.

The goal is to explain what the tensor product is, how it is characterized by a universal property, how tensor powers are formed, and how the tensor algebra is built from them. The tensor algebra is the **free associative algebra** on a module: the algebra of formal products of vectors, with no relations imposed beyond bilinearity, associativity, and a unit. Imposing one relation produces the exterior algebra, the symmetric algebra, and the Clifford algebra, which are treated in this and the following articles.

We assume familiarity with modules, linear maps, and algebras, as developed in the preceding articles. The article treats the general theory over a **commutative ring** $R$. The main differences from the field case are:

- Modules need not be free, so the tensor product need not be free and need not have a well-defined rank.
- The tensor product is right exact but not left exact; flatness is the relevant condition.
- In a ring in which 2 is not invertible, the symmetric and exterior algebras must be defined as quotients of the tensor algebra, not as symmetric and antisymmetric parts of it; the two descriptions agree in characteristic 0.

We indicate where these differences matter, and specialize to a field only when we say so. This is the first of three articles treating the **tensor**, **exterior**, and **symmetric** algebras; the Clifford algebra, treated in the article *Clifford Algebras*, is a further quotient of the tensor algebra.

# Part I: The Tensor Product

## 1. Bilinear Maps and the Universal Property

Let $M, N, P$ be $R$-modules. A map $B : M \times N \to P$ is **bilinear** if it is linear in each argument separately: for all $m, m' \in M$, $n, n' \in N$, $r \in R$,

$$
B(m + m', n) = B(m, n) + B(m', n), \qquad B(m, n + n') = B(m, n) + B(m, n'),
$$

$$
B(rm, n) = r B(m, n), \qquad B(m, rn) = r B(m, n).
$$

A **tensor product** of $M$ and $N$ over $R$ is an $R$-module $M \otimes_R N$ with a bilinear map $\tau : M \times N \to M \otimes_R N$, written $\tau(m, n) = m \otimes n$, such that for every $R$-module $P$ and every bilinear $B : M \times N \to P$ there is a unique $R$-linear map $\tilde{B} : M \otimes_R N \to P$ with $\tilde{B}(m \otimes n) = B(m, n)$. Equivalently,

$$
\operatorname{Bilin}_R(M, N; P) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_R(M \otimes_R N, P),
$$

naturally in $P$. This **universal property** is the definition; it determines $M \otimes_R N$ up to a unique isomorphism, since two such modules receive mutually inverse linear maps from each other's bilinear map.

## 2. Construction and Elementary Tensors

Existence follows from a quotient construction. Let $F$ be the free $R$-module on $M \times N$ and let $K$ be the submodule generated by the elements

$$
e_{(m + m', n)} - e_{(m, n)} - e_{(m', n)}, \qquad e_{(m, n + n')} - e_{(m, n)} - e_{(m, n')},
$$

$$
e_{(rm, n)} - r e_{(m, n)}, \qquad e_{(m, rn)} - r e_{(m, n)}.
$$

Set $M \otimes_R N = F / K$ and write $m \otimes n$ for the class of $e_{(m,n)}$. The relations of $K$ are exactly those that make $\tau$ bilinear, so a bilinear $B$ vanishes on $K$ and descends to $\tilde{B}$, while uniqueness follows because the **elementary tensors** $m \otimes n$ generate $M \otimes_R N$. Every element is a finite sum $\sum_i m_i \otimes n_i$, but the elementary tensors do not form a basis and a general element has no canonical expression. Bilinearity gives the identities

$$
(m + m') \otimes n = m \otimes n + m' \otimes n, \qquad m \otimes (n + n') = m \otimes n + m \otimes n',
$$

$$
(rm) \otimes n = r(m \otimes n) = m \otimes (rn).
$$

The map $\tau$ need not be injective, and a tensor product of nonzero modules can vanish: for example $\mathbb{Q} \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} = 0$, since $kq = ns$ for suitable $s \in \mathbb{Q}$ makes $q \otimes \bar{k} = (kq) \otimes \bar{1} = s \otimes (n \bar{1}) = 0$, and $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} = 0$ because the moduli are coprime.

## 3. Functoriality

If $f : M \to M'$ and $g : N \to N'$ are $R$-linear, the bilinear map $(m, n) \mapsto f(m) \otimes g(n)$ induces an $R$-linear map

$$
f \otimes g : M \otimes_R N \to M' \otimes_R N', \qquad (f \otimes g)(m \otimes n) = f(m) \otimes g(n).
$$

It satisfies $\operatorname{id}_M \otimes \operatorname{id}_N = \operatorname{id}_{M \otimes_R N}$ and $(f' \circ f) \otimes (g' \circ g) = (f' \otimes g') \circ (f \otimes g)$, so the tensor product is a **bifunctor**.

## 4. Bases and Rank

If $M$ has basis $(e_i)_{i \in I}$ and $N$ has basis $(f_j)_{j \in J}$, then $M \otimes_R N$ has basis $(e_i \otimes f_j)_{(i,j) \in I \times J}$. In particular, free modules of finite ranks $m$ and $n$ have a tensor product free of rank $mn$, and over a field

$$
\dim_F (V \otimes_F W) = \dim_F V \cdot \dim_F W.
$$

More generally, if $M$ is free with basis $(e_i)_{i \in I}$, then $M \otimes_R N \cong \bigoplus_{i \in I} N$ via $e_i \otimes n \mapsto n$, so the tensor product distributes over arbitrary direct sums:

$$
\Big( \bigoplus_{i \in I} M_i \Big) \otimes_R N \cong \bigoplus_{i \in I} (M_i \otimes_R N).
$$

**Key difference from the field case.** Over a field every module is free and the dimension formula always applies. Over a general commutative ring the tensor product of non-free modules need not be free: for instance $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is not a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module.

## 5. Examples

- $R \otimes_R M \cong M$ and $M \otimes_R R \cong M$, via $r \otimes m \mapsto rm$ with inverse $m \mapsto 1 \otimes m$.
- $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/\gcd(m, n)\mathbb{Z}$; thus $\mathbb{Z}/2 \otimes \mathbb{Z}/2 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$ while $\mathbb{Z}/2 \otimes \mathbb{Z}/3 = 0$.
- $\mathbb{Q} \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Q} \cong \mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{Q} \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} = 0$.
- For $R^2$ with basis $x, y$ and $R^2$ with basis $x', y'$, the four tensors $x \otimes x'$, $x \otimes y'$, $y \otimes x'$, $y \otimes y'$ form a basis of $R^2 \otimes_R R^2$.
- For finite-dimensional $V, W$ over a field $F$, there is a canonical isomorphism $V^* \otimes_F W \cong \operatorname{Hom}_F(V, W)$ given by $(\varphi \otimes w)(v) = \varphi(v) w$; taking $W = V$ gives $V^* \otimes_F V \cong \operatorname{End}_F(V)$.

## 6. Exactness

If $M' \to M \to M'' \to 0$ is exact, then so is $M' \otimes_R N \to M \otimes_R N \to M'' \otimes_R N \to 0$: the functor $(-) \otimes_R N$ is **right exact**. It is not left exact: the exact sequence $0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\cdot 2} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to 0$ becomes

$$
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\ \cdot 2\ } \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to 0,
$$

whose first map is zero and so is not injective, although the original $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ was injective. An $R$-module $N$ is **flat** if $(-) \otimes_R N$ preserves injective maps; free modules are flat, and over a field every module is free, while $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, $n \geq 2$, is not flat. Two facts are used throughout: $M \otimes_R N$ is generated by elementary tensors subject only to bilinearity, and it is additive in each variable. Over a non-commutative ring the construction requires compatible one-sided structures, which we do not consider.

# Part II: Tensor Powers and the Tensor Algebra

## 7. Tensor Powers

The **$k$-th tensor power** of $M$ is defined for $k \geq 0$ by

$$
M^{\otimes 0} = R, \qquad M^{\otimes 1} = M, \qquad M^{\otimes k} = \underbrace{M \otimes_R \cdots \otimes_R M}_{k \text{ factors}} \quad (k \geq 2).
$$

The tensor product is associative and commutative up to canonical isomorphism,

$$
(M \otimes_R N) \otimes_R P \cong M \otimes_R (N \otimes_R P), \qquad M \otimes_R N \cong N \otimes_R M,
$$

given on elementary tensors by $(m \otimes n) \otimes p \mapsto m \otimes (n \otimes p)$ and $m \otimes n \mapsto n \otimes m$. These are compatible with each other and with the unit isomorphisms, so $M^{\otimes k}$ is independent of bracketing and

$$
M^{\otimes m} \otimes_R M^{\otimes n} \cong M^{\otimes (m+n)}.
$$

If $M$ is free of finite rank $n$, then $M^{\otimes k}$ is free of rank $n^k$, with basis the tensors $e_{i_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes e_{i_k}$.

## 8. The Tensor Algebra

The **tensor algebra** of $M$ is the direct sum of all tensor powers,

$$
T(M) = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} M^{\otimes k} = M^{\otimes 0} \oplus M^{\otimes 1} \oplus M^{\otimes 2} \oplus \cdots,
$$

with elements the finite sums $x_0 + x_1 + \cdots$ where $x_k \in M^{\otimes k}$. The **product** is defined on homogeneous elements $x \in M^{\otimes m}$, $y \in M^{\otimes n}$ by $x \cdot y = x \otimes y \in M^{\otimes (m+n)}$ and extended bilinearly. Associativity of the tensor product makes $T(M)$ an **associative unital $R$-algebra**, with unit $1 \in M^{\otimes 0} = R$. The inclusion $\iota : M \to T(M)$ of $M = M^{\otimes 1}$ is $R$-linear and injective, and $\iota(M)$ generates $T(M)$ as a unital algebra.

## 9. The Universal Property

**Theorem.** Let $A$ be a unital associative $R$-algebra and $f : M \to A$ an $R$-linear map. There is a unique unital algebra homomorphism $F : T(M) \to A$ with $F \circ \iota = f$.

**Proof.** On a decomposable element set

$$
F(m_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes m_k) = f(m_1) f(m_2) \cdots f(m_k)
$$

and set $F(r) = r \cdot 1_A$ on $R$. The right side is multilinear, so this extends to each $M^{\otimes k}$ and hence to $T(M)$; it is multiplicative on decomposable elements because the tensor product concatenates and $A$ is associative, and bilinearity extends this to all of $T(M)$. Uniqueness holds because $\iota(M)$ generates. $\square$

So for every unital associative $R$-algebra $A$ there is a natural bijection

$$
\operatorname{Hom}_{\text{Alg}}(T(M), A) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_R(M, A),
$$

given by restriction along $\iota$: the tensor algebra functor is **left adjoint** to the forgetful functor to $R$-modules. This is the precise sense in which $T(M)$ is the largest unital associative $R$-algebra generated by $M$ with no relations imposed. Associativity and a unit in $A$ are essential: without them the formula for $F$ presupposes a bracketing that need not be well defined.

## 10. The Grading and the Free Algebra

The decomposition $T(M) = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} T^k(M)$ with $T^k(M) = M^{\otimes k}$ is a **grading**: $T^m(M) \cdot T^n(M) \subseteq T^{m+n}(M)$. The degree-zero part is $R$, the degree-one part is $M$, and the algebra is generated in degree one. A two-sided ideal generated by homogeneous elements is graded, so quotienting by such an ideal produces a graded algebra; this is how the exterior and symmetric algebras acquire their gradings. If $A = \bigoplus_k A_k$ is graded and $f : M \to A_1$, then the homomorphism $F$ above is graded, with $F(T^k(M)) \subseteq A_k$.

Now let $M$ be free with basis indexed by a set $I$, with basis elements $x_i$. Then $T(M)$ is the **free associative $R$-algebra** on $I$,

$$
T(M) = R\langle x_i : i \in I \rangle,
$$

and the words $x_{i_1} x_{i_2} \cdots x_{i_k}$ (the empty word being the unit $1$) form an $R$-basis. If $|I| = n$ is finite, the degree-$k$ part is free of rank $n^k$, with Hilbert–Poincaré series

$$
\sum_{k \geq 0} n^k t^k = \frac{1}{1 - nt}.
$$

For $n \geq 2$ this algebra is non-commutative, since $x_1 x_2$ and $x_2 x_1$ are distinct words; for $n = 1$ it is the polynomial algebra $R[t]$, and for $n = 0$ it is $R$. If $M \neq 0$ is free, then $T(M)$ is not finitely generated as an $R$-module, and over a field it is infinite-dimensional.

## 11. Example: the Tensor Algebra on a Two-Dimensional Space

Let $M = R^2$ with basis $x, y$. Then $T(R^2) = R\langle x, y \rangle$ with grading

$$
T^0 = R, \qquad T^1 = \operatorname{span}\{x, y\}, \qquad T^2 = \operatorname{span}\{x^2, xy, yx, y^2\}, \qquad \dim_R T^k = 2^k,
$$

and $xy \neq yx$, so $T(R^2)$ is non-commutative. By the universal property, an $R$-linear map $f : R^2 \to A$ is determined by the pair $(f(x), f(y)) \in A \times A$, and every pair $(a, b)$ extends uniquely to a unital algebra homomorphism $R\langle x, y \rangle \to A$ with $x \mapsto a$ and $y \mapsto b$. Thus homomorphisms from $R\langle x, y \rangle$ are exactly ordered pairs of elements of $A$.

Different relations on the same two generators give the examples that organize the series:

- $xy - yx = 0$ gives the polynomial algebra $R[x, y]$, the symmetric algebra;
- $x^2 = 0$, $y^2 = 0$, $xy + yx = 0$ gives the exterior algebra $\Lambda(R^2)$, of rank $4$;
- $x^2 = -1$, $y^2 = -1$, $xy + yx = 0$ over $\mathbb{R}$ gives the quaternions $\mathbb{H}$, of dimension $4$.

# Part III: The Exterior and Symmetric Algebras

## 12. Quotients of the Tensor Algebra

If $I \subseteq T(M)$ is a two-sided ideal, then $T(M)/I$ is a unital associative $R$-algebra generated by the image of $M$, so a homomorphism out of it is determined by its values on $M$. Imposing **relations** on the generators means quotienting by the ideal they generate. If the ideal is generated by homogeneous elements it is graded and the quotient inherits the grading of §10. The exterior and symmetric algebras are the quotients obtained from the degree-two relations $v \otimes v = 0$ and $v \otimes w = w \otimes v$; the Clifford algebra comes from the inhomogeneous relation $v \otimes v = Q(v) \cdot 1$ and is filtered rather than graded.

## 13. The Exterior Algebra

The **exterior algebra** is

$$
\Lambda(M) = T(M) \Big/ \big( v \otimes v : v \in M \big).
$$

Its product is written $\wedge$, so the image of $m_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes m_k$ is $m_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge m_k$. The defining relation is $v \wedge v = 0$ for all $v$; substituting $v = u + w$ gives $u \wedge w + w \wedge u = 0$, hence

$$
u \wedge w = - w \wedge u
$$

for all $u, w \in M$, with no invertibility of $2$ required. The condition $v \wedge v = 0$ is the **alternating** condition; it implies antisymmetry, but the converse needs $2$ invertible, so over a ring in which $2$ is not invertible the two describe different quotients, and the exterior algebra uses the alternating one.

The ideal is generated in degree two, so $\Lambda(M) = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} \Lambda^k(M)$ with $\Lambda^0(M) = R$, $\Lambda^1(M) = M$, and $\Lambda^k(M)$ the **$k$-th exterior power**. An $R$-linear map $f : M \to A$ into a unital associative $R$-algebra with $f(v)^2 = 0$ for all $v$ extends uniquely to $\Lambda(M) \to A$; equivalently, linear maps $\Lambda^k(M) \to P$ correspond to alternating $R$-multilinear maps $M^k \to P$.

If $M$ is free with basis $(e_i)_{i \in I}$, then $\Lambda(M)$ is free with basis

$$
e_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{i_k}, \qquad i_1 < \cdots < i_k, \quad k \geq 0.
$$

For finite $|I| = n$, $\Lambda^k(M)$ has rank $\binom{n}{k}$ and

$$
\operatorname{rank} \Lambda(M) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} = 2^n,
$$

with $\Lambda^k(M) = 0$ for $k > n$ and $\Lambda^n(M)$ free of rank one. Over a field, $\dim_F \Lambda(V) = 2^n$. Thus the exterior algebra of a free module of finite rank is finite-dimensional. It is graded-commutative: for homogeneous $u, w$ of degrees $p, q$, one has $u \wedge w = (-1)^{pq} w \wedge u$. Finally, it is the Clifford algebra with zero form:

$$
\Lambda(M) = Cl(M, 0).
$$

## 14. The Symmetric Algebra

The **symmetric algebra** is

$$
\operatorname{Sym}(M) = T(M) \Big/ \big( v \otimes w - w \otimes v : v, w \in M \big).
$$

It is commutative, and graded as $\operatorname{Sym}(M) = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} \operatorname{Sym}^k(M)$, with $\operatorname{Sym}^0(M) = R$, $\operatorname{Sym}^1(M) = M$, and the image of $m_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes m_k$ written $m_1 m_2 \cdots m_k$. An $R$-linear map $f : M \to A$ into a **commutative** unital associative $R$-algebra extends uniquely to $\operatorname{Sym}(M) \to A$; equivalently, linear maps $\operatorname{Sym}^k(M) \to P$ correspond to symmetric $R$-multilinear maps $M^k \to P$.

If $M$ is free with basis $(e_i)_{i \in I}$, then $\operatorname{Sym}(M)$ is the polynomial algebra

$$
\operatorname{Sym}(M) \cong R[x_i : i \in I], \qquad x_i \leftrightarrow e_i.
$$

For finite $|I| = n$, $\operatorname{Sym}^k(M)$ is free of rank $\binom{n+k-1}{k}$, with the degree-$k$ monomials in $n$ variables as a basis, and

$$
\sum_{k \geq 0} \binom{n+k-1}{k} t^k = \frac{1}{(1-t)^n}.
$$

**Key difference from the field case.** Over a field of characteristic $0$, $\operatorname{Sym}^k(M)$ and $\Lambda^k(M)$ are the images of the symmetric and antisymmetric idempotents $\frac{1}{k!}\sum_\sigma \sigma$ and $\frac{1}{k!}\sum_\sigma \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\sigma$ on $M^{\otimes k}$. Over a general commutative ring division by $k!$ is unavailable, and the quotient definition above is the one that is correct in general.

## 15. Comparison and the One-Dimensional Case

The three algebras are the free objects, generated in degree one, of three categories, each the universal algebra receiving a linear map from $M$:

- $T(M)$, the **free unital associative algebra**, with no relation between distinct products;
- $\operatorname{Sym}(M)$, the **free commutative algebra**, with the relation $vw = wv$;
- $\Lambda(M)$, the **free graded-commutative algebra with $v^2 = 0$**, with the alternating relation.

Over a field and a free module of rank $n$, the degree-$k$ dimensions are $n^k$ for $T(M)$, $\binom{n+k-1}{k}$ for $\operatorname{Sym}(M)$, and $\binom{n}{k}$ for $\Lambda(M)$. The first two are infinite-dimensional, growing exponentially and polynomially respectively; the exterior algebra is finite-dimensional of total dimension $2^n$.

For $M = R$ with generator $t$, all tensor powers are $\cong R$, and

$$
T(R) \cong R[t], \qquad \operatorname{Sym}(R) \cong R[t], \qquad \Lambda(R) = R \oplus Rt \cong R[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2),
$$

the last being the algebra of **dual numbers**, with $\varepsilon^2 = 0$. Over $\mathbb{R}$, the same free algebra has the quadratic quotient

$$
\mathbb{C} \cong \mathbb{R}[t]/(t^2 + 1),
$$

which is the Clifford algebra $Cl(\mathbb{R}, Q)$ with $Q(t) = -1$ under the convention $v^2 = Q(v)$.

# Part IV: Tensor Products of Algebras, and the Clifford Algebra

## 16. The Tensor Product of Algebras

Let $A, B$ be unital associative $R$-algebras. Their **tensor product of algebras** is the module $A \otimes_R B$ with the product defined on elementary tensors by

$$
(a_1 \otimes b_1)(a_2 \otimes b_2) = (a_1 a_2) \otimes (b_1 b_2)
$$

and extended bilinearly. It is well defined because for fixed $(a_2, b_2)$ the map $(a_1, b_1) \mapsto a_1 a_2 \otimes b_1 b_2$ is bilinear, and the result is linear in $(a_2, b_2)$. It is associative when $A$ and $B$ are, with unit $1_A \otimes 1_B$, and commutative when both factors are. The maps $a \mapsto a \otimes 1_B$ and $b \mapsto 1_A \otimes b$ are unital algebra homomorphisms with commuting images, since

$$
(a \otimes 1_B)(1_A \otimes b) = a \otimes b = (1_A \otimes b)(a \otimes 1_B).
$$

If $A$ and $B$ are free of ranks $m$ and $n$, then $A \otimes_R B$ is free of rank $mn$. As algebras, $R \otimes_R A \cong A$, $A \otimes_R B \cong B \otimes_R A$, and $(A \otimes_R B) \otimes_R C \cong A \otimes_R (B \otimes_R C)$.

## 17. Universal Property of the Tensor Product of Algebras

**Theorem.** Let $f : A \to D$ and $g : B \to D$ be unital algebra homomorphisms into a unital associative $R$-algebra $D$ whose images commute, $f(a) g(b) = g(b) f(a)$. Then there is a unique unital algebra homomorphism $h : A \otimes_R B \to D$ with $h(a \otimes b) = f(a) g(b)$.

**Proof.** The map $(a, b) \mapsto f(a) g(b)$ is bilinear, so it induces a linear $h$. On elementary tensors it is multiplicative, since

$$
h(a_1 \otimes b_1)\, h(a_2 \otimes b_2) = f(a_1) g(b_1) f(a_2) g(b_2) = f(a_1) f(a_2) g(b_1) g(b_2) = h\big((a_1 \otimes b_1)(a_2 \otimes b_2)\big),
$$

where the middle step uses the commuting hypothesis; bilinearity extends this to all of $A \otimes_R B$. Uniqueness holds because elementary tensors generate. $\square$

In the category of **commutative** unital $R$-algebras the commuting hypothesis is automatic, so $A \otimes_R B$ is the **coproduct**:

$$
\operatorname{Hom}_{\text{CommAlg}}(A \otimes_R B, C) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\text{CommAlg}}(A, C) \times \operatorname{Hom}_{\text{CommAlg}}(B, C).
$$

**Key difference from the field case.** Over a field the tensor product of two algebras has the expected dimension, but it need not be a field or even a domain, even when both factors are fields. The coproduct in the category of all (not necessarily commutative) unital associative algebras is not the tensor product but the free product.

## 18. Example: the Complex Numbers

The tensor product of algebras makes the complex numbers an instructive example.

- $\mathbb{C} \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C} \cong \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C}$. Writing $\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}[i]/(i^2+1)$ and $u = i \otimes i$, we have $u^2 = 1$, so $e_{\pm} = \frac{1}{2}(1 \otimes 1 \pm i \otimes i)$ are orthogonal idempotents with sum $1$. The isomorphism is given by $z \otimes w \mapsto (zw, z\bar{w})$; the two factors correspond to the identity and the conjugation as maps $\mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}$. In particular the tensor product of two fields over a common subfield can have zero divisors.
- $\mathbb{C} \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{H} \cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$: the quaternion algebra becomes a matrix algebra after complexification.
- $\mathbb{C} \cong \mathbb{R}[t]/(t^2+1)$ exhibits $\mathbb{C}$ as a quadratic quotient of the free algebra $\mathbb{R}[t] = T(\mathbb{R})$, hence as a Clifford algebra.

## 19. Graded Tensor Products and the Clifford Algebra

For $\mathbb{Z}/2$-graded algebras there is a refinement. The **graded tensor product** $A \mathbin{\hat{\otimes}} B$ has the same underlying module as $A \otimes_R B$ but the product

$$
(a \otimes b)(a' \otimes b') = (-1)^{|b| |a'|} (aa') \otimes (bb')
$$

on homogeneous elements. The sign makes the construction compatible with gradings: if $M$ and $N$ are $R$-modules, then

$$
\operatorname{Sym}(M \oplus N) \cong \operatorname{Sym}(M) \otimes_R \operatorname{Sym}(N), \qquad \Lambda(M \oplus N) \cong \Lambda(M) \mathbin{\hat{\otimes}} \Lambda(N),
$$

the second because an odd element from $\Lambda(M)$ and an odd element from $\Lambda(N)$ anticommute in $\Lambda(M \oplus N)$ but would commute in the ordinary tensor product.

The **Clifford algebra** of a quadratic module $(M, Q)$ is

$$
Cl(M, Q) = T(M) \Big/ \big( v \otimes v - Q(v) \cdot 1 : v \in M \big),
$$

imposing $v^2 = Q(v) \cdot 1$. Its generator is inhomogeneous, so the ideal is not $\mathbb{Z}$-graded and $Cl(M, Q)$ is not $\mathbb{Z}$-graded; instead it is **filtered** by tensor length, and the associated graded algebra is the exterior algebra:

$$
\operatorname{gr} Cl(M, Q) \cong \Lambda(M), \qquad Cl(M, 0) = \Lambda(M).
$$

Thus $Cl(M, Q)$ is a filtered deformation of $\Lambda(M)$. It inherits from $T(M)$ the universal property that an $R$-linear $f : M \to A$ with $f(v)^2 = Q(v) \cdot 1_A$ extends uniquely to $Cl(M, Q) \to A$; with $2$ invertible this gives the fundamental relation $uv + vu = 2 B(u, v) \cdot 1$, so orthogonal vectors anticommute, exactly as in the exterior algebra. Direct sums behave as for the exterior algebra:

$$
Cl(M_1 \oplus M_2, Q) \cong Cl(M_1, Q_1) \mathbin{\hat{\otimes}} Cl(M_2, Q_2)
$$

when $Q = Q_1 + Q_2$ on $M_1 \oplus M_2$, which reduces the classification of Clifford algebras to the one-dimensional cases in the finite-rank free case, where the module decomposes as an orthogonal direct sum of one-dimensional submodules, and is developed in the article *Clifford Algebras*.

# Part V: Summary

**The tensor product** $M \otimes_R N$ is the universal target of bilinear maps, $\operatorname{Bilin}_R(M, N; P) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_R(M \otimes_R N, P)$; it is functorial, additive, right exact but not left exact, and free of rank $mn$ when $M, N$ are free of ranks $m, n$.

**The tensor algebra** $T(M) = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} M^{\otimes k}$ is the free unital associative algebra on $M$, graded and generated in degree one, with $\operatorname{Hom}_{\text{Alg}}(T(M), A) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_R(M, A)$; here $M^{\otimes 0} = R$ and $M^{\otimes m} \otimes M^{\otimes n} \cong M^{\otimes(m+n)}$.

**Quotients and the Clifford algebra.** $\operatorname{Sym}(M) = T(M)/(v \otimes w - w \otimes v)$ is the free commutative algebra, equal to $R[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ for free $M$ of rank $n$; $\Lambda(M) = T(M)/(v \otimes v)$ is the free graded-commutative algebra with $v^2 = 0$, of rank $2^n$ for free $M$ of rank $n$. The Clifford algebra $Cl(M, Q) = T(M)/(v \otimes v - Q(v) \cdot 1)$ is a filtered deformation of $\Lambda(M)$, with $\operatorname{gr} Cl(M, Q) \cong \Lambda(M)$ and $uv + vu = 2B(u, v) \cdot 1$ when $2$ is invertible.

**Tensor products of algebras** have product $(a_1 \otimes b_1)(a_2 \otimes b_2) = a_1 a_2 \otimes b_1 b_2$ and are the coproduct in the commutative category; for example $\mathbb{C} \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C} \cong \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C}$.

**Key field-case differences:** modules need not be free; the tensor product is only right exact; the exterior and symmetric algebras coincide with the antisymmetric and symmetric tensor parts only in characteristic $0$; and a tensor product of fields need not be a field.

The next articles, *Exterior Algebras* and *Symmetric Algebras*, develop the two quotients introduced here.
