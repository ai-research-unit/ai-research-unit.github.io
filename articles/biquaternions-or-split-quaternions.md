
# Biquaternion or Split Quaternion? Criteria for Choosing Between the Two Real Forms

## Introduction

This article studies the problem of choosing between the biquaternion algebra and the split quaternion algebra as the algebraic structure that contains a copy of Minkowski space. Both algebras are eight-dimensional real algebras, both contain a four-dimensional subspace with the Minkowski quadratic form, and both have a rich algebraic structure. The question is whether there are criteria that favor one over the other, and whether any of these criteria is decisive.

The treatment is purely mathematical. The physical interpretation of the algebras is not invoked, except where a criterion has a natural physical reading, in which case the physical reading is stated as a separate remark. No examples are given. The quaternion algebra $\mathbb{H}$, the biquaternion algebra $\mathbb{B}$, and the split quaternion algebra $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{D}}$ are assumed from the preceding articles, together with their conjugations, their fixed-point subspaces, and their zero divisor structures.

Every claim is either proved or stated as a definition.

## The Two Candidates

### The Biquaternion Algebra

The **biquaternion algebra** is

$$
\mathbb{B} = \mathbb{C} \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{H}.
$$

It is an eight-dimensional real algebra. It is isomorphic to the matrix algebra $M_2(\mathbb{C})$, and it is **simple**: it has no nontrivial two-sided ideals.

Its zero divisor set is a seven-dimensional complex cone. Its idempotents are generated by the roots of $-1$ via the formula $\tilde{P} = \tfrac{1}{2} e_0 \pm \tfrac{1}{2} \xi i$.

It contains a copy of Minkowski space as the anti-Hermitian subspace $\mathbb{M}_-$ (timelike direction $i e_0$, spacelike directions $e_1, e_2, e_3$) or the Hermitian subspace $\mathbb{M}_+$ (timelike direction $e_0$, spacelike directions $i e_1, i e_2, i e_3$).

### The Split Quaternion Algebra

The **split quaternion algebra** is

$$
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{D}} = \mathbb{D} \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{H}.
$$

It is an eight-dimensional real algebra. It is isomorphic to the direct sum $\mathbb{H} \oplus \mathbb{H}$, and it is **semisimple but not simple**: it has two nontrivial two-sided ideals $Z_+$ and $Z_-$.

Its zero divisor set is the union of the two four-dimensional linear subspaces $Z_+$ and $Z_-$. Its idempotents are the four elements $0, e_+, e_-, 1$, where $e_\pm = \tfrac{1}{2}(1 \pm j)$.

It contains a copy of Minkowski space as the Hermitian subspace $\mathbb{M}_+$ (timelike direction $e_0$, spacelike directions $j e_1, j e_2, j e_3$) or the anti-Hermitian subspace $\mathbb{M}_-$ (timelike direction $j e_0$, spacelike directions $e_1, e_2, e_3$).

### The Common Structure

Both algebras are **real forms** of the same complex algebra:

$$
\mathbb{B} \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C} \cong \mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{D}} \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C} \cong M_2(\mathbb{C}) \oplus M_2(\mathbb{C}).
$$

So the two algebras are different real forms of the same complexification. The biquaternion algebra is the **compact real form**, and the split quaternion algebra is the **split real form**. This is the source of all the differences between them, and it is the reason the choice between them is a choice of real form.

## Mathematical Criteria

### Simplicity

The biquaternion algebra is simple. The split quaternion algebra is semisimple but not simple.

**Criterion.** If one requires the algebra to be simple, the split quaternion algebra is eliminated.

**Remark.** Simplicity is a natural requirement in the theory of central simple algebras, in the structure theory of semisimple Lie algebras, and in contexts where one wants the algebra to admit no nontrivial quotients. In these contexts, the biquaternion algebra is the candidate.

### Semisimplicity with Idempotent Decomposition

The split quaternion algebra has a nontrivial idempotent decomposition $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{D}} = \mathbb{H} e_+ \oplus \mathbb{H} e_-$. The biquaternion algebra has no nontrivial idempotents other than the ones generated by the roots of $-1$.

**Criterion.** If one requires the algebra to have a nontrivial idempotent decomposition, the biquaternion algebra is eliminated.

**Remark.** The idempotent decomposition is a natural requirement in contexts where one wants the algebra to be a direct sum of smaller algebras, or where one wants the invertibility criterion to be linear. In these contexts, the split quaternion algebra is the candidate.

### The Zero Divisor Set

The zero divisor set of the biquaternion algebra is a seven-dimensional complex cone. The zero divisor set of the split quaternion algebra is the union of two four-dimensional linear subspaces.

**Criterion.** If one requires the zero divisor set to be a single cone, the split quaternion algebra is eliminated. If one requires the zero divisor set to be a union of linear subspaces, the biquaternion algebra is eliminated.

**Remark.** In the complexified-spacetime program, the zero divisor cone of the biquaternion algebra is natural. In the split-signature program, the union of linear subspaces of the split quaternion algebra is natural. The choice depends on the context.

### The Two-Dimensional Fixed-Point Subspace

The biquaternion algebra has a two-dimensional fixed-point subspace $\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{B}}$ that is a copy of the complex field, a division algebra. The split quaternion algebra has a two-dimensional fixed-point subspace $\mathbb{D}_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{D}}}$ that is a copy of the split complex algebra, which is not a division algebra.

**Criterion.** If one requires the two-dimensional subspace to be a division algebra, the split quaternion algebra is eliminated. If one requires the two-dimensional subspace to be the split complex algebra, the biquaternion algebra is eliminated.

**Remark.** The complex field is natural in contexts where one wants the algebra to contain a copy of $\mathbb{C}$ as a subalgebra. The split complex algebra is natural in contexts where one wants the algebra to contain idempotents in the scalar part.

### The Roots of Minus One

The biquaternion algebra has a four-dimensional family of roots of $-1$, including a central root $\pm i$. The split quaternion algebra has a four-dimensional family of roots of $-1$, but no central root.

**Criterion.** If one requires the algebra to have a central root of $-1$, the split quaternion algebra is eliminated. If one requires the algebra to have no central root of $-1$, the biquaternion algebra is eliminated.

**Remark.** The central root $i$ is the extra unit that distinguishes the biquaternion algebra from the quaternion algebra. In the split quaternion algebra, the extra unit $j$ satisfies $j^2 = +1$, so there is no central root of $-1$. The choice depends on whether one wants the extra unit to be a square root of $-1$ or a square root of $+1$.

### The Real Form

The biquaternion algebra is the compact real form of the complexified algebra. The split quaternion algebra is the split real form.

**Criterion.** If one requires the algebra to be the compact real form, the split quaternion algebra is eliminated. If one requires the algebra to be the split real form, the biquaternion algebra is eliminated.

**Remark.** In the theory of real forms of complex algebras, the compact form and the split form are the two extremes. The choice depends on the context. In the theory of Lie groups, the compact form gives rise to compact groups, and the split form gives rise to non-compact groups.

## Physical Criteria

The mathematical criteria have natural physical readings, and the physical readings are stated here as separate remarks. The physical readings are not part of the mathematical content of the article; they are interpretations that may be useful in applications.

### The Signature of the Embedded Minkowski Space

In the biquaternion algebra, the standard embedding of Minkowski space is in the anti-Hermitian subspace $\mathbb{M}_-$, with the timelike direction given by the imaginary scalar part $i e_0$. In the split quaternion algebra, the standard embedding is in the Hermitian subspace $\mathbb{M}_+$, with the timelike direction given by the real scalar part $e_0$.

**Criterion.** If one requires the timelike direction to be imaginary, the split quaternion algebra is eliminated. If one requires the timelike direction to be real, the biquaternion algebra is eliminated.

### The Nature of the Extra Dimensions

In the biquaternion algebra, the extra dimensions beyond Minkowski space are the real scalar part and the real vector part (in the anti-Hermitian embedding). In the split quaternion algebra, the extra dimensions are the split-imaginary scalar part and the split-imaginary vector part (in the Hermitian embedding).

**Criterion.** If one requires the extra dimensions to be real, the split quaternion algebra is eliminated. If one requires the extra dimensions to be split-imaginary, the biquaternion algebra is eliminated.

### The Lorentz Group Action

In the biquaternion algebra, the Lorentz group acts by conjugation by unit elements of $\mathbb{B}$, and the spin group is a subgroup of the simple algebra. In the split quaternion algebra, the Lorentz group acts by conjugation by unit elements of $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{D}}$, and the spin group acts on the two idempotent components independently.

**Criterion.** If one requires the spin group to be a subgroup of a simple algebra, the split quaternion algebra is eliminated. If one requires the spin group to act on the two components independently, the biquaternion algebra is eliminated.

## The Decisive Question

The decisive question is: **which real form is the physically relevant one?** This is not a mathematical question, because both real forms are mathematically consistent. It is a question about which structure is realized in nature, and it can only be answered by comparing the predictions of the two candidates with experiment.

The mathematical criteria provide a framework in which the question can be posed precisely. They do not provide the answer.

There is, however, a mathematical asymmetry that is worth noting. The biquaternion algebra is the **unique** simple real form of the complexified algebra, while the split quaternion algebra is the **unique** split real form. In the classification of real forms, the simple form and the split form are the two extremes, and no other real form exists. So the choice between the two candidates is a binary choice: either the algebra is the simple real form, or it is the split real form.

This binary structure is the reason the question is well-posed. It is also the reason the answer, if it exists, is a single bit of information.

## The Role of the Clifford Algebra

The two algebras are the even subalgebras of the Clifford algebras of four-dimensional quadratic spaces:

- The biquaternion algebra is the even subalgebra of the Lorentzian Clifford algebras $\mathrm{Cl}_{3,1}$ and $\mathrm{Cl}_{1,3}$.
- The split quaternion algebra is the even subalgebra of the definite Clifford algebra $\mathrm{Cl}_{4,0}$, the split Clifford algebra $\mathrm{Cl}_{2,2}$, and the anti-definite Clifford algebra $\mathrm{Cl}_{0,4}$.

So the choice between the two algebras is equivalent to the choice between the Lorentzian signature and the other signatures for the underlying quadratic space. If the underlying space is Minkowski space, the even subalgebra is the biquaternion algebra. If the underlying space is Euclidean or split, the even subalgebra is the split quaternion algebra.

This is the cleanest formulation of the choice: the algebra is determined by the signature of the underlying quadratic space, and the signature is determined by the physics.

## Summary

The biquaternion and split quaternion algebras are different real forms of the same complexified algebra. They differ in their simplicity, their zero divisor structure, their idempotent decomposition, the nature of their two-dimensional fixed-point subspace, the presence of a central root of $-1$, and their position in the classification of real forms.

Each of these differences is a possible criterion for elimination. The mathematical criteria are sharp, and they eliminate one of the two candidates in each case. But no single criterion is decisive, because the two algebras are both mathematically consistent, and the choice between them is a choice of which structure is realized in nature.

The decisive question is physical, and it can only be answered by experiment. The mathematics provides the framework, and the framework is the classification of real forms of the complexified algebra. The answer, if it exists, is a single bit: compact or split.
