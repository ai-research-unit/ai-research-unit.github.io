
# __Fields categorization__

## Introduction

This article classifies fields by their structural properties. We treat the characteristic and the prime field, cardinality, the closure properties (algebraically closed, real closed, separably closed), perfection and inseparability, orderability, and the valuation-theoretic properties (valued, complete, local), together with the division into number fields and function fields. For each class we state the defining property, give concrete examples and non-examples, and place the class in the hierarchy.

The general theory is in the companion article *Fields*, and the ring-theoretic background in *Rings categorization*. A **field** is a commutative ring $F$ with $1 \neq 0$ in which every nonzero element is a unit. The rings with zero divisors of *Rings categorization* — the split complex numbers $\mathbb{D}$, the dual numbers, and the biquaternions $\mathbb{B} = \mathbb{H} \otimes_\mathbb{R} \mathbb{C}$ — are not fields and do not appear below.

The standard examples are $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$; the prime field $\mathbb{F}_p = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ and the finite fields $\mathbb{F}_q$ with $q = p^n$; the function fields $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$ and $\mathbb{Q}(t)$; the local fields $\mathbb{Q}_p$ and $\mathbb{F}_q((t))$; and the algebraic closure $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$.

# Part I: Characteristic and the Prime Field

## 1. The Prime Field

Let $F$ be a field. There is a unique ring homomorphism

$$
\varphi : \mathbb{Z} \to F, \qquad \varphi(n) = n \cdot 1_F .
$$

The subfield generated by its image is the smallest subfield of $F$, called the **prime field**; it equals the subfield generated by $1_F$ and the intersection of all subfields of $F$.

The kernel of $\varphi$ is a prime ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$, hence is $(0)$ or $(p\mathbb{Z})$ for a prime $p$. This gives the fundamental dichotomy:

- if $\ker \varphi = (0)$, the prime field is $\mathbb{Q}$, and $F$ has **characteristic $0$**;
- if $\ker \varphi = (p\mathbb{Z})$, the prime field is $\mathbb{F}_p$, and $F$ has **characteristic $p$**.

Thus the prime field of any field is $\mathbb{Q}$ or $\mathbb{F}_p$, with no third possibility, and every field is an extension of its prime field.

## 2. Characteristic Zero

A field of characteristic $0$ contains a copy of $\mathbb{Q}$ as its prime field, and is therefore infinite.

**Examples.** $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$; every number field, such as $\mathbb{Q}(i)$ and $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$; the $p$-adic fields $\mathbb{Q}_p$; every function field over a characteristic-zero field, such as $\mathbb{Q}(t)$ and $\mathbb{C}(t)$; and the algebraic closure $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$.

**Properties.** Every field of characteristic $0$ is infinite and, as shown in §12, perfect. Ordered fields in particular have characteristic $0$ (§8).

## 3. Prime Characteristic and the Frobenius Endomorphism

A field of characteristic $p$ contains a copy of $\mathbb{F}_p$ as its prime field and may be finite or infinite.

**Examples.** The finite fields $\mathbb{F}_p$ and $\mathbb{F}_q$; the infinite fields $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$, $\mathbb{F}_p((t))$, and $\overline{\mathbb{F}_p}$.

The **Frobenius endomorphism** is

$$
\operatorname{Frob} : F \to F, \qquad x \mapsto x^p .
$$

It is a field endomorphism: multiplicative by definition, and additive because the binomial coefficients $\binom{p}{k}$ with $0 < k < p$ are divisible by $p$. It fixes $\mathbb{F}_p$ and is injective. It is bijective precisely when $F = F^p$, that is, when every element is a $p$-th power; this characterises the perfect fields of characteristic $p$ (§12). No field of characteristic $p$ is orderable (§8).

# Part II: Finite and Infinite Fields

## 4. Finite Fields

A field is **finite** if it has finitely many elements.

**Classification.** A finite field has prime field $\mathbb{F}_p$ and is a finite-dimensional vector space over it, so $|F| = p^n$ for some $n \geq 1$. Conversely, for every prime power $q = p^n$ there is exactly one field with $q$ elements up to isomorphism, denoted $\mathbb{F}_q$; it is the splitting field of $X^q - X$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$. Hence the finite fields are exactly the fields $\mathbb{F}_q$ with $q$ a prime power.

**Structure.** The group $\mathbb{F}_q^\times$ is cyclic of order $q - 1$. The subfields of $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ are exactly the fields $\mathbb{F}_{p^d}$ with $d \mid n$. Frobenius is an automorphism of $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$, and $\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{F}_{p^n}/\mathbb{F}_p)$ is cyclic of order $n$, generated by Frobenius.

**Examples.** $\mathbb{F}_2 = \{0, 1\}$; $\mathbb{F}_4 = \mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^2 - x - 1)$; $\mathbb{F}_8 = \mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^3 - x - 1)$; $\mathbb{F}_9 = \mathbb{F}_3[x]/(x^2 - 2)$.

**Properties.** Every finite field is perfect, since Frobenius is an injective self-map of a finite set. No finite field is algebraically closed, since $\mathbb{F}_q$ has an extension $\mathbb{F}_{q^m}$ of every degree $m \geq 2$. No finite field is orderable, since it has characteristic $p$.

**Non-examples.** The ring $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ with $n$ composite is not a field, and the polynomial ring $\mathbb{F}_p[t]$ is infinite and is not a field.

## 5. Infinite Fields and Wedderburn's Little Theorem

Characteristic-zero fields are infinite; characteristic-$p$ fields may be either. Infinite fields may be countably infinite ($\mathbb{Q}$, $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$, every number field, $\mathbb{Q}(t)$, $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$, the real algebraic numbers) or uncountable ($\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{Q}_p$, $\mathbb{F}_p((t))$, $\mathbb{C}(t)$).

**Theorem (Wedderburn).** Every finite division ring is a field. In particular a noncommutative division ring such as $\mathbb{H}$ is infinite, and the classification of finite fields classifies all finite division rings.

# Part III: Algebraic and Transcendental Extensions

## 6. Algebraic and Transcendental Elements

Let $K \subseteq F$ be a field extension. An element $\alpha \in F$ is **algebraic over $K$** if it is a root of a nonzero polynomial in $K[X]$, and **transcendental** otherwise; the **minimal polynomial** of an algebraic $\alpha$ is the unique monic irreducible polynomial of least degree having $\alpha$ as a root. The extension $K(\alpha)/K$ is finite if and only if $\alpha$ is algebraic, with degree equal to that of the minimal polynomial. An extension is **algebraic** if all its elements are algebraic. Every finitely generated extension has a **transcendence basis**, whose cardinality is the **transcendence degree**; an extension is algebraic exactly when this degree is $0$.

**Examples.** $\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}$ is algebraic of degree $2$; $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q}$ is algebraic; $\mathbb{Q}(t)/\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{F}_p(t)/\mathbb{F}_p$ have transcendence degree $1$; and $\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{Q}$ has transcendence degree $2^{\aleph_0}$.

## 7. Algebraically Closed Fields and Algebraic Closure

A field $F$ is **algebraically closed** if every nonconstant polynomial in $F[X]$ has a root in $F$; equivalently, every irreducible polynomial over $F$ has degree $1$, equivalently $F$ has no proper algebraic extension.

**Examples.** $\mathbb{C}$, by the fundamental theorem of algebra; $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$; and $\overline{\mathbb{F}_p}$.

**Non-examples.** $\mathbb{R}$ ($X^2 = -1$ has no real root); $\mathbb{Q}$ ($X^2 = 2$ has no rational root); every finite field; every ordered or real closed field; $\mathbb{Q}_p$; $\mathbb{F}_p((t))$; and every number field or function field.

**Properties.** Every algebraically closed field is infinite, perfect, and separably closed. Cardinalities differ: $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ and $\overline{\mathbb{F}_p}$ are countable, while $\mathbb{C}$ is uncountable. By the **Steinitz theorem**, two algebraically closed fields are isomorphic if and only if they have the same characteristic and the same transcendence degree over their prime field.

**Algebraic closure.** Every field $F$ has an **algebraic closure** $\overline{F}$: an algebraic extension of $F$ that is algebraically closed, unique up to an isomorphism fixing $F$, and $F$ is algebraically closed if and only if $F = \overline{F}$. For example $\overline{\mathbb{R}} = \mathbb{C}$, and $\overline{\mathbb{F}_q} = \overline{\mathbb{F}_p}$, which is the union of the fields $\mathbb{F}_{q^m}$ for $m \geq 1$ and is countably infinite.

# Part IV: Ordered and Real Closed Fields

## 8. Ordered and Formally Real Fields

A field $F$ is **ordered** if it carries a total order compatible with the field operations: adding the same element to both sides of an inequality preserves it, and multiplying both sides by a positive element preserves it.

In an ordered field, squares are nonnegative, so $1 > 0$ and every sum of squares is nonnegative; hence $-1$ is not a sum of squares, and the characteristic is $0$. Thus every ordered field is infinite with prime field $\mathbb{Q}$.

A field is **formally real** if $-1$ is not a sum of squares. By the **Artin–Schreier theorem**, a field is orderable if and only if it is formally real, so the orderable fields are exactly the formally real fields.

**Examples.** $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, and every subfield of $\mathbb{R}$, such as $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ and the real algebraic numbers; and the rational function fields $\mathbb{Q}(t)$ and $\mathbb{R}(t)$, ordered so that $t$ is larger than every rational number.

**Non-examples.** $\mathbb{C}$ and $\mathbb{Q}(i)$, since $-1 = i^2$ is a square and hence a sum of squares; every field of characteristic $p$, including finite fields and $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$; the $p$-adic fields $\mathbb{Q}_p$, in which $-1$ is a sum of squares; and $\mathbb{C}(t)$, which contains $\mathbb{C}$.

A formally real field may carry several orderings: $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ has exactly two, according to the sign chosen for $\sqrt{2}$.

## 9. Real Closed Fields

A field $F$ is **real closed** if it is formally real and no proper algebraic extension of $F$ is formally real. The following conditions are equivalent:

- $F$ is formally real and has no proper formally real algebraic extension;
- $F$ is not algebraically closed and $F(i)$ is algebraically closed, where $i^2 = -1$;
- $F$ admits an ordering in which every positive element is a square and every polynomial of odd degree has a root in $F$.

The equivalence of the first two is the **Artin–Schreier theorem**; the third is the characterisation of real closed fields by squares and odd-degree polynomials. A real closed field has a unique ordering, and its positive elements are exactly its nonzero squares.

**Properties.** Every real closed field has characteristic $0$, is infinite and perfect, is ordered, and is not algebraically closed; its algebraic closure is $F(i)$. Every formally real field $F$ has a **real closure**: an algebraic extension of $F$ that is real closed with an ordering extending that of $F$, unique up to an isomorphism fixing $F$.

**Examples.** $\mathbb{R}$; the field $\overline{\mathbb{Q}} \cap \mathbb{R}$ of real algebraic numbers, which is the real closure of $\mathbb{Q}$; and the real closure of any ordered field, for instance of $\mathbb{Q}(t)$.

**Non-examples.** $\mathbb{Q}$, because $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ is a proper formally real algebraic extension; $\mathbb{C}$; and $\mathbb{Q}(t)$, which is formally real but has the positive non-square $t$ under the ordering in which $t$ is positive.

## 10. Algebraically Closed versus Real Closed

The two notions are complementary: $\mathbb{R}$ is real closed and not algebraically closed, with algebraic closure $\mathbb{C}$, while $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed and not real closed, indeed not formally real. A field $F$ is real closed if and only if it is not algebraically closed and $F(i)$ is algebraically closed, so no field is both.

# Part V: Perfection, Separability, and Inseparability

## 11. Separable and Purely Inseparable Extensions

Let $E/K$ be algebraic. An element $\alpha \in E$ is **separable over $K$** if its minimal polynomial over $K$ has no repeated roots, equivalently is coprime to its formal derivative; the extension is separable if all its elements are. In characteristic $0$ every algebraic extension is separable, and an irreducible polynomial over a field of characteristic $p$ is inseparable if and only if it is a polynomial in $X^p$.

An element $\alpha$ is **purely inseparable over $K$** (characteristic $p$) if $\alpha^{p^e} \in K$ for some $e \geq 0$, equivalently if its minimal polynomial is of the form $X^{p^e} - a$. A finite purely inseparable extension has degree a power of $p$. Every finite extension $E/K$ has a unique intermediate field $K_s$ with $K_s/K$ separable and $E/K_s$ purely inseparable, so every finite extension factors into a separable part followed by a purely inseparable part.

## 12. Perfect Fields

A field $K$ is **perfect** if every algebraic extension of $K$ is separable, equivalently if every irreducible polynomial over $K$ is separable.

**Theorem.** $K$ is perfect if and only if either $K$ has characteristic $0$, or $K$ has characteristic $p$ and $K = K^p$ (Frobenius surjective).

**Examples.** Every field of characteristic $0$, including $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, every number field, $\mathbb{Q}_p$, $\mathbb{Q}(t)$, and $\mathbb{C}(t)$; every finite field; every algebraically closed field; and every field algebraic over a perfect field, such as the real algebraic numbers.

**Non-examples.** $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$, since $t$ is not a $p$-th power; every function field over a finite field, a finite extension of the imperfect field $\mathbb{F}_q(t)$; and $\mathbb{F}_q((t))$, since a $p$-th root of $t$ would have valuation $1/p$.

Every field $K$ has a **perfect closure** $K^{p^{-\infty}}$: a purely inseparable extension of $K$ that is perfect and minimal with this property, unique up to an isomorphism fixing $K$.

## 13. Purely Inseparably Closed Fields

A field $K$ is **purely inseparably closed** if it has no proper purely inseparable algebraic extension.

**Theorem.** A field is purely inseparably closed if and only if it is perfect.

If $K$ is perfect, every algebraic extension of $K$ is separable, so none is purely inseparable. Conversely, if $K$ is not perfect, then in characteristic $p$ some $a \in K$ is not a $p$-th power, and $K(a^{1/p})$ is a proper purely inseparable extension of $K$. Thus the classes "perfect" and "purely inseparably closed" coincide in characteristic $p$, and in characteristic $0$ the condition is automatic.

## 14. Separably Closed Fields

A field $K$ is **separably closed** if it has no proper separable algebraic extension, equivalently if $K = K^{\mathrm{sep}}$, its separable closure.

**Examples.** Every algebraically closed field. In characteristic $0$, separably closed is the same as algebraically closed, because every algebraic extension in characteristic $0$ is separable. In characteristic $p$, the separable closure $\mathbb{F}_p(t)^{\mathrm{sep}}$ of $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$ is separably closed but not algebraically closed.

**Properties.** Every separably closed field is infinite. A field is algebraically closed if and only if it is separably closed and perfect, equivalently separably closed and purely inseparably closed. A separably closed field of characteristic $p$ need not be perfect: $\mathbb{F}_p(t)^{\mathrm{sep}}$ is imperfect, since it contains $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$ with the non-$p$-th-power $t$, and a separable extension cannot supply a $p$-th root of $t$. Its algebraic closure is purely inseparable over it. Every field has a separable closure, unique up to an isomorphism fixing $K$.

# Part VI: Valued Fields, Local Fields, and Global Fields

## 15. Valued Fields

A **valuation** on a field $K$ is a function $v : K \to \Gamma \cup \{\infty\}$, where $\Gamma$ is a totally ordered abelian group written additively, such that $v(x) = \infty$ if and only if $x = 0$; $v(xy)$ is the value-group sum of $v(x)$ and $v(y)$; and $v(x - y) \geq \min(v(x), v(y))$, the **ultrametric inequality**. The **valuation ring** $\mathcal{O}_v = \{x \in K : v(x) \geq 0\}$ is local, with unique maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}_v = \{x \in K : v(x) > 0\}$; its **residue field** is $k_v = \mathcal{O}_v/\mathfrak{m}_v$ and its **value group** is $\Gamma_v = v(K^\times)$. A valuation ring is integrally closed in its field of fractions.

**Examples.** The **trivial valuation**, with valuation ring $K$; the **$p$-adic valuation** $v_p$ on $\mathbb{Q}$, with value group $\mathbb{Z}$ and residue field $\mathbb{F}_p$; and the **$t$-adic valuation** on $k(t)$ and on $k((t))$, with value group $\mathbb{Z}$ and residue field $k$.

The archimedean absolute value on $\mathbb{R}$ is not a valuation in this sense, since it satisfies the triangle inequality; the local fields below are defined using absolute values and so include $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$.

## 16. Completions and Local Fields

A valued field is **complete** if every Cauchy sequence converges; every valued field has a completion, unique up to isomorphism, with the same value group and the same residue field.

A **local field** is a field with a nontrivial absolute value that is locally compact for the induced metric; equivalently, in the non-archimedean case, a field complete with respect to a discrete valuation with finite residue field. Every local field is one of: $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$ (archimedean); a finite extension of $\mathbb{Q}_p$ (characteristic $0$); or one of the fields $\mathbb{F}_q((t))$, that is, a finite extension of $\mathbb{F}_p((t))$ (characteristic $p$).

**Properties.** Every local field is a complete valued field; a finite extension of a local field is local; the only algebraically closed local field is $\mathbb{C}$; characteristic-$0$ local fields are perfect and characteristic-$p$ ones are imperfect; and every non-archimedean local field has value group $\mathbb{Z}$ and finite residue field.

**Examples.** $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{Q}_p$, $\mathbb{F}_q((t))$, and finite extensions such as the unramified extension of $\mathbb{Q}_p$ of degree $n$, whose residue field is $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$.

**Non-examples.** $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$ are not complete, so they are not local. $\mathbb{C}((t))$ is complete and discretely valued but has the infinite residue field $\mathbb{C}$, so it is not a local field. Number fields, function fields, $\mathbb{Q}(t)$, and $\mathbb{C}(t)$ are not complete for any nontrivial valuation.

## 17. Number Fields and Function Fields

A **number field** is a finite extension of $\mathbb{Q}$, of **degree** equal to its dimension over $\mathbb{Q}$; examples are $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{Q}(i)$, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})$, and the cyclotomic fields $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$. It has characteristic $0$, is perfect, countably infinite, not algebraically closed, and not local; it is formally real exactly when it embeds in $\mathbb{R}$, so $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ is formally real while $\mathbb{Q}(i)$ is not. Its completions at its places are $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, or finite extensions of $\mathbb{Q}_p$, hence local fields.

A **function field** in one variable over a field $k$ is a finitely generated extension of $k$ of transcendence degree $1$, equivalently a finite extension of $k(t)$; examples are $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$, $\mathbb{Q}(t)$, $\mathbb{R}(t)$, $\mathbb{C}(t)$, and finite extensions of $\mathbb{F}_q(t)$. A function field over a finite field has characteristic $p$, is countably infinite, and is imperfect, being a finite extension of the imperfect field $\mathbb{F}_q(t)$; it is neither algebraically closed nor formally real. A function field of characteristic $0$ is perfect, and $\mathbb{Q}(t)$ and $\mathbb{R}(t)$ are formally real while $\mathbb{C}(t)$ is not. A function field is not local; over a finite field its completions at its places are the local fields $\mathbb{F}_q((t))$.

## 18. Global Fields

A **global field** is a number field or a function field in one variable over a finite field. By **Ostrowski's theorem**, the nontrivial absolute values on $\mathbb{Q}$ are, up to equivalence, the archimedean absolute value and the $p$-adic absolute values. The completions of a global field at its places are local fields. A global field is not itself a local field and is not complete with respect to any nontrivial absolute value.

# Part VII: Summary and Hierarchy

## 19. The Hierarchies

Every field is an extension of its prime field, which is $\mathbb{Q}$ in characteristic $0$ and $\mathbb{F}_p$ in characteristic $p$; the two branches are mutually exclusive. Fields of characteristic $0$ are infinite and perfect, and may or may not be formally real. Fields of characteristic $p$ are never orderable, and are perfect precisely when Frobenius is surjective.

**Characteristic and ordering:**

$$
\{\text{real closed}\} \subsetneq \{\text{formally real}\} = \{\text{orderable}\} \subsetneq \{\text{characteristic } 0\},
$$

$$
\{\text{finite fields}\} \subsetneq \{\text{characteristic } p\}.
$$

**Closure and perfection:**

$$
\{\text{algebraically closed}\} = \{\text{separably closed}\} \cap \{\text{perfect}\},
$$

$$
\{\text{algebraically closed}\} \subsetneq \{\text{perfect}\} \supsetneq \{\text{finite fields}\}.
$$

**Valuation:**

$$
\{\text{local fields}\} \subsetneq \{\text{complete valued fields}\} \subsetneq \{\text{valued fields}\}.
$$

Each inclusion is strict: $\mathbb{Q}$ is formally real but not real closed, $\mathbb{Q}(i)$ has characteristic $0$ but is not formally real, $\mathbb{C}((t))$ is complete and discretely valued but not local, and $\mathbb{Q}(t)$ is valued but not complete.

## 21. Standard Examples in the Hierarchy

$\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{F}_p$ are the prime fields. The finite fields $\mathbb{F}_q$ are perfect and not algebraically closed; the infinite perfect fields include $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, and the number fields, while the infinite imperfect fields include $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$, its finite extensions, and $\mathbb{F}_q((t))$. The algebraically closed fields are $\mathbb{C}$, $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$, and $\overline{\mathbb{F}_p}$; the real closed fields are $\mathbb{R}$ and the real algebraic numbers. The orderable fields include $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, $\mathbb{Q}(t)$, and $\mathbb{R}(t)$, while $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{Q}(i)$, $\mathbb{Q}_p$, and $\mathbb{C}(t)$ are not orderable. The local fields are $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{Q}_p$, and $\mathbb{F}_q((t))$; the global fields are the number fields and the function fields in one variable over finite fields.
