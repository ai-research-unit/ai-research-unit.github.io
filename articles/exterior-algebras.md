# __Exterior Algebras__

## Introduction

This article introduces the exterior powers and the exterior algebra of a module, over a **commutative ring** $R$. The treatment is introductory and purely mathematical.

The exterior algebra is the quotient of the tensor algebra by the single relation $v \otimes v = 0$; it is the free graded-commutative algebra with $v^2 = 0$, and it is the Clifford algebra of the zero quadratic form, $\Lambda(M) = Cl(M, 0)$. We explain its universal property, its grading, its bases, its behaviour under direct sums, the determinant as the induced map on the top exterior power, and its relation to alternating forms, to differential forms, and to the Clifford algebra. We assume familiarity with modules, linear maps, algebras, and the tensor algebra, in particular the article *Tensor Algebras*.

The main differences from the field case are:

- Modules need not be free, so the exterior algebra need not be free and need not have a well-defined rank.
- The **alternating** condition, vanishing when two arguments are equal, and the **antisymmetric** condition, sign change under a transposition, are equivalent when $2$ is invertible in $R$, and differ otherwise; the exterior algebra uses the alternating condition.
- Alternating multilinear forms are the natural dual objects, and the determinant is a one-dimensional phenomenon: it exists for an endomorphism of a free module of finite rank, and invertibility over a ring means that the determinant is a unit.

We specialize to a field only when we say so.

# Part I: Exterior Powers

## 1. Alternating Multilinear Maps

Let $M, P$ be $R$-modules and $k \geq 1$. A map $f : M^k \to P$ is **multilinear** if it is linear in each argument separately. It is **alternating** if
$$
f(m_1, \dots, m_k) = 0
$$
whenever two adjacent arguments are equal, and **antisymmetric** if
$$
f(m_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, m_{\sigma(k)}) = \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) f(m_1, \dots, m_k)
$$
for every permutation $\sigma$.

**Proposition.** An alternating multilinear map is antisymmetric. Conversely, an antisymmetric multilinear map is alternating when $2$ is invertible in $R$.

**Proof.** Fix all arguments except two adjacent slots, giving a bilinear $g$ with
$$
g(x + y, x + y) = g(x, x) + g(x, y) + g(y, x) + g(y, y) = 0.
$$
If $f$ is alternating, the outer terms vanish, so $g(x, y) = - g(y, x)$, and $f$ changes sign under an adjacent transposition; since transpositions generate the symmetric group, $f$ is antisymmetric. If instead $f$ is antisymmetric and two arguments are equal, transposing them leaves the tuple fixed and multiplies the value by $-1$, so $f = -f$, which gives $f = 0$ when $2$ is invertible. $\square$

Hence an alternating map vanishes whenever any two arguments are equal, since a permutation may bring them adjacent.

**Key difference from the field case.** Over a field of characteristic not $2$ the two conditions coincide, and most texts use them interchangeably; over a general commutative ring they do not, and the exterior algebra is built from the alternating condition. This is the same distinction noted in the article *Tensor Algebras* between $v \otimes v = 0$ and $u \otimes w + w \otimes u = 0$.

## 2. The Universal Property

An **exterior power** of order $k$ of $M$ is an $R$-module $\Lambda^k(M)$ with an alternating multilinear map
$$
\omega : M^k \to \Lambda^k(M), \qquad \omega(m_1, \dots, m_k) = m_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge m_k,
$$
such that for every $R$-module $P$ and every alternating multilinear $f : M^k \to P$ there is a unique $R$-linear $\tilde{f} : \Lambda^k(M) \to P$ with $\tilde{f}(m_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge m_k) = f(m_1, \dots, m_k)$. Equivalently,
$$
\operatorname{Alt}^k(M; P) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_R\big(\Lambda^k(M), P\big)
$$
naturally in $P$, where $\operatorname{Alt}^k(M; P)$ is the module of alternating multilinear maps $M^k \to P$. This universal property determines $\Lambda^k(M)$ up to a unique isomorphism. We set $\Lambda^0(M) = R$ and $\Lambda^1(M) = M$.

## 3. Construction and Elementary Wedges

Let $\Gamma^k \subseteq M^{\otimes k}$ be the submodule generated by the decomposable tensors $x_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes x_k$ in which two adjacent factors are equal, and set
$$
\Lambda^k(M) = M^{\otimes k} / \Gamma^k,
$$
writing $m_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge m_k$ for the class of $m_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes m_k$. An alternating multilinear $f$ vanishes on $\Gamma^k$ and descends to $\tilde{f}$, while uniqueness holds because the elementary wedges generate $\Lambda^k(M)$. The relations say that $m_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge m_k = 0$ whenever two adjacent arguments are equal, hence whenever any two are equal. Bilinearity gives
$$
(m + m') \wedge n = m \wedge n + m' \wedge n, \qquad m \wedge (n + n') = m \wedge n + m \wedge n',
$$
$$
(rm) \wedge n = r(m \wedge n) = m \wedge (rn),
$$
and $m \wedge n = - n \wedge m$, since $0 = (m + n) \wedge (m + n) = m \wedge m + m \wedge n + n \wedge m + n \wedge n$. Every element of $\Lambda^k(M)$ is a finite sum of elementary wedges, but these do not form a basis and a general element has no canonical expression.

The **exterior algebra** is
$$
\Lambda(M) = T(M) \Big/ \big( v \otimes v : v \in M \big),
$$
the quotient of the tensor algebra by the two-sided ideal generated by all $v \otimes v$. That generator is homogeneous of degree $2$, so the ideal is graded, with degree-$k$ part $\Gamma^k$, and
$$
\Lambda(M) = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} \Lambda^k(M).
$$

## 4. Functoriality

An $R$-linear map $f : M \to N$ makes $(m_1, \dots, m_k) \mapsto f(m_1) \wedge \cdots \wedge f(m_k)$ alternating multilinear, so it induces a unique $R$-linear map
$$
\Lambda^k(f) : \Lambda^k(M) \to \Lambda^k(N), \qquad \Lambda^k(f)(m_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge m_k) = f(m_1) \wedge \cdots \wedge f(m_k),
$$
with $\Lambda^k(\operatorname{id}_M) = \operatorname{id}$ and $\Lambda^k(g \circ f) = \Lambda^k(g) \circ \Lambda^k(f)$. Summing over $k$ gives unital algebra homomorphisms $\Lambda(f) : \Lambda(M) \to \Lambda(N)$, so $\Lambda$ is a functor from $R$-modules to graded unital $R$-algebras; an invertible $f$ induces an algebra automorphism.

## 5. Bases, Rank, and Dimension

**Theorem.** Let $M$ be free with basis $(e_i)_{i \in I}$. Then $\Lambda^k(M)$ is free with basis the wedges
$$
e_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{i_k}, \qquad i_1 < \cdots < i_k,
$$
and $\Lambda(M)$ is free with basis these elements for all $k \geq 0$, the empty product being the unit.

**Proof sketch.** Spanning: a wedge of basis elements can be sorted by $e_i \wedge e_j = - e_j \wedge e_i$, and a repeated index gives $e_i \wedge e_i = 0$, so each wedge is $0$ or $\pm$ a wedge with strictly increasing indices. Independence: for strictly increasing $I = (i_1, \dots, i_k)$, the alternating multilinear form
$$
\varphi_I(x_1, \dots, x_k) = \det\big(\varepsilon^{i_p}(x_q)\big)_{p, q},
$$
with $(\varepsilon^i)$ the dual basis, induces a linear functional on $\Lambda^k(M)$ which takes the value $1$ on $e_I$ and $0$ on every other strictly increasing basis wedge, since some row of the matrix is then zero. Hence the basis wedges are independent. $\square$

For finite $|I| = n$,
$$
\operatorname{rank} \Lambda^k(M) = \binom{n}{k}, \qquad \Lambda^k(M) = 0 \text{ for } k > n, \qquad \operatorname{rank} \Lambda(M) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} = 2^n,
$$
with $\Lambda^n(M)$ free of rank one. Over a field,
$$
\dim_F \Lambda^k(V) = \binom{n}{k}, \qquad \dim_F \Lambda(V) = 2^n, \qquad \sum_{k \geq 0} \dim_F \Lambda^k(V) \, t^k = (1 + t)^n.
$$
If $M$ is generated by $n$ elements then $\Lambda^k(M) = 0$ for $k > n$.

**Key difference from the field case.** Over a field every module is free. Over a general commutative ring the exterior algebra of a non-free module need not be free: for $M = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ one has $\Lambda(M) \cong \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, since $\Lambda^k(M) = 0$ for $k \geq 2$.

# Part II: The Exterior Algebra

## 6. The Product, the Grading, and the Unit

Because $\Lambda(M) = T(M)/I$ with $I$ an ideal generated by homogeneous elements, the tensor-algebra product descends and the grading is inherited:
$$
\wedge : \Lambda^p(M) \times \Lambda^q(M) \to \Lambda^{p+q}(M).
$$
So $\Lambda(M)$ is a unital associative graded $R$-algebra with unit $1 \in \Lambda^0(M) = R$, generated in degree one by $M = \Lambda^1(M)$; the image of $m_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes m_k$ is $m_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge m_k$.

## 7. Graded-Commutativity

**Theorem.** For homogeneous $u \in \Lambda^p(M)$ and $w \in \Lambda^q(M)$,
$$
u \wedge w = (-1)^{pq} \, w \wedge u.
$$

**Proof.** It suffices to take elementary wedges $u = v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_p$ and $w = v_{p+1} \wedge \cdots \wedge v_{p+q}$ of elements of $M$, since these span. Moving $w$ past $u$ exchanges $q$ factors with $p$ factors, that is $pq$ transpositions, each contributing $-1$; bilinearity gives the general case. $\square$

If $p$ or $q$ is even the products commute, and if both are odd they anticommute. For homogeneous $u$ of odd degree this gives $u \wedge u = - u \wedge u$, so $2(u \wedge u) = 0$; over a field of characteristic not $2$ this alone forces $u \wedge u = 0$, while over an arbitrary commutative ring the defining relation $v \wedge v = 0$ for $v \in M$, together with graded-commutativity, gives $u \wedge u = 0$ for every odd-degree $u$. The algebra is also $\mathbb{Z}/2$-graded by degree modulo $2$, with even part $\Lambda^{\mathrm{even}}(M) = \bigoplus_{k \text{ even}} \Lambda^k(M)$, and is **supercommutative** for this grading.

## 8. The Universal Property of the Algebra

**Theorem.** Let $A$ be a unital associative $R$-algebra and $f : M \to A$ an $R$-linear map with $f(v)^2 = 0$ for all $v \in M$. Then $f$ extends uniquely to a unital algebra homomorphism $F : \Lambda(M) \to A$.

**Proof.** By the universal property of the tensor algebra, $f$ extends uniquely to a unital algebra homomorphism $T(M) \to A$. Its kernel contains every $v \otimes v$, hence contains the ideal $I$, so it factors through $\Lambda(M) = T(M)/I$. Uniqueness holds because $M$ generates $\Lambda(M)$. $\square$

Equivalently,
$$
\operatorname{Hom}_{\text{Alg}}\big(\Lambda(M), A\big) \cong \big\{ f \in \operatorname{Hom}_R(M, A) : f(v)^2 = 0 \text{ for all } v \in M \big\},
$$
naturally in $A$. So $\Lambda(M)$ is the largest unital associative $R$-algebra generated by $M$ in which every element of $M$ squares to zero. The map $v \mapsto -v$ extends to an algebra automorphism of $\Lambda(M)$ acting as $(-1)^k$ on $\Lambda^k(M)$.

## 9. The Exterior Algebra of a Direct Sum

**Theorem.** For $R$-modules $M, N$ there is a natural isomorphism of graded $R$-algebras
$$
\Lambda(M \oplus N) \cong \Lambda(M) \mathbin{\hat{\otimes}} \Lambda(N),
$$
where $\mathbin{\hat{\otimes}}$ is the **graded tensor product**, with product $(u \otimes w)(u' \otimes w') = (-1)^{|w||u'|} (uu') \otimes (ww')$ on homogeneous elements.

**Proof.** The inclusions induce a linear map $\Phi$ with $\Phi(u \otimes w) = u \wedge w$, which is multiplicative because the images of $M$ and $N$ in $\Lambda(M \oplus N)$ anticommute: $u \wedge w = (-1)^{|u||w|} w \wedge u$ for homogeneous $u, w$. Conversely, the linear map
$$
M \oplus N \to \Lambda(M) \mathbin{\hat{\otimes}} \Lambda(N), \qquad m \oplus n \mapsto m \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes n,
$$
has square zero, since $m \otimes 1$ and $1 \otimes n$ anticommute in the graded tensor product and $(m \otimes 1)^2 = (1 \otimes n)^2 = 0$. By the universal property of $\Lambda(M \oplus N)$ it extends to a homomorphism $\Psi$. The compositions are algebra homomorphisms which are the identity on generators, hence the identity, so $\Phi$ and $\Psi$ are inverse. $\square$

The underlying module is the ordinary tensor product; only the product carries the sign. In degree $k$,
$$
\Lambda^k(M \oplus N) \cong \bigoplus_{p + q = k} \Lambda^p(M) \otimes_R \Lambda^q(N), \qquad \binom{m + n}{k} = \sum_{p + q = k} \binom{m}{p} \binom{n}{q},
$$
the second identity for free modules of ranks $m, n$. Iterating, the exterior algebra of a direct sum is the graded tensor product of the exterior algebras of the summands.

## 10. The Finite-Rank Case and Examples

For $M$ free of rank $n$, $\Lambda(M)$ is free of rank $2^n$, with $\Lambda^k(M) = 0$ for $k > n$, so the ideal $\Lambda^{\geq 1}(M) = \bigoplus_{k \geq 1} \Lambda^k(M)$ satisfies $\Lambda^{\geq 1}(M)^{n+1} = 0$. If $R$ is a field, $\Lambda(M)$ is therefore a local ring with maximal ideal $\Lambda^{\geq 1}(M)$, since an element with nonzero degree-zero component is a unit and every element of positive degree is nilpotent.

Examples.

- For $M = R$ with generator $t$, $\Lambda(R) = R \oplus Rt$ with $t \wedge t = 0$, the algebra of dual numbers $R[t]/(t^2)$.
- For $M = R^2$ with basis $x, y$, the elements $1, x, y, x \wedge y$ form a basis, so $\Lambda(R^2)$ has rank $4$, with $x \wedge y = - y \wedge x$.
- For $M = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ over $\mathbb{Z}$, $\Lambda(M) \cong \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, which is not free.

# Part III: The Determinant

## 11. Induced Maps and Minors

Let $M, N$ be free with bases $(e_i)$ and $(f_j)$, and let $f : M \to N$ have matrix $A = (a_{ji})$, so that $f(e_i) = \sum_j a_{ji} f_j$. For strictly increasing $I$ put $e_I = e_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{i_k}$. Then
$$
\Lambda^k(f)(e_I) = \sum_{J} \det(A_{J, I}) \, f_J,
$$
where $A_{J, I}$ is the submatrix with rows $J$ and columns $I$; so the matrix of $\Lambda^k(f)$ is the matrix of $k \times k$ **minors** of $A$. Functoriality of $\Lambda^k$ gives the **Cauchy–Binet formula**
$$
\det\big((BA)_{K, I}\big) = \sum_{J} \det(B_{K, J}) \, \det(A_{J, I}).
$$

## 12. The Determinant

Let $M$ be free of rank $n$ and let $f : M \to M$ be $R$-linear. Since $\Lambda^n(M)$ is free of rank one, $\Lambda^n(f)$ is multiplication by a unique scalar, and the **determinant** is defined by
$$
\Lambda^n(f)(x) = \det(f) \, x \qquad \text{for all } x \in \Lambda^n(M).
$$
This is basis-free, and in a basis
$$
\Lambda^n(f)(e_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge e_n) = f(e_1) \wedge \cdots \wedge f(e_n) = \det(A) \, e_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge e_n,
$$
so it recovers the usual determinant; the scalar is independent of the basis because $\Lambda^n(f)$ is.

**Properties.** $\det(\operatorname{id}_M) = 1$, and $\det(g \circ f) = \det(g) \det(f)$ by functoriality, so $\det$ is a homomorphism $\operatorname{GL}(M) \to R^\times$. Moreover $f$ is invertible precisely when $\det(f)$ is a **unit** of $R$: one direction is $\det(f^{-1}) \det(f) = 1$, and the other follows from $A \operatorname{adj}(A) = \det(A) I$ in a basis.

**Key difference from the field case.** Over a field, invertibility is equivalent to $\det(f) \neq 0$; over a general commutative ring it is equivalent to $\det(f)$ being a unit, which is stronger. For instance, multiplication by $2$ on $\mathbb{Z}$ has determinant $2$, nonzero but not a unit, and is not invertible.

Geometrically $\Lambda^n(M)$ is the module of oriented volume elements, and $\det(f)$ is the factor by which $f$ rescales oriented volume.

# Part IV: Alternating Forms, the Dual, and the Clifford Algebra

## 13. The Exterior Algebra of the Dual

The pairing $M^* \times M \to R$ extends to
$$
\Lambda^k(M^*) \times \Lambda^k(M) \to R, \qquad (\varphi_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi_k, \, m_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge m_k) \mapsto \det\big(\varphi_i(m_j)\big),
$$
which is well defined because the determinant is alternating multilinear in the $m_j$ and in the $\varphi_i$. It yields a natural map $\Lambda^k(M^*) \to (\Lambda^k(M))^*$, corresponding under the universal property to the alternating form on $M^k$ given by that determinant.

**Theorem.** If $M$ is free of finite rank $n$, then this map is an isomorphism, so $\operatorname{rank} \Lambda^k(M^*) = \binom{n}{k}$, and the pairing is perfect.

**Proof.** If $(\varepsilon^i)$ is the dual basis, then $\varepsilon^I = \varepsilon^{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \varepsilon^{i_k}$ pairs with $e_J = e_{j_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{j_k}$ by
$$
\big(\varepsilon^I, e_J\big) = \det\big(\varepsilon^{i_p}(e_{j_q})\big) = \det(\delta_{i_p, j_q}) = \delta_{I, J},
$$
the last equality because $I$ and $J$ are strictly increasing of the same size. So $(\varepsilon^I)$ is the dual basis of $(e_I)$. $\square$

## 14. Alternating Forms

The **alternating $k$-forms** on $M$ are the alternating multilinear maps $f : M^k \to R$, and
$$
\operatorname{Alt}^k(M) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_R\big(\Lambda^k(M), R\big) = \big(\Lambda^k(M)\big)^*,
$$
so for free $M$ of finite rank, by §13, alternating forms, dual exterior powers, and linear functionals on exterior powers agree. Their product is the wedge product in $\Lambda(M^*)$: for $\varphi \in \Lambda^p(M^*)$ and $\psi \in \Lambda^q(M^*)$,
$$
(\varphi \wedge \psi)(x_1, \dots, x_{p+q}) = \sum_{\sigma} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) \, \varphi(x_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, x_{\sigma(p)}) \, \psi(x_{\sigma(p+1)}, \dots, x_{\sigma(p+q)}),
$$
summed over the $(p, q)$-**shuffles**, the permutations with $\sigma(1) < \cdots < \sigma(p)$ and $\sigma(p+1) < \cdots < \sigma(p+q)$. This formula uses no division, so it is valid over any commutative ring; the equivalent divided formula requires $p!$ and $q!$ invertible. For $v \in M$, contraction is the map $\iota_v : \Lambda^k(M^*) \to \Lambda^{k-1}(M^*)$ with $(\iota_v \omega)(x_1, \dots, x_{k-1}) = \omega(v, x_1, \dots, x_{k-1})$; it satisfies $\iota_v^2 = 0$ and $\iota_v(\omega \wedge \eta) = (\iota_v \omega) \wedge \eta + (-1)^{\deg \omega} \omega \wedge (\iota_v \eta)$.

## 15. Differential Forms

For a smooth manifold $X$, the exterior algebra of the cotangent space at $x$ is
$$
\Lambda(T_x^* X) = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} \Lambda^k(T_x^* X),
$$
with $\Lambda^k(T_x^* X)$ the alternating $k$-linear forms on the tangent space $T_x X$. A **differential $k$-form** is a smooth section of $\Lambda^k(T^* X)$, and
$$
\Omega(X) = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} \Omega^k(X)
$$
is a graded-commutative algebra over $\Omega^0(X) = C^\infty(X)$ under the pointwise wedge product. The **exterior derivative** $d : \Omega^k(X) \to \Omega^{k+1}(X)$ is $\mathbb{R}$-linear, agrees with the differential on functions, satisfies
$$
d(\omega \wedge \eta) = d\omega \wedge \eta + (-1)^p \, \omega \wedge d\eta \qquad (\omega \in \Omega^p(X)),
$$
and satisfies $d^2 = 0$, making $\Omega(X)$ a differential graded algebra, the de Rham complex. Pullback by a smooth map is a homomorphism of graded algebras commuting with $d$. Differential forms are the smooth, global realization of the exterior algebra.

## 16. Relation to the Clifford Algebra

Let $Q$ be a quadratic form on $M$ with associated bilinear form $B$. The Clifford algebra is
$$
Cl(M, Q) = T(M) \Big/ \big( v \otimes v - Q(v) \cdot 1 : v \in M \big),
$$
so $Cl(M, 0) = \Lambda(M)$: the exterior algebra is the Clifford algebra of the zero form. For general $Q$ the two have the same underlying module in the free finite-rank case, both of rank $2^n$, but are not isomorphic as algebras when $Q \neq 0$, since there is no algebra homomorphism $Cl(M, Q) \to \Lambda(M)$ carrying generators to generators: $v \otimes v$ maps to $0$ while $Q(v) \cdot 1$ maps to $Q(v)$.

The precise relation is by **filtration** by the length of products of elements of $M$,
$$
F^0 \subseteq F^1 \subseteq F^2 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq Cl(M, Q), \qquad \operatorname{gr} Cl(M, Q) \cong \Lambda(M).
$$
So $\Lambda(M)$ is the associated graded of $Cl(M, Q)$: the Clifford algebra is a filtered deformation, or quantization, of the exterior algebra, and the exterior algebra is its metric-free limit. The defining ideal is generated by inhomogeneous elements, which is why $Cl(M, Q)$ is filtered rather than graded.

When $2$ is invertible in $R$, the Clifford product of two vectors decomposes as
$$
uv = B(u, v) \cdot 1 + u \wedge v,
$$
the symmetric scalar part together with the exterior part; equivalently $uv + vu = 2 B(u, v) \cdot 1$. Geometrically, $\Lambda(M)$ is the algebra of oriented volumes $m_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge m_k$, and the Clifford algebra is that algebra with the metric $B$ added. Both are treated in the articles *Tensor Algebras* and *Clifford Algebras*.

# Part V: Summary

**Exterior powers.** $\Lambda^k(M)$ is the universal target of alternating multilinear maps $M^k \to P$, so $\operatorname{Alt}^k(M; P) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_R(\Lambda^k(M), P)$. It is the quotient of $M^{\otimes k}$ by the relations making two equal arguments vanish, and its elements are the elementary wedges $m_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge m_k$.

**The exterior algebra.** $\Lambda(M) = T(M)/(v \otimes v : v \in M) = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} \Lambda^k(M)$ is a unital associative graded algebra, generated in degree one by $M = \Lambda^1(M)$, and graded-commutative: $u \wedge w = (-1)^{pq} w \wedge u$ for $u \in \Lambda^p(M)$ and $w \in \Lambda^q(M)$. Its universal property is $\operatorname{Hom}_{\text{Alg}}(\Lambda(M), A) \cong \{ f \in \operatorname{Hom}_R(M, A) : f(v)^2 = 0 \}$.

**Bases and dimension.** For free $M$ of rank $n$, the wedges $e_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{i_k}$ with $i_1 < \cdots < i_k$ form a basis of $\Lambda^k(M)$, so $\operatorname{rank} \Lambda^k(M) = \binom{n}{k}$, $\Lambda^k(M) = 0$ for $k > n$, and $\operatorname{rank} \Lambda(M) = 2^n$.

**Direct sums.** $\Lambda(M \oplus N) \cong \Lambda(M) \mathbin{\hat{\otimes}} \Lambda(N)$ for the graded tensor product, with $\binom{m+n}{k} = \sum_{p+q=k} \binom{m}{p} \binom{n}{q}$ in the free finite-rank case.

**The determinant.** For free $M$ of rank $n$, $\Lambda^n(M)$ is free of rank one and $\det(f)$ is defined by $\Lambda^n(f)(x) = \det(f) x$; it is multiplicative, and $f$ is invertible precisely when $\det(f)$ is a unit.

**Duals, forms, and the Clifford algebra.** $\Lambda^k(M^*) \cong (\Lambda^k(M))^* \cong \operatorname{Alt}^k(M)$ for free $M$ of finite rank, with the pairing given by determinants; differential forms realize this fibrewise. $\Lambda(M) = Cl(M, 0)$, and $\operatorname{gr} Cl(M, Q) \cong \Lambda(M)$, with $uv = B(u, v) \cdot 1 + u \wedge v$ when $2$ is invertible.

**Key differences from the field case.** Modules need not be free, so the exterior algebra need not be free; the alternating and antisymmetric conditions differ when $2$ is not invertible; and invertibility requires the determinant to be a unit, not merely nonzero.

The next article, *Symmetric Algebras*, develops the other quotient of the tensor algebra.
