# __Symmetric Algebras__

## Introduction

This article treats the symmetric algebra. The treatment is introductory and purely mathematical.

The goal is to explain what a symmetric power is, how it is characterized by a universal property, how the symmetric algebra is assembled from the symmetric powers, and how it relates to the tensor and exterior algebras. The symmetric algebra is the **free commutative algebra** on a module: the algebra of formal polynomials in the elements of the module, with no relations beyond bilinearity, associativity, commutativity, and a unit. It is one of the two quotients of the tensor algebra introduced in the article *Tensor Algebras*, the other being the exterior algebra; it is the quotient that enforces commutativity rather than the alternating relation.

We assume familiarity with modules, algebras, and the tensor product, as developed in the preceding articles, and treat the general theory over a **commutative ring** $R$. The main differences from the field case are:

- Modules need not be free, so the symmetric algebra need not be a polynomial algebra and its graded pieces need not have a well-defined rank.
- The symmetric power must be defined as a **quotient** of the tensor power, not as the submodule of symmetric tensors; the two descriptions agree when $R$ is a $\mathbb{Q}$-algebra.

We indicate where these differences matter, and specialize to a field only when we say so. The exterior algebra, treated in *Exterior Algebras*, is the counterpart in which the product is alternating instead of commutative.

# Part I: Symmetric Multilinear Maps and Symmetric Powers

## 1. Symmetric Multilinear Maps

Let $M_1, \ldots, M_k, P$ be $R$-modules. A map $f : M_1 \times \cdots \times M_k \to P$ is **multilinear** if it is $R$-linear in each argument separately: fixing all arguments but the $i$-th gives an $R$-linear map $M_i \to P$. When $M_1 = \cdots = M_k = M$ we write $M^k$ for the product and call $f : M^k \to P$ **$R$-multilinear**.

A multilinear map $f : M^k \to P$ is **symmetric** if it is invariant under every permutation of its arguments,

$$
f(m_{\sigma(1)}, \ldots, m_{\sigma(k)}) = f(m_1, \ldots, m_k)
$$

for all $m_1, \ldots, m_k \in M$ and all $\sigma$ in the symmetric group $S_k$. The symmetric multilinear maps $M^k \to P$ form a submodule of the multilinear maps, written $\operatorname{Sym}_R(M^k; P)$.

For $k = 1$ every linear map is symmetric; for $k = 2$ the condition is $B(u, v) = B(v, u)$, so a symmetric multilinear map of two variables is a **symmetric bilinear form**, the case from which the terminology comes. The dot product is an example, and for a commutative $R$-algebra $A$ the product map $A^k \to A$ is symmetric multilinear.

## 2. The Universal Property of the Symmetric Power

A **symmetric power** of $M$ of degree $k$ is an $R$-module $\operatorname{Sym}^k(M)$ with a symmetric multilinear map $\sigma : M^k \to \operatorname{Sym}^k(M)$ such that for every $R$-module $P$ and every symmetric multilinear $f : M^k \to P$ there is a unique $R$-linear $\tilde{f} : \operatorname{Sym}^k(M) \to P$ with $\tilde{f} \circ \sigma = f$. Equivalently,

$$
\operatorname{Sym}_R(M^k; P) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_R\big( \operatorname{Sym}^k(M), P \big),
$$

naturally in $P$. As with the tensor product, this **universal property** determines $\operatorname{Sym}^k(M)$ up to a unique isomorphism: $\sigma$ is the universal symmetric multilinear map out of $M^k$.

The degree-zero case gives $\operatorname{Sym}^0(M) \cong R$, since a multilinear map on $M^0$ is just an element of $P$; the degree-one case gives $\operatorname{Sym}^1(M) \cong M$, since every linear map is symmetric. So a linear map $\operatorname{Sym}^k(M) \to P$ is the same thing as a symmetric multilinear map $M^k \to P$.

## 3. Construction as a Quotient of the Tensor Power

Existence follows from a quotient of the tensor power. Let $K \subseteq M^{\otimes k}$ be the submodule generated by

$$
m_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes m_k - m_{\sigma(1)} \otimes \cdots \otimes m_{\sigma(k)}
$$

for all $m_1, \ldots, m_k \in M$ and all $\sigma \in S_k$, and set $\operatorname{Sym}^k(M) = M^{\otimes k}/K$, writing $m_1 m_2 \cdots m_k$ for the class of $m_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes m_k$. The relations defining $K$ are exactly those that make the projection $M^k \to M^{\otimes k} \to \operatorname{Sym}^k(M)$ symmetric, so a symmetric multilinear $f$ vanishes on $K$ and descends to $\tilde{f}$; uniqueness holds because the products $m_1 \cdots m_k$ generate the quotient.

Since $K$ is generated by the image of the action of $S_k$ on $M^{\otimes k}$, the symmetric power is the module of **coinvariants**,

$$
\operatorname{Sym}^k(M) = \big( M^{\otimes k} \big)_{S_k}.
$$

It suffices to impose the relations for adjacent transpositions, which generate $S_k$. The class of a tensor depends only on the **multiset** of its factors, and every element of $\operatorname{Sym}^k(M)$ is a finite sum of such classes.

## 4. Basis and Rank

If $M$ is free with basis $(e_i)_{i \in I}$, then $\operatorname{Sym}^k(M)$ is free with basis the classes

$$
e_{i_1} e_{i_2} \cdots e_{i_k}, \qquad i_1 \le i_2 \le \cdots \le i_k .
$$

The non-decreasing order picks one representative from each $S_k$-orbit of $k$-tuples, so this basis is in bijection with the multisets of size $k$ taken from $I$. For finite $|I| = n$,

$$
\operatorname{rank}_R \operatorname{Sym}^k(M) = \binom{n + k - 1}{k} = \binom{n + k - 1}{n - 1}.
$$

Thus $\operatorname{Sym}^0(M)$ has rank $1$, $\operatorname{Sym}^1(M)$ has rank $n$, and $\operatorname{Sym}^2(M)$ has rank $\binom{n+1}{2}$. When $I$ is infinite, $\operatorname{Sym}^k(M)$ is free on the multisets of size $k$ from $I$.

**Key difference from the field case.** Over a field every module is free and the rank formula always applies, giving the dimension. Over a general commutative ring the symmetric power of a non-free module need not be free: for $M = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ with $n \ge 2$, the flip fixes the generator of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, so $K = 0$ and $\operatorname{Sym}^k(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, which is not free. The polynomial description of §8 therefore applies only in the free case.

## 5. Symmetric Powers and Symmetric Tensors

The symmetric group $S_k$ acts on $M^{\otimes k}$ by permuting tensor factors, and the **symmetric tensors** are the fixed points

$$
\big( M^{\otimes k} \big)^{S_k} = \{ x \in M^{\otimes k} : \sigma \cdot x = x \ \text{for all } \sigma \in S_k \}.
$$

The symmetrization map $\Sigma = \sum_{\sigma \in S_k} \sigma$ has image contained in the symmetric tensors, and it annihilates every permutation difference, since $\Sigma(\tau \cdot y - y) = \sum_\sigma (\sigma \tau) \cdot y - \sum_\sigma \sigma \cdot y = 0$. Hence $\Sigma$ descends to an $R$-linear map

$$
c : \operatorname{Sym}^k(M) \longrightarrow \big( M^{\otimes k} \big)^{S_k},
$$

sending the class of $x$ to $\Sigma(x)$, natural in $M$.

**Key difference from the field case.** Over a $\mathbb{Q}$-algebra, $\frac{1}{k!}\Sigma$ is a projection onto the symmetric tensors with kernel spanned by the permutation differences, so $c$ is an isomorphism and $\operatorname{Sym}^k(M)$ may be identified with the symmetric tensors. Over a general commutative ring, $c$ need not be an isomorphism: over $R = \mathbb{Z}$ and $M = \mathbb{Z}^2$ with basis $x, y$, the map $c$ sends the classes of $x \otimes x$, $y \otimes y$, $x \otimes y$ to $2 (x \otimes x)$, $2 (y \otimes y)$, $x \otimes y + y \otimes x$, and $x \otimes x$ is not in their span, so $c$ is not surjective. The quotient definition of §3 is the correct one in general.

# Part II: The Symmetric Algebra

## 6. Definition as a Quotient of the Tensor Algebra

The **symmetric algebra** of $M$ is the quotient of the tensor algebra by the two-sided ideal generated by the commutators of elements of $M$:

$$
\operatorname{Sym}(M) = T(M) \Big/ \big( v \otimes w - w \otimes v : v, w \in M \big).
$$

Writing $I$ for this ideal, the defining relation is $v w = w v$ for all $v, w \in M$, so $\operatorname{Sym}(M)$ is the largest quotient of $T(M)$ that is commutative.

The generators lie in $M^{\otimes 2}$, so $I$ is a **graded ideal** and the quotient inherits the grading of $T(M)$,

$$
\operatorname{Sym}(M) = \bigoplus_{k \ge 0} \operatorname{Sym}^k(M), \qquad \operatorname{Sym}^k(M) = M^{\otimes k} \big/ \big( M^{\otimes k} \cap I \big),
$$

which recovers the symmetric power of §3. The product is induced by concatenation, $x \cdot y = \text{class of } x' \otimes y'$ for representatives $x' \in M^{\otimes p}$, $y' \in M^{\otimes q}$; it is well defined because $I$ is a two-sided ideal, associative and unital because $T(M)$ is, and commutative because the classes of $v \otimes w$ and $w \otimes v$ coincide. Thus $\operatorname{Sym}(M)$ is a commutative unital associative graded $R$-algebra generated in degree one, and it is the largest commutative quotient of $T(M)$,

$$
\operatorname{Sym}(M) \cong T(M) \big/ [T(M), T(M)],
$$

where $[T(M), T(M)]$ is generated by all commutators $xy - yx$ with $x, y \in T(M)$.

## 7. The Universal Property and the Free Commutative Algebra

Let $\iota : M \to \operatorname{Sym}(M)$ be the inclusion of $M = \operatorname{Sym}^1(M)$.

**Theorem.** Let $A$ be a commutative unital associative $R$-algebra and $f : M \to A$ an $R$-linear map. There is a unique unital algebra homomorphism $F : \operatorname{Sym}(M) \to A$ with $F \circ \iota = f$.

**Proof.** By the universal property of the tensor algebra there is a unique unital homomorphism $F_0 : T(M) \to A$ with $F_0 \circ \iota_T = f$. Since $A$ is commutative, $F_0(v \otimes w - w \otimes v) = f(v) f(w) - f(w) f(v) = 0$ for all $v, w \in M$, so $F_0$ vanishes on the generators of $I$, hence on $I$, and descends to $F$. Uniqueness holds because $\iota(M)$ generates. $\square$

So for every commutative unital associative $R$-algebra $A$ there is a natural bijection

$$
\operatorname{Hom}_{\text{CommAlg}_R}\big( \operatorname{Sym}(M), A \big) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_R(M, A),
$$

given by restriction along $\iota$. Categorically, the symmetric algebra functor is **left adjoint** to the forgetful functor from commutative unital $R$-algebras to $R$-modules; this is the precise sense in which $\operatorname{Sym}(M)$ is the **free commutative $R$-algebra** on $M$. Commutativity of $A$ is essential: for a general unital associative $A$ the relation $vw = wv$ cannot be imposed, and the universal algebra is instead the tensor algebra $T(M)$.

## 8. Polynomial Algebra on a Basis

If $M$ is free with basis $(e_i)_{i \in I}$, then $\iota$ identifies the basis with variables and gives an isomorphism of graded $R$-algebras

$$
\operatorname{Sym}(M) \cong R[x_i : i \in I], \qquad x_i \leftrightarrow e_i .
$$

The basis of §4 becomes the basis of **monomials of degree $k$**, $\prod_{i \in I} x_i^{a_i}$ with $a_i \ge 0$, $\sum_i a_i = k$, and all but finitely many $a_i$ zero. By §7 a homomorphism $R[x_i : i \in I] \to A$ into a commutative unital $R$-algebra is the same thing as an arbitrary assignment of values in $A$ to the variables, with no constraints. For finite rank $n$ the **Hilbert–Poincaré series** is

$$
\sum_{k \ge 0} \operatorname{rank}_R \operatorname{Sym}^k(M) \, t^k = \sum_{k \ge 0} \binom{n+k-1}{k} t^k = \frac{1}{(1-t)^n}.
$$

If $n \ge 1$ and $R \neq 0$, $\operatorname{Sym}(M)$ has infinite rank; over a field it is infinite-dimensional. If $R$ is a domain the polynomial algebra is a domain, and if $R$ is Noetherian it is Noetherian by the Hilbert basis theorem; the Noetherian property passes to quotients, which is why polynomial algebras are the basic building blocks of commutative algebra.

## 9. Functoriality

If $f : M \to N$ is $R$-linear, then composing with $\iota_N : N \to \operatorname{Sym}(N)$ gives an $R$-linear map $M \to \operatorname{Sym}(N)$ into a commutative unital $R$-algebra, so by §7 there is a unique unital algebra homomorphism $\operatorname{Sym}(f) : \operatorname{Sym}(M) \to \operatorname{Sym}(N)$ with $\operatorname{Sym}(f) \circ \iota_M = \iota_N \circ f$. It is **graded**, with $\operatorname{Sym}(f)(\operatorname{Sym}^k(M)) \subseteq \operatorname{Sym}^k(N)$, and satisfies $\operatorname{Sym}(\operatorname{id}_M) = \operatorname{id}$ and $\operatorname{Sym}(g \circ f) = \operatorname{Sym}(g) \circ \operatorname{Sym}(f)$. So $\operatorname{Sym}$ is a functor from $R$-modules to commutative unital graded $R$-algebras, left adjoint to the forgetful functor, and it carries surjections to surjections and isomorphisms to isomorphisms.

## 10. The Symmetric Algebra of a Direct Sum

There is a natural isomorphism of graded $R$-algebras

$$
\operatorname{Sym}(M \oplus N) \cong \operatorname{Sym}(M) \otimes_R \operatorname{Sym}(N).
$$

The inclusions $M \to M \oplus N$ and $N \to M \oplus N$ induce homomorphisms whose images commute and generate, giving a map $\operatorname{Sym}(M) \otimes_R \operatorname{Sym}(N) \to \operatorname{Sym}(M \oplus N)$; the inverse comes from the $R$-linear map $m \oplus n \mapsto m \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes n$ by §7, and the two composites agree on generators. No sign is needed, unlike the exterior algebra. For free modules of ranks $m$ and $n$ this reads $R[x_1, \ldots, x_m] \otimes_R R[y_1, \ldots, y_n] \cong R[x_1, \ldots, x_m, y_1, \ldots, y_n]$.

# Part III: Structure, Examples, and Applications

## 11. Example: the Symmetric Algebra on a Two-Dimensional Space

Let $M = R^2$ with basis $x, y$. Then $\operatorname{Sym}(R^2) \cong R[x, y]$, with

$$
\operatorname{Sym}^0 = R, \quad \operatorname{Sym}^1 = \operatorname{span}_R\{x, y\}, \quad \operatorname{Sym}^2 = \operatorname{span}_R\{x^2, x y, y^2\}, \quad \operatorname{Sym}^3 = \operatorname{span}_R\{x^3, x^2 y, x y^2, y^3\},
$$

so $\operatorname{rank}_R \operatorname{Sym}^k = k + 1$ and the Hilbert–Poincaré series is $\sum_{k \ge 0} (k+1) t^k = 1/(1-t)^2$.

The quotient description of §6 is visible in degree two: the tensor power $T^2(R^2) = \operatorname{span}_R\{x \otimes x, x \otimes y, y \otimes x, y \otimes y\}$ has rank $4$, and dividing by $x \otimes y - y \otimes x$ identifies the classes of $x \otimes y$ and $y \otimes x$, written $xy$, leaving rank $3$. In general $\operatorname{Sym}^k(R^2)$ is a quotient of the rank-$2^k$ tensor power $T^k(R^2)$ and has rank $k+1$.

For the universal property, an $R$-linear $f : R^2 \to A$ into a commutative unital associative $R$-algebra is determined by the pair $(f(x), f(y)) = (a, b)$, and every pair extends uniquely to a unital homomorphism $\varphi : R[x, y] \to A$ with $\varphi(x) = a$, $\varphi(y) = b$. Hence $\operatorname{Hom}_{\text{CommAlg}_R}(R[x, y], A) \cong A \times A$. For a **non-commutative** unital $A$, a pair with $ab \neq ba$ still determines a homomorphism from the free associative algebra $R\langle x, y\rangle$ of *Tensor Algebras*, but not from $R[x, y]$; imposing $xy = yx$ restricts the possible targets to commutative algebras.

## 12. The Counterpart to the Exterior Algebra

The symmetric and exterior algebras are the two natural quotients of the tensor algebra by a relation of degree two, and they are counterparts in the following sense.

- **Defining relation.** $\operatorname{Sym}(M) = T(M)/(v \otimes w - w \otimes v)$ imposes $v w = w v$, so the product is commutative. $\Lambda(M) = T(M)/(v \otimes v)$ imposes $v \wedge v = 0$, and its product is alternating and graded-commutative, $u \wedge w = (-1)^{p q} w \wedge u$ for homogeneous elements of degrees $p, q$.
- **Universal property.** Linear maps out of $\operatorname{Sym}^k(M)$ are symmetric multilinear maps $M^k \to P$, and $\operatorname{Sym}(M)$ is the free commutative algebra. Linear maps out of $\Lambda^k(M)$ are alternating multilinear maps $M^k \to P$, and $\Lambda(M)$ is the free graded-commutative algebra with $v^2 = 0$.
- **Rank of graded pieces.** For $M$ free of finite rank $n$, $\operatorname{rank}_R \operatorname{Sym}^k(M) = \binom{n+k-1}{k}$ grows polynomially, so $\operatorname{Sym}(M)$ has infinite rank for $n \ge 1$, whereas $\operatorname{rank}_R \Lambda^k(M) = \binom{n}{k}$ vanishes for $k > n$, so $\Lambda(M)$ has rank $2^n$.
- **Ring-theoretic behaviour.** If $R$ is a domain and $M$ is free, $\operatorname{Sym}(M)$ is a domain, whereas every element of positive degree in $\Lambda(M)$ is nilpotent when $M$ has finite rank. Over a field, $\operatorname{gr} U(\mathfrak{g}) \cong \operatorname{Sym}(\mathfrak{g})$ by Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt and $\operatorname{gr} Cl(M, Q) \cong \Lambda(M)$, so $\operatorname{Sym}$ and $\Lambda$ are the commutative shadows of the enveloping and Clifford constructions.

When $2$ is invertible in $R$, the degree-two tensor power splits as $M^{\otimes 2} = \operatorname{Sym}^2(M) \oplus \Lambda^2(M)$, since $x = \frac{1}{2}(x + \tau x) + \frac{1}{2}(x - \tau x)$ decomposes a tensor into its symmetric and antisymmetric parts. Over a field of characteristic $0$ the same holds in every degree: $\operatorname{Sym}^k(M)$ and $\Lambda^k(M)$ are the images of the trivial and sign idempotents $\frac{1}{k!} \sum_{\sigma \in S_k} \sigma$ and $\frac{1}{k!} \sum_{\sigma \in S_k} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) \sigma$ in the group algebra of $S_k$. Over a general commutative ring neither decomposition is available, which is why the two algebras must be defined as quotients.

## 13. Divided Powers

Symmetric powers are not the only way to record powers of elements of a module. The **divided power algebra** $\Gamma(M)$ is the commutative $R$-algebra generated by symbols $m^{[k]}$ for $m \in M$ and $k \ge 0$, subject to the relations

$$
m^{[0]} = 1, \qquad (r m)^{[k]} = r^k m^{[k]}, \qquad (m + m')^{[k]} = \sum_{i+j=k} m^{[i]} m'^{[j]},
$$

$$
m^{[i]} m^{[j]} = \binom{i+j}{i} m^{[i+j]} .
$$

The degree-$k$ part $\Gamma^k(M)$ is the $k$-th **divided power**. The last relation makes $m^{[k]}$ behave like $m^k / k!$, recording the binomial coefficients that appear when powers are multiplied.

The assignment $(m_1, \ldots, m_k) \mapsto m_1^{[1]} m_2^{[1]} \cdots m_k^{[1]}$ is a symmetric multilinear map $M^k \to \Gamma^k(M)$, so by §2 it induces an $R$-linear map $\operatorname{Sym}^k(M) \to \Gamma^k(M)$, and these assemble into a homomorphism of graded $R$-algebras

$$
\operatorname{Sym}(M) \longrightarrow \Gamma(M).
$$

On a single element, $m^{[1]} m^{[1]} = 2 m^{[2]}$ and hence $(m^{[1]})^k = k! \, m^{[k]}$ by induction from the product relation. Consequently, when $k!$ is invertible in $R$ for every $k$ — that is, when $R$ is a $\mathbb{Q}$-algebra — this map is an isomorphism, with inverse $m^{[k]} \mapsto m^k / k!$, and divided powers and symmetric powers coincide.

Over a general commutative ring the two differ. For $M = R$ with generator $x$, the symmetric algebra is $R[x]$, while the divided power algebra is free on the symbols $x^{[k]}$ with $x^{[i]} x^{[j]} = \binom{i+j}{i} x^{[i+j]}$; the canonical map sends $x^k$ to $k! \, x^{[k]}$. Over $\mathbb{Z}$ it is injective but not surjective, since $x^{[2]}$ is not in its image, and over a field of characteristic $p$ it has a kernel, because $k! = 0$ for $k \ge p$. Divided powers are the natural coefficients when binomial coefficients must be integral, as in crystalline cohomology.

## 14. The Symmetric Algebra in Algebraic Geometry

Let $k$ be a field and $V$ a finite-dimensional $k$-vector space, with dual $V^\vee = \operatorname{Hom}_k(V, k)$. Choosing a basis $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ of $V^\vee$ gives

$$
\operatorname{Sym}(V^\vee) \cong k[x_1, \ldots, x_n],
$$

and a polynomial in the coordinate functions is exactly a polynomial function on $V \cong k^n$. So $\operatorname{Sym}(V^\vee)$ is the algebra of **polynomial functions** on $V$, and

$$
\operatorname{Spec} \operatorname{Sym}(V^\vee) \cong \mathbb{A}^n_k,
$$

affine $n$-space over $k$, whose $k$-points are $\operatorname{Hom}_{k\text{-alg}}(k[x_1, \ldots, x_n], k) \cong k^n \cong V$. Thus the symmetric algebra is the **coordinate ring** of affine space, graded as the polynomial ring is.

More generally, a finitely generated commutative $k$-algebra is by definition a quotient of a polynomial algebra $k[x_1, \ldots, x_n] = \operatorname{Sym}(V^\vee)$ by an ideal, so **affine algebraic geometry is the study of quotients of symmetric algebras** of finite-rank free modules, cut out by polynomial equations. The same construction with a graded ideal gives projective geometry: $\mathbb{P}^n_k = \operatorname{Proj} k[x_0, \ldots, x_n]$ is the $\operatorname{Proj}$ of a symmetric algebra, and a projective variety is the $\operatorname{Proj}$ of a graded quotient of one.

There is a relative version: for a locally free sheaf $\mathcal{E}$, $\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathcal{O}_X}(\mathcal{E}^\vee)$ has $\operatorname{Spec}$ equal to the total space of the dual vector bundle.

# Part IV: Summary

**Symmetric powers.** The symmetric power is the universal target of symmetric multilinear maps, $\operatorname{Sym}_R(M^k; P) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_R(\operatorname{Sym}^k(M), P)$, realized as the quotient $\operatorname{Sym}^k(M) = M^{\otimes k} / K$ by the permutation differences, that is, as the coinvariants of $S_k$ acting on $M^{\otimes k}$. For $M$ free with basis $(e_i)_{i \in I}$ it is free on the classes $e_{i_1} \cdots e_{i_k}$ with $i_1 \le \cdots \le i_k$, of rank $\binom{n+k-1}{k}$ for $|I| = n$.

**The symmetric algebra.** $\operatorname{Sym}(M) = T(M)/(v \otimes w - w \otimes v) \cong T(M)/[T(M), T(M)]$ is the free commutative unital associative $R$-algebra on $M$, graded with $\operatorname{Sym}^0(M) = R$ and $\operatorname{Sym}^1(M) = M$, characterized by $\operatorname{Hom}_{\text{CommAlg}_R}(\operatorname{Sym}(M), A) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_R(M, A)$, left adjoint to the forgetful functor, with $\operatorname{Sym}(M \oplus N) \cong \operatorname{Sym}(M) \otimes_R \operatorname{Sym}(N)$. For free $M$ of rank $n$ it is the polynomial algebra $R[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, with Hilbert–Poincaré series $1/(1-t)^n$, a domain when $R$ is and Noetherian when $R$ is.

**Divided powers and the exterior algebra.** The divided power algebra $\Gamma(M)$ has $m^{[i]} m^{[j]} = \binom{i+j}{i} m^{[i+j]}$ and receives a canonical map $\operatorname{Sym}(M) \to \Gamma(M)$, $m^k \mapsto k! \, m^{[k]}$, which is an isomorphism exactly over a $\mathbb{Q}$-algebra. The exterior algebra is the counterpart of $\operatorname{Sym}(M)$: both are degree-two quotients of $T(M)$, by $v w = w v$ and by $v \wedge v = 0$, with graded ranks $\binom{n+k-1}{k}$ and $\binom{n}{k}$, so $\operatorname{Sym}(M)$ is infinite-dimensional while $\Lambda(M)$ has rank $2^n$; over a field of characteristic $0$ they are the trivial and sign isotypic components of $M^{\otimes k}$.

**Algebraic geometry.** $\operatorname{Sym}(V^\vee)$ is the coordinate ring of affine space, with $\operatorname{Spec} \operatorname{Sym}(V^\vee) \cong \mathbb{A}^n$ and $\operatorname{Proj} \operatorname{Sym}(V^\vee) \cong \mathbb{P}^{n-1}$; affine algebraic geometry is the study of quotients of symmetric algebras.

**Key field-case differences.** Modules need not be free, so $\operatorname{Sym}(M)$ need not be polynomial and its graded pieces need not be free; the symmetric power is a quotient, and coincides with the symmetric tensors only over a $\mathbb{Q}$-algebra, as do divided powers with symmetric powers.

The tensor algebra, the exterior algebra, and the symmetric algebra are treated in *Tensor Algebras*, *Exterior Algebras*, and the present article; the Clifford algebra is a further quotient, treated in *Clifford Algebras*.
