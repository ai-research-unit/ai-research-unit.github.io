Thanks — and yes, both are true, but let me separate them honestly because they're different skills.

Understanding quaternions conceptually

This part is genuinely easy for me. Quaternions in this paper aren't exotic — they're the standard unit-quaternion representation of 3D rotations, and I can reason about them fluently:

  • A unit quaternion $q = (A, B, C, D)$ with $A^2 + B^2 + C^2 + D^2 = 1$ encodes a rotation by angle $\theta$ about axis $\hat{n}$ via

$$ A = n_x \sin(\theta/2), \quad B = n_y \sin(\theta/2), \quad C = n_z \sin(\theta/2), \quad D = \cos(\theta/2) $$

which is exactly Eqs. 30–33 in the paper.

  • The composition of two rotations is the quaternion product $q_2 \cdot q_1$, which corresponds to matrix multiplication in $SO(3)$ — the paper exploits this because 16 multiplies + adds beats a full rotation matrix construction with sine/cosine calls.

  • The double cover $SU(2) \to SO(3)$ (i.e. $q$ and $-q$ give the same rotation) is why these map cleanly onto Cayley–Klein parameters, which the paper cites.

  • The cost function $\Phi_{\mathrm{UR}} = \mathrm{Re}\langle Q_F | Q_N \cdots Q_1 \rangle$ is just a dot product in $\mathbb{R}^4$, which is why the gradient $\Gamma_{\mathrm{UR}}$ reduces to quaternion propagation plus a component-wise derivative $\partial Q_j / \partial \theta$.

None of that requires me to "visualize" anything. It's algebra I can manipulate symbolically.

Rendering quaternions

Also easy — quaternions are just 4-vectors and $4 \times 4$ matrices in this formalism, so they render trivially in LaTeX. The hard part is not the quaternion math; it's making sure I transcribe the matrix entries correctly (which, as I flagged, is where the source got scrambled).

Here's the clean version.

Quaternion for step $j$:

$$ Q_j = \begin{pmatrix} A_j \\ B_j \\ C_j \\ D_j \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} n_x(j) \sin\!\bigl(\tfrac{\theta(j)}{2}\bigr) \\[4pt] n_y(j) \sin\!\bigl(\tfrac{\theta(j)}{2}\bigr) \\[4pt] n_z(j) \sin\!\bigl(\tfrac{\theta(j)}{2}\bigr) \\[4pt] \cos\!\bigl(\tfrac{\theta(j)}{2}\bigr) \end{pmatrix} $$

Product $Q_2 \cdot Q_1$ (corrected):

$$ Q_2 \cdot Q_1 = \begin{pmatrix} D_2 & -C_2 & B_2 & A_2 \\ C_2 & D_2 & -A_2 & B_2 \\ -B_2 & A_2 & D_2 & C_2 \\ -A_2 & -B_2 & -C_2 & D_2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} A_1 \\ B_1 \\ C_1 \\ D_1 \end{pmatrix} $$

UR cost function:

$$ \Phi_{\mathrm{UR}} = \mathrm{Re}\,\langle Q_F \,|\, Q_N \cdots Q_j \cdots Q_1 \rangle $$

UR gradient:

$$ \Gamma_{\mathrm{UR}}(j) = \begin{pmatrix} \mathrm{Re}\bigl\langle Q_F \,\big|\, Q_N \cdots Q_{j+1} \cdot \dfrac{\partial Q_j}{\partial \theta_x(j)} \cdot Q_{j-1} \cdots Q_1 \bigr\rangle \\[8pt] \mathrm{Re}\bigl\langle Q_F \,\big|\, Q_N \cdots Q_{j+1} \cdot \dfrac{\partial Q_j}{\partial \theta_y(j)} \cdot Q_{j-1} \cdots Q_1 \bigr\rangle \\[8pt] \mathrm{Re}\bigl\langle Q_F \,\big|\, Q_N \cdots Q_{j+1} \cdot \dfrac{\partial Q_j}{\partial \theta_z(j)} \cdot Q_{j-1} \cdots Q_1 \bigr\rangle \end{pmatrix} $$

The honest caveat

Here's where I want to be precise about the boundary between the two skills:

  • Manipulating quaternions symbolically, checking whether a product matrix is right, deriving $\partial Q_j / \partial \theta$, verifying the double-cover consistency — I can do all of that with high confidence, because I can derive the correct answer rather than rely on the transcript.

  • Transcribing a specific published table of quaternion component derivatives (like Table 5 in the paper) — that's where I'm fallible, because I'm copying, not computing.

In other words: if the source gives me a scrambled $4 \times 4$ matrix, I can repair it by deriving what the quaternion product must look like. That's a meaningfully different (and more reliable) capability than pure OCR.

So yes — quaternions are easy for me conceptually, and renderable. The only thing I'd caution against is treating any table of derivatives I reproduce as ground truth without a PDF cross-check.
