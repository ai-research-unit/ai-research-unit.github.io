
# __Spinors__

## Introduction

This article introduces spinors as the representation spaces of the spin group. It belongs to the algebra series and continues *Clifford Algebras in Finite Dimensions*. The treatment is purely mathematical, and it covers the Pin and Spin groups as subgroups of the Clifford group, the classification of the spin representations including their real structure, the low-dimensional isomorphisms with the classical groups, and the passage to spinors on manifolds.

We assume familiarity with modules, bilinear and quadratic forms, Clifford algebras, and even subalgebras, as developed in the preceding articles. Groups, Lie algebras, and the rudiments of fibre bundles are assumed.

**Conventions.** The Clifford algebra is defined by $v^2 = Q(v)\cdot 1$, as throughout the series. For a real vector space with a quadratic form of signature $(p,q)$ --- meaning $p$ positive squares and $q$ negative squares in an orthogonal basis --- we write $Cl_{p,q}$ for $Cl(\mathbb{R}^{p,q})$ and set $n = p+q$. The even subalgebra is $Cl^+(W,Q)$ and the odd part is $Cl^-(W,Q)$, so that

$$
Cl(W,Q) = Cl^+(W,Q) \oplus Cl^-(W,Q).
$$

Complexification is written $\mathbb{C}\otimes Cl(W,Q)$, or $\mathbb{C}l_n$ when only the dimension of the underlying space matters. The three canonical involutions are reversion $x\mapsto x^\dagger$, Clifford conjugation $x\mapsto \bar{x}$, and the grade involution $\alpha$, of which the first two are anti-involutions; on an element of grade $k$ they act by

$$
x^\dagger = (-1)^{k(k-1)/2}x, \qquad \bar{x} = (-1)^{k(k+1)/2}x, \qquad \alpha(x) = (-1)^k x.
$$

Part I constructs the spin group; Part II treats the spin representations and their classification; Part III works out the low-dimensional cases; Part IV passes to spinors on manifolds; Part V summarizes.

# Part I: The Spin Group

## 1. Unit vectors and reflections

Let $W$ be a real vector space of dimension $n \geq 2$ with a non-degenerate quadratic form $Q$, and let $B$ be the associated bilinear form. A vector $v \in W$ is a **unit vector** if $Q(v) = \pm 1$. Every non-isotropic vector can be normalized to a unit vector by scaling.

A non-isotropic vector $v$ defines the **reflection** in the hyperplane orthogonal to $v$,

$$
r_v(u) = u - 2\frac{B(u,v)}{Q(v)}\,v .
$$

In the Clifford algebra the same map is given by twisted conjugation. Let $\alpha$ be the grade involution of $Cl(W,Q)$ and let

$$
\rho(v)(u) = \alpha(v)\, u\, v^{-1} = -\,v u v^{-1}.
$$

Using the fundamental relation $uv + vu = 2B(u,v)\cdot 1$ one computes $vuv^{-1} = -r_v(u)$, so that $\rho(v) = r_v$ is the reflection. The orthogonal group $O(W,Q)$ is generated by reflections, and every reflection is of this form.

## 2. The Clifford group, the Pin group and the Spin group

The conjugation map of the preceding section is defined for any invertible element. This leads to the following groups.

**The Clifford group.** The **Clifford group** (also called the Lipschitz group) is the group

$$
\Gamma(W,Q) = \{\, s \in Cl(W,Q)^\times \;:\; sWs^{-1} \subseteq W \,\},
$$

where $Cl(W,Q)^\times$ denotes the group of units. For $s \in \Gamma(W,Q)$ the grade-twisted adjoint

$$
\rho(s)(v) = \alpha(s)\, v\, s^{-1}, \qquad v \in W,
$$

is an orthogonal transformation of $W$, and $\rho : \Gamma(W,Q) \to O(W,Q)$ is a surjective homomorphism.

**The Pin group.** The **Pin group** is the subgroup generated by the unit vectors:

$$
\operatorname{Pin}(W,Q) = \langle\, v \in W \;:\; Q(v) = \pm 1 \,\rangle \subseteq Cl(W,Q)^\times .
$$

It is a subgroup of the Clifford group, since generators act by reflections. Its restriction $\rho : \operatorname{Pin}(W,Q) \to O(W,Q)$ is still surjective, and it is a **double cover** of the orthogonal group: the kernel is $\{\pm 1\}$.

**The Spin group.** The **spin group** is the even part of the Pin group:

$$
\operatorname{Spin}(W,Q) = \operatorname{Pin}(W,Q) \cap Cl^+(W,Q)
= \{ v_1 v_2 \cdots v_{2k} : v_i \in W,\ Q(v_i) = \pm 1 \}.
$$

Equivalently, it is the subgroup generated by products of an even number of unit vectors. An even element has $\alpha(s) = s$, so on $\operatorname{Spin}(W,Q)$ the twisted adjoint reduces to ordinary conjugation,

$$
\rho(s)(v) = s\, v\, s^{-1}, \qquad s \in \operatorname{Spin}(W,Q).
$$

Because an even product of reflections is a rotation, the image of $\operatorname{Spin}(W,Q)$ lies in $SO(W,Q)$. The restriction

$$
\operatorname{Spin}(W,Q) \longrightarrow SO(W,Q)
$$

is again a double cover, with kernel $\{\pm 1\}$.

## 3. The double cover of the special orthogonal group

The map $\rho : \operatorname{Spin}(W,Q) \to SO(W,Q)$ is a surjective homomorphism with kernel $\{\pm 1\}$. Its image is the identity component $\operatorname{SO}^+(W,Q)$ of $SO(W,Q)$, because $\operatorname{Spin}(W,Q)$ is connected for $n \geq 2$ and the image is a connected subgroup of the same dimension. For a **definite** form, $SO(W,Q)$ is itself connected, so the image is all of it.

The two elements $s$ and $-s$ of the spin group have the same image in $SO(W,Q)$. They act on $W$ in exactly the same way, so $W$ does not carry the extra sign. It is precisely the spin representations of Part II, on which $\pm 1$ act differently, that turn the double cover into something more than a bookkeeping device.

Two facts explain the topology behind the double cover. For $n \geq 3$ with a definite form, $\pi_1(SO(n)) = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, so $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ is the universal cover of $SO(n)$. For $n = 2$, $\pi_1(SO(2)) = \mathbb{Z}$, and $\operatorname{Spin}(2) \to SO(2)$ is the connected double cover $z \mapsto z^2$, which is not the universal cover.

**Remark.** An element of the spin group is a group element, not a path. It is the non-contractible loops of $SO(W,Q)$ that lift to paths joining $+1$ to $-1$; the group records the obstruction, but its elements are not paths.

## 4. The Lie algebra of the spin group

The differential of the double cover is an isomorphism of Lie algebras. The bivectors of $Cl(W,Q)$ are closed under the commutator bracket, and $u\wedge v\mapsto \tfrac{1}{2}(uv-vu)$ identifies them with the orthogonal Lie algebra, so that $\operatorname{Lie}(\operatorname{Spin}(W,Q)) \cong \mathfrak{so}(W,Q)$. The spin group is therefore indistinguishable from the rotation group infinitesimally; the difference between them is purely topological.

# Part II: Spin Representations

## 5. Clifford modules and the spinor module

A **Clifford module** for $Cl(W,Q)$ is a left module on which the Clifford algebra acts. Since we want the standard spinor representations, whose natural field is $\mathbb{C}$, we complexify:

$$
\mathbb{C}\otimes Cl(W,Q) = Cl(W_{\mathbb{C}}, Q_{\mathbb{C}}), \qquad W_{\mathbb{C}} = \mathbb{C}\otimes W,
$$

with $Q_{\mathbb{C}}$ the complex bilinear extension of $Q$. The complexified algebra depends only on $n = \dim W$, not on the signature.

A **spinor module** is a simple $\mathbb{C}\otimes Cl(W,Q)$-module. Its elements are **spinors**. Because $\operatorname{Spin}(W,Q) \subseteq Cl^+(W,Q) \subseteq Cl(W,Q)$, a spinor module restricts to a representation of the spin group, called a **spin representation**. This representation does not factor through $SO(W,Q)$, because $-1 \in \operatorname{Spin}(W,Q)$ acts on any nonzero Clifford module as $-\mathrm{id}$:

$$
(-1)\cdot\psi = -\psi, \qquad \psi \in \Delta .
$$

This is the precise sense in which a spinor is not a vector or a tensor: it is an element of a module for the Clifford algebra, not for the tensor algebra of $W$.

A concrete model is the exterior algebra $\Lambda(U)$ of a maximal isotropic subspace $U\subseteq W_{\mathbb{C}}$ of dimension $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$; the Clifford algebra acts on it by exterior and interior multiplication. This model and the minimal-left-ideal model give isomorphic spinor modules.

## 6. The complex spin representation and its dimension

Let $n = \dim W$ and recall the structure of the complexified Clifford algebra:

$$
\mathbb{C}\otimes Cl_{p,q} \;\cong\;
\begin{cases}
M_{2^{n/2}}(\mathbb{C}), & n \text{ even},\\[2pt]
M_{2^{(n-1)/2}}(\mathbb{C}) \oplus M_{2^{(n-1)/2}}(\mathbb{C}), & n \text{ odd}.
\end{cases}
$$

Consequently the simple modules have complex dimension

$$
\dim_{\mathbb{C}} \Delta = 2^{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}.
$$

For $n$ even the algebra is simple and $\Delta$ is the unique simple module; for $n$ odd there are two non-isomorphic simple modules, and they are isomorphic as representations of the spin group. In both cases the spin representation has dimension $2^{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}$ over $\mathbb{C}$.

The table of small cases is

| $n$ | $\dim_{\mathbb{C}} \Delta$ | half-spin dimensions |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 2 | $1 + 1$ |
| 3 | 2 | --- |
| 4 | 4 | $2 + 2$ |
| 5 | 4 | --- |
| 6 | 8 | $4 + 4$ |

For $n$ even, the spin representation is not irreducible: it splits into the two half-spin representations of the next section.

## 7. Chirality, the volume element, and half-spin representations

Let $e_1, \ldots, e_n$ be an orthogonal basis of $W$ and let

$$
\omega = e_1 e_2 \cdots e_n
$$

be the **volume element**. Its square is the scalar

$$
\omega^2 = (-1)^{n(n-1)/2}\, Q(e_1) Q(e_2) \cdots Q(e_n) \cdot 1 .
$$

When $n$ is even, $\omega$ has even grade, so it lies in $Cl^+(W,Q)$ and commutes with it; it anticommutes with the odd elements, hence with $W$. When $n$ is odd, $\omega$ is central in all of $Cl(W,Q)$.

For $n$ even, the eigenspaces of $\omega$ inside the spinor module give the decomposition into **half-spin representations** $\Delta = \Delta^+ \oplus \Delta^-$. Over $\mathbb{C}$ one may replace $\omega$ by the **chirality operator**

$$
\gamma = i^{\,n/2}\,\omega, \qquad \gamma^2 = 1,
$$

whose $\pm 1$-eigenspaces are $\Delta^\pm$. Each half-spin module has complex dimension $2^{\,n/2 - 1}$, and the projections are

$$
P_{\pm} = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(1 \pm \gamma\big).
$$

The two half-spin representations are the **left-handed** and **right-handed** Weyl representations. They are irreducible as representations of the spin group.

For $n$ odd there is no chirality decomposition: $\omega$ is central and acts as a scalar on the spinor module, and the spin representation is irreducible over $\mathbb{C}$.

Whether $\omega$ itself is a chirality operator depends on the signature: in Euclidean signature $(n,0)$ one has $\omega^2=(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}$, while in Lorentzian signature $(1,3)$ with $Q=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$ one has $\omega^2=-1$, so there the real chirality operator is $i\omega$. Over $\mathbb{C}$ the normalization is immaterial.

## 8. Real structures: Dirac, Weyl and Majorana spinors

The complex spinor module carries additional structure that depends on the signature. Let $n = p+q$ and let $\Delta$ be the complex spinor module.

**Dirac spinors.** A **Dirac spinor** is an element of $\Delta$, the full spinor module of complex dimension $2^{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}$. For even $n$ it has $2^{\,n/2-1}$ components of each chirality.

**Weyl spinors.** For even $n$, a **Weyl spinor** is an element of one of the half-spin modules $\Delta^\pm$. Weyl spinors therefore exist only in even dimensions. They are the carriers of chirality.

**Majorana spinors.** A **Majorana spinor** is a spinor lying in a real form of the spinor module, that is, in a real spin representation. Such a real structure need not exist, and its existence is governed by the type of the complex representation, which depends on the signature modulo $8$. If $\Delta^\pm$ is of real type, one speaks of **Majorana--Weyl** spinors; if it is of quaternionic type, one speaks of symplectic Majorana spinors; if it is of complex type, no Majorana condition can be imposed on either half $\Delta^\pm$, although the full module $\Delta$ is then self-conjugate, $\Delta^- \cong \overline{\Delta^+}$, and so still admits a real form.

The type is read off from $s = p - q \bmod 8$.

*For $n$ even*, the half-spin representations $\Delta^\pm$ have type

| $p-q \bmod 8$ | type of $\Delta^\pm$ | consequence |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | real | Majorana--Weyl |
| 2, 6 | complex | Weyl only |
| 4 | quaternionic | symplectic Majorana |

*For $n$ odd*, the spin representation $\Delta$ has type

| $p-q \bmod 8$ | type of $\Delta$ |
|---|---|
| 1, 7 | real (Majorana) |
| 3, 5 | quaternionic (symplectic Majorana) |

The table is standard and can be checked against the classical low-dimensional groups: for $SO(4)$ the half-spin representations are quaternionic, for $SO(6)$ they are the mutually conjugate representations $4$ and $\bar{4}$ of $SU(4)$, and for $SO(8)$ they are real.

For **Lorentzian signature $(1,D-1)$** we have $p - q = 2 - D$, and the table gives the pattern:

- Weyl spinors exist for every even $D$;
- Majorana--Weyl spinors exist precisely for $D \equiv 2 \pmod 8$, that is, for $D = 2, 10, 18, \ldots$;
- for $D \equiv 6 \pmod 8$ the half-spin representations are quaternionic, so there are Weyl spinors and symplectic Majorana spinors but no ordinary Majorana spinors;
- for $D \equiv 4 \pmod 8$, as in $D = 4$, Majorana spinors exist but Majorana--Weyl spinors do not;
- for $D \equiv 0 \pmod 8$ the two half-spin representations are mutually conjugate complex representations, so again there is no Majorana--Weyl spinor.

In particular, in signature $(1,3)$, the Dirac spinor has four complex components and splits into two two-component Weyl spinors, $\Delta^+$ and $\Delta^-$; the two Weyl representations are complex conjugates of one another, and there are Majorana spinors but no Majorana--Weyl spinors.

# Part III: Spinors in Low Dimensions

## 9. Dimension 2

For the Euclidean form, $Cl^+_{2,0} \cong \mathbb{C}$ and $\operatorname{Spin}(2) \cong U(1) = SO(2)$, the double cover being $z \mapsto z^2$. The spinor module has complex dimension $2$, splitting into two one-dimensional half-spin modules; a half-spinor transforms by $\psi \mapsto z^{1/2}\psi$ under the lift of a rotation by $z$, and the two choices of sign of the square root are the two elements of the spin group lying over the rotation.

For the split form $(1,1)$, $Cl^+ \cong \mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}$, the spin group is the connected group $\{a + b\,e_1e_2 : a^2-b^2=1\} \cong \mathbb{R}_{>0}$, and the spinor module is $\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}$; its spinors transform by a hyperbolic phase.

## 10. Dimension 3 and the quaternions

For the Euclidean form, $Cl_{3,0} \cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$ as a real algebra, and the even subalgebra is

$$
Cl^+_{3,0} \cong \mathbb{H}.
$$

Hence the spin group is the group of unit quaternions:

$$
\operatorname{Spin}(3) \cong SU(2) \cong Sp(1).
$$

The double cover $\operatorname{Spin}(3) \to SO(3)$ is the classical map that sends a unit quaternion $q$ to the rotation $v \mapsto q v q^{-1}$ of the imaginary quaternions, identified with $\mathbb{R}^3$. The spinor module is $\mathbb{C}^2$, of complex dimension $2$. The bivectors form a copy of $\mathfrak{so}(3) \cong \mathfrak{su}(2)$, corresponding to the imaginary quaternions.

## 11. Dimension 4, Euclidean signature

For the Euclidean form, $Cl_{4,0} \cong M_2(\mathbb{H})$ as a real algebra, and the even subalgebra splits:

$$
Cl^+_{4,0} \cong \mathbb{H} \oplus \mathbb{H}, \qquad
\operatorname{Spin}(4) \cong SU(2)\times SU(2) \cong Sp(1)\times Sp(1).
$$

Identifying $\mathbb{R}^4$ with the quaternions $\mathbb{H}$, the double cover acts by

$$
(q_L, q_R)\cdot v = q_L\, v\, \overline{q_R}, \qquad (q_L,q_R) \in Sp(1)\times Sp(1).
$$

The spinor module is $\mathbb{C}^4$, of complex dimension $4$, and it splits into two half-spin modules of complex dimension $2$:

$$
\Delta = \Delta^+\oplus\Delta^-, \qquad \Delta^\pm \cong \mathbb{C}^2 .
$$

The two halves are the two copies of the fundamental representation of the two $SU(2)$ factors. Each is of quaternionic type, consistent with $p-q = 4 \equiv 4 \pmod 8$. This is the algebraic origin of the left--right distinction of the Weyl spinors in four dimensions.

## 12. Dimension 4, Lorentzian signature and the biquaternions

Let $Q$ have signature $(1,3)$, the convention used in the companion articles, with an orthonormal basis $e_0, e_1, e_2, e_3$ satisfying $Q(e_0) = 1$ and $Q(e_k) = -1$. Then, as real algebras,

$$
Cl_{1,3} \cong M_2(\mathbb{H}), \qquad Cl^+_{1,3} \cong Cl_{3,0} \cong M_2(\mathbb{C}),
$$

and the complexified algebras are

$$
\mathbb{C}\otimes Cl_{1,3} \cong M_4(\mathbb{C}), \qquad
\mathbb{C}\otimes Cl^+_{1,3} \cong M_2(\mathbb{C}) \oplus M_2(\mathbb{C}).
$$

The spin group is

$$
\operatorname{Spin}(3,1) \cong SL(2,\mathbb{C}),
$$

which is the double cover of the identity component of the Lorentz group. Its Lie algebra satisfies

$$
\mathfrak{so}(3,1) \cong \mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C}), \qquad
\mathfrak{so}(3,1)\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C} \cong \mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})\oplus\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C}),
$$

the two complex summands corresponding to the self-dual and anti-self-dual bivectors.

The spinor module is $\mathbb{C}^4$; it is of complex type, and the two half-spin modules are the two-dimensional **Weyl spinors** $\Delta^+$ and $\Delta^-$, which are complex conjugate to one another. The full Dirac spinor is the direct sum

$$
\Delta = \Delta^+\oplus\Delta^-, \qquad \dim_{\mathbb{C}}\Delta^\pm = 2 .
$$

There are Majorana spinors, of real dimension $4$, because the full Dirac representation is self-conjugate; but there are no Majorana--Weyl spinors, because the Weyl representations are a complex-conjugate pair.

**Relation to the biquaternions.** The real even Clifford algebra in this signature is the biquaternion algebra:

$$
Cl^+_{1,3} \cong \mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H} = \mathbb{B} \cong M_2(\mathbb{C}).
$$

Under this identification the bivectors of $Cl_{1,3}$ correspond to the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$, and the scalar imaginary of $\mathbb{B}$ corresponds to the volume element, up to a sign. The biquaternion algebra acts naturally on a two-dimensional complex module, and this module is the Weyl spinor module. Its complexification $\mathbb{C}\otimes Cl^+_{1,3}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})\oplus M_2(\mathbb{C})$ has two simple summands, which give the two chiralities, so a biquaternion may be read as a pair of Weyl spinors. The signature matters: for the split signature $(2,2)$ the real even Clifford algebra is $M_2(\mathbb{R})\oplus M_2(\mathbb{R})$, which is not the biquaternion algebra.

## 13. Dimensions 5 and 6

For the Euclidean form in dimension $5$,

$$
Cl^+_{5,0} \cong Cl_{4,0} \cong M_2(\mathbb{H}), \qquad \operatorname{Spin}(5) \cong Sp(2),
$$

the compact symplectic group of rank $2$. The spinor module has complex dimension $4$ and is of quaternionic type, consistent with $p - q = 5 \equiv 5 \pmod 8$.

For the Euclidean form in dimension $6$,

$$
Cl^+_{6,0} \cong M_4(\mathbb{C}), \qquad \operatorname{Spin}(6) \cong SU(4).
$$

The spinor module has complex dimension $8$ and splits into two half-spin modules of complex dimension $4$:

$$
\Delta = \Delta^+\oplus\Delta^-, \qquad \Delta^+ \cong 4, \quad \Delta^- \cong \bar{4},
$$

the defining representation of $SU(4)$ and its conjugate. The half-spin representations are of complex type, consistent with $p-q = 6 \equiv 6 \pmod 8$, and the double cover $\operatorname{Spin}(6) \to SO(6)$ is the covering $SU(4) \to SO(6)$.

For Lorentzian signatures the corresponding isomorphisms are $\operatorname{Spin}(2,1) \cong SL(2,\mathbb{R})$, $\operatorname{Spin}(5,1) \cong SL(2,\mathbb{H})$, and $\operatorname{Spin}(4,1) \cong Sp(1,1)$.

# Part IV: Spinors on Manifolds

## 14. Spin structures

The constructions of Part I are pointwise algebraic. To put spinors on a manifold one must globalize the double cover, and this is not always possible.

Let $(M,g)$ be an oriented Riemannian manifold of dimension $n \geq 2$, and let $P_{SO}(M) \to M$ be its bundle of oriented orthonormal frames, a principal $SO(n)$-bundle. A **spin structure** on $M$ is a principal $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$-bundle

$$
P_{\operatorname{Spin}}(M) \longrightarrow M
$$

together with a two-to-one bundle map $P_{\operatorname{Spin}}(M) \to P_{SO}(M)$ that restricts to the covering $\operatorname{Spin}(n) \to SO(n)$ on each fibre. Equivalently, a spin structure is a lift of the structure group of the tangent bundle through the double cover $\operatorname{Spin}(n) \to SO(n)$.

**Existence.** The obstruction is the second Stiefel--Whitney class. An oriented Riemannian manifold admits a spin structure if and only if

$$
w_2(TM) = 0 \in H^2(M; \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}).
$$

When a spin structure exists, the set of all spin structures is a torsor over $H^1(M;\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})$: there is no canonical choice, and the group acts freely and transitively. A manifold that admits a spin structure is called a **spin manifold**.

**spin$^{\,c}$ structures.** Replacing $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ by $\operatorname{Spin}^{c}(n) = (\operatorname{Spin}(n)\times U(1))/\{\pm1\}$ gives the weaker notion of a **spin$^{\,c}$ structure**; it exists whenever $w_2(TM)$ is the mod-$2$ reduction of an integral class, and in particular on every almost complex manifold.

## 15. The spinor bundle and the Dirac operator

Assume that $(M,g)$ is a spin manifold of dimension $n$ with a chosen spin structure, and let $\Delta$ be the spinor module of $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$. The **spinor bundle** is the associated vector bundle

$$
S = P_{\operatorname{Spin}}(M) \times_{\operatorname{Spin}(n)} \Delta \longrightarrow M .
$$

Its sections are the **spinor fields** on $M$. For even $n$ the half-spin decomposition of the module globalizes, and $S$ splits as

$$
S = S^+ \oplus S^- .
$$

**Clifford multiplication and the spin connection.** The Clifford action globalizes to a bundle map $c : TM \otimes S \to S$, $X\otimes\psi\mapsto X\cdot\psi$, with $X\cdot(X\cdot\psi) = g(X,X)\psi$, the sign convention inherited from $v^2 = Q(v)$; the Levi-Civita connection lifts, because the double cover is a local isomorphism of Lie algebras, to a covariant derivative $\nabla$ on $S$.

**The Dirac operator.** The **Dirac operator** is the first-order differential operator

$$
D = \sum_{i=1}^{n} c(e_i)\, \nabla_{e_i} : \Gamma(S) \longrightarrow \Gamma(S),
$$

where $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ is a local orthonormal frame. It is independent of the frame, formally self-adjoint, and elliptic. For even $n$ it is odd with respect to chirality and therefore has the block form

$$
D = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & D^- \\ D^+ & 0 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad D^\pm : \Gamma(S^\pm) \longrightarrow \Gamma(S^\mp).
$$

**The Lichnerowicz formula.** The square of the Dirac operator is the spin Laplacian plus a curvature term:

$$
D^2 = \nabla^*\nabla + \tfrac{1}{4}\operatorname{Scal},
$$

where $\operatorname{Scal}$ is the scalar curvature of $g$ and the sign of the curvature term is convention-dependent. The formula shows that a metric of positive scalar curvature forces the Dirac operator to be invertible, and is the starting point of the index-theoretic applications.

**The index.** For even $n$ and closed $M$, the chiral Dirac operator $D^+$ is Fredholm, and the Atiyah--Singer index theorem gives $\operatorname{ind}(D^+) = \int_M \hat{A}(M)$, where $\hat{A}(M)$ is the $\hat{A}$-class of $M$. The index is constant under deformation of the metric and vanishes when $M$ admits a metric of positive scalar curvature; for odd $n$ it is zero.

**Dimension and signature.** The chirality splitting $S = S^+\oplus S^-$ exists only for even $n$; in odd dimensions there is no Weyl spinor field and no chiral Dirac operator, although $D$ is still defined. The real structure of the spinor bundle, and hence the existence of Majorana fields, is governed by the signature modulo $8$, by the table of §8.

# Part V: Summary

## 16. Summary

**Groups.** The Clifford group $\Gamma(W,Q)$ consists of the units $s$ with $sWs^{-1}\subseteq W$, and the grade-twisted adjoint $\rho(s)(v)=\alpha(s)vs^{-1}$ maps it onto $O(W,Q)$. The **Pin group** is the subgroup generated by the unit vectors, a double cover of $O(W,Q)$ with kernel $\{\pm1\}$; the **spin group** is its even part, a double cover of the identity component of $SO(W,Q)$, and for a definite form the image is all of it. The Lie algebra of the spin group is $\mathfrak{so}(W,Q)$, realized by the bivectors.

**Representations.** A spinor module is a simple module over the complexified Clifford algebra, of complex dimension $2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$; on it $-1$ acts as $-\mathrm{id}$, so the spin representation does not descend to the rotation group. For even $n$ the volume element gives the chirality decomposition $\Delta=\Delta^+\oplus\Delta^-$ with $\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\Delta^\pm = 2^{\,n/2-1}$, whose elements are the Weyl spinors; for odd $n$ there is no such splitting.

**Spinor types.** A Dirac spinor lies in $\Delta$, a Weyl spinor in $\Delta^\pm$, and a Majorana spinor in a real form of the spinor module. The real, complex or quaternionic type is governed by the signature modulo $8$, as in the table of §8; in Lorentzian signature, Majorana--Weyl spinors exist exactly for spacetime dimension $D\equiv 2 \pmod 8$.

**Low dimensions and manifolds.** $\operatorname{Spin}(3)\cong SU(2)$, $\operatorname{Spin}(4)\cong SU(2)\times SU(2)$, $\operatorname{Spin}(6)\cong SU(4)$ and $\operatorname{Spin}(3,1)\cong SL(2,\mathbb{C})$; the real even Clifford algebra in signature $(1,3)$ is the biquaternion algebra $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$. An oriented Riemannian manifold admits a spin structure if and only if $w_2(TM)=0$, and the spin structures then form a torsor over $H^1(M;\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})$; the Dirac operator satisfies $D^2=\nabla^*\nabla+\tfrac{1}{4}\operatorname{Scal}$, and its chiral part has index $\int_M\hat{A}(M)$ on a closed even-dimensional spin manifold.
