
# __Lie Groups categorization__

## Introduction

A **Lie group** is a group $G$ that is also a smooth manifold, in such a way that the maps

$$
\mu : G \times G \to G, \qquad \iota : G \to G, \qquad \mu(x, y) = xy, \quad \iota(x) = x^{-1},
$$

are smooth. The general theory is in the companion article *Lie Groups*; this article classifies the objects of the family by structural properties, and assumes the definitions given there.

Two invariants run through everything: the **Lie algebra** $\mathfrak{g} = \mathrm{Lie}(G) = T_e G$, the tangent space at the identity with the bracket from the group commutator, which controls the local structure; and the **topology** of $G$ as a manifold, which controls the global structure. The topological data are the new information beyond the companion article *Lie Algebras: Categorization*.

Three running examples recur: $\mathrm{U}(1)$ (dimension $1$: compact, connected, abelian, not simply connected); $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ (dimension $3$: compact, connected, simple, simply connected); and the Heisenberg group $H_3$ (dimension $3$: nilpotent, non-abelian, non-compact, simply connected).

---

# Part I: Finite versus Positive-Dimensional Lie Groups

## 1. Dimension

The **dimension** of a Lie group is the dimension of its manifold, and it equals the dimension of its Lie algebra:

$$
\dim G = \dim \mathfrak{g}.
$$

Dimension is local, hence unchanged by passing to a covering group or a quotient by a discrete subgroup. It splits the family into two blocks.

## 2. Zero-dimensional Lie groups

A zero-dimensional manifold is discrete, and every map between discrete spaces is smooth, so the zero-dimensional Lie groups are exactly the discrete groups; with the usual second-countability convention they are countable. The Lie algebra is the zero algebra $\{0\}$, so a discrete group carries no infinitesimal information. Examples: the finite groups $\mathbb{Z}/n$ and $S_n$, and the infinite discrete groups $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Z}^n$, $F_2$, $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{Z})$.

## 3. Positive-dimensional Lie groups

A Lie group is **positive-dimensional** if $\dim G \ge 1$; its Lie algebra is then nonzero and determines it locally. Examples include the circle $\mathrm{U}(1)$ and the line $\mathbb{R}$ (dimension $1$), the tori $T^n$ and vector groups $\mathbb{R}^n$ (dimension $n$), $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ and $H_3$ (dimension $3$), and $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ and $\mathrm{SL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ (dimensions $n^2$ and $n^2 - 1$).

---

# Part II: Discrete Lie Groups

## 4. Place in the hierarchy

A **discrete Lie group** is a group with the discrete topology, viewed as a zero-dimensional Lie group. Every finite group is one, and a discrete Lie group is compact if and only if it is finite. Discrete groups sit at the bottom of both the dimensional and the algebraic hierarchy: their Lie algebra is abelian, so infinitesimally they are indistinguishable from the trivial group. Their role in Lie theory is as **subgroups** of positive-dimensional groups.

## 5. Lattices

A **lattice** in $G$ is a discrete subgroup $\Gamma \le G$ for which $G/\Gamma$ has finite volume; for example $\mathbb{Z}^n$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$, with $\mathbb{R}^n/\mathbb{Z}^n = T^n$.

## 6. Discrete abelian groups

A finitely generated abelian group $A \cong \mathbb{Z}^r \oplus (\text{finite})$, viewed as a discrete Lie group, is compact exactly when it is finite.

---

# Part III: Connected and Simply Connected Lie Groups

## 7. Connectedness

A Lie group is **connected** if it has no proper nonempty open and closed subset. The **identity component** $G^0$ is an open, closed, normal subgroup and

$$
\pi_0(G) = G/G^0
$$

is discrete, so every Lie group is an extension of a discrete group by a connected one. A connected Lie group is generated by the image of the exponential map, $G = \langle \exp \mathfrak{g} \rangle$, so for connected groups

$$
G \text{ is abelian} \iff \mathfrak{g} \text{ is abelian}
$$

(if $\mathfrak{g}$ is abelian, $\exp$ is a homomorphism onto $G$ from the vector group $\mathfrak{g}$). Connectedness is essential: the discrete group $S_3$ has abelian Lie algebra but is not abelian.

## 8. Simply connected groups and universal covers

A connected Lie group is **simply connected** if $\pi_1(G) = 0$. Every connected $G$ has a **universal covering group** $\widetilde{G}$, simply connected, with a covering homomorphism $\widetilde{G} \to G$ whose kernel is $\pi_1(G)$. The fundamental group of a Lie group is abelian, and the kernel of a covering of connected Lie groups is discrete and central.

## 9. The correspondence with Lie algebras

**Lie's third theorem.** Every finite-dimensional real Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is the Lie algebra of a Lie group.

Together with covering-space theory this gives the precise correspondence:

> For every finite-dimensional real Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ there is, up to isomorphism, exactly one simply connected Lie group $\widetilde{G}$ with $\mathrm{Lie}(\widetilde{G}) = \mathfrak{g}$. Every connected Lie group with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is a quotient $\widetilde{G}/N$ by a discrete central subgroup $N \le Z(\widetilde{G})$.

So the connected groups with a fixed Lie algebra are the quotients of one simply connected group by discrete central subgroups, governed by its center. For example:

- $\mathfrak{sl}(2, \mathbb{R})$: the simply connected group $\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}}(2, \mathbb{R})$ has infinite cyclic center; its quotients include $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{R})$ and $\mathrm{PSL}(2, \mathbb{R}) = \mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{R})/\{\pm I\}$.

## 10. Fundamental groups

The standard values include $\pi_1(\mathrm{U}(1)) = \mathbb{Z}$, $\pi_1(T^n) = \mathbb{Z}^n$, $\pi_1(\mathrm{SO}(2)) = \mathbb{Z}$, $\pi_1(\mathrm{SO}(n)) = \mathbb{Z}/2$ for $n \ge 3$, $\pi_1(\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{R})) = \mathbb{Z}$, $\pi_1(\mathrm{SL}(n, \mathbb{R})) = \mathbb{Z}/2$ for $n \ge 3$, and $\pi_1 = 0$ for $\mathbb{R}^n$, $\mathrm{SU}(n)$, $\mathrm{Sp}(n)$, $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ and $\mathrm{SL}(n, \mathbb{C})$.

---

# Part IV: Compact Lie Groups

## 11. Definition and examples

A Lie group is **compact** if its underlying space is compact; it then has finitely many components. Examples are the finite groups, the tori $T^n$, the orthogonal, unitary, symplectic and spin groups, and the compact real forms of the exceptional groups; the vector groups $\mathbb{R}^n$, $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$, $\mathrm{SL}(n, \mathbb{R})$, $\mathrm{SL}(n, \mathbb{C})$, $H_3$ and $\mathrm{Sp}(2n, \mathbb{R})$ are not compact.

## 12. Structure

A compact Lie group admits a **bi-invariant Riemannian metric**, obtained by averaging a left-invariant metric with the Haar measure, which is finite exactly because the group is compact; and every compact Lie group is a **matrix group**, embedding in some $\mathrm{U}(n)$.

> A connected compact nilpotent Lie group is a torus, hence abelian; indeed a connected compact **solvable** Lie group is already a torus.

## 13. Compactness is not a Lie-algebra invariant

The real Lie algebras $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ and $\mathfrak{sl}(2, \mathbb{R})$ are not isomorphic, yet both complexify to $\mathfrak{sl}(2, \mathbb{C})$; the first is the Lie algebra of the compact group $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, the second of the non-compact group $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{R})$. Over $\mathbb{R}$ compactness is detected by the Killing form: a real Lie algebra is that of a compact group exactly when it is reductive and its Killing form is negative semidefinite (Cartan's criterion).

---

# Part V: Abelian Lie Groups

## 14. Definition and classification

A Lie group is **abelian** if $xy = yx$ for all $x, y$; for connected groups this is equivalent to $[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}] = 0$, by §7. The connected abelian Lie groups are classified:

> Every connected abelian Lie group is isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^n \times T^k$ with $n + k = \dim G$. It is compact exactly when $n = 0$, simply connected exactly when $k = 0$, and a torus exactly when it is compact.

Here $\exp : \mathfrak{g} \to G$ is a surjective homomorphism with kernel a discrete subgroup $\Lambda$, and $G \cong \mathfrak{g}/\Lambda$; the groups $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathrm{U}(1)$ share the Lie algebra $\mathbb{R}$ but are not isomorphic. Combined with §6, this completes the abelian classification.

---

# Part VI: Nilpotent Lie Groups

## 15. Definition and examples

With the lower central series $G_0 = G$, $G_{i+1} = [G, G_i]$, a group is **nilpotent** if some $G_i = \{e\}$. For a connected Lie group,

$$
G \text{ nilpotent} \iff \mathfrak{g} \text{ is nilpotent}.
$$

Abelian groups are nilpotent of class $1$, and the class-$2$ groups are the first non-abelian ones. The standard examples are:

- the abelian groups;
- the **Heisenberg group** $H_3$ of upper unitriangular real $3 \times 3$ matrices, of dimension $3$:
  $$
  H_3 = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & x & z \\ 0 & 1 & y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} : x, y, z \in \mathbb{R} \right\},
  $$
  nilpotent of class $2$, with one-dimensional center, non-compact and simply connected; its Lie algebra $\mathfrak{h}_3$ has basis $X, Y, Z$ with $[X, Y] = Z$ and $[X, Z] = [Y, Z] = 0$;

## 16. Structure

> If $G$ is connected, simply connected and nilpotent, then $\exp : \mathfrak{g} \to G$ is a diffeomorphism; in particular $G$ is diffeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^n$.

So every non-abelian connected nilpotent Lie group is non-compact, since a compact connected nilpotent group is a torus and hence abelian. The inclusions

$$
\text{abelian} \subsetneq \text{nilpotent} \subsetneq \text{solvable}
$$

are strict, and Engel's theorem is the infinitesimal counterpart: $\mathfrak{g}$ is nilpotent if and only if every $\mathrm{ad}_X$ is nilpotent.

---

# Part VII: Solvable Lie Groups

## 17. Definition, examples and structure

With the derived series $G^{(0)} = G$, $G^{(i+1)} = [G^{(i)}, G^{(i)}]$, a group is **solvable** if some $G^{(i)} = \{e\}$. For a connected Lie group,

$$
G \text{ solvable} \iff \mathfrak{g} \text{ is solvable}.
$$

Every nilpotent group is solvable, strictly so. Examples: the **affine group** $\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{R})$ of transformations $x \mapsto ax + b$, $a \ne 0$, of dimension $2$ (solvable, not nilpotent, non-compact); the **upper triangular group** $B(n, \mathbb{R})$, of dimension $n(n+1)/2$ (solvable, non-compact, non-nilpotent for $n \ge 2$); and the groups $\mathbb{R}^n \rtimes \mathbb{R}$ with the second factor acting by scalings.

> If $G$ is connected, simply connected and solvable, then $\exp : \mathfrak{g} \to G$ is a diffeomorphism, so $G$ is diffeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^n$.

Hence a connected compact solvable Lie group is a torus and abelian, and no non-abelian connected solvable group is compact.

Solvable and semisimple algebras are complementary, by the **Levi decomposition**: every finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a field of characteristic zero is a semidirect product

$$
\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{r} \rtimes \mathfrak{s},
$$

with $\mathfrak{r}$ the solvable radical and $\mathfrak{s}$ semisimple. The cases $\mathfrak{s} = 0$ (solvable) and $\mathfrak{r} = 0$ (semisimple) are the two ends of the classification, and solvable and semisimple algebras meet only in the trivial algebra.

---

# Part VIII: Semisimple Lie Groups

## 18. Definition and examples

A Lie algebra is **semisimple** if it has no nonzero solvable ideal; equivalently, by Cartan's criterion, its Killing form

$$
\kappa(X, Y) = \mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{ad}_X \circ \mathrm{ad}_Y)
$$

is nondegenerate; equivalently, it is a direct sum of simple Lie algebras. A connected Lie group is **semisimple** if its Lie algebra is.

**Semisimple:** $\mathrm{SL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ and $\mathrm{SL}(n, \mathbb{C})$ for $n \ge 2$; $\mathrm{SU}(n)$ for $n \ge 2$; $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ for $n \ge 3$; $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$; $\mathrm{Sp}(2n, \mathbb{R})$ and $\mathrm{Sp}(n)$; and $\mathrm{SO}^+(1, 3) \cong \mathrm{PSL}(2, \mathbb{C})$. Here $\mathfrak{sl}(n)$ is simple for $n \ge 2$ and $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ for $n \ge 3$, $n \ne 4$; the exception is $\mathfrak{so}(4) \cong \mathfrak{so}(3) \oplus \mathfrak{so}(3)$, semisimple but not simple.

**Not semisimple:** every positive-dimensional abelian algebra, e.g. $\mathbb{R}$; every nonzero nilpotent or solvable algebra, e.g. $\mathfrak{h}_3$; $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$, with positive-dimensional center; and the Euclidean group $\mathbb{R}^n \rtimes \mathrm{SO}(n)$, which contains the abelian ideal $\mathbb{R}^n$.

## 19. Structure

For connected $G$, semisimplicity of $\mathfrak{g}$, absence of a nontrivial connected solvable normal subgroup, and nondegeneracy of the Killing form are equivalent. A connected semisimple group has discrete center, and its adjoint group is a product of simple adjoint groups; if it is also compact, its center and fundamental group are finite and its universal cover is compact.

---

# Part IX: Simple Lie Groups

## 20. Definition and the adjoint group

A Lie algebra is **simple** if it is non-abelian and has no nonzero proper ideals. A connected Lie group is a **simple Lie group** if its Lie algebra is simple, equivalently if it is non-abelian and has no nontrivial connected normal subgroups.

The group notion is finer than the algebra one: a simple Lie algebra may integrate to a group with a nontrivial finite center (e.g. $\mathrm{SU}(n)$ has center $\mathbb{Z}/n$), which is a normal subgroup in the abstract sense; some authors say **almost simple** for the Lie-theoretic notion used here.

For a connected simple $G$, the quotient $G_{\mathrm{ad}} = G/Z(G)$ is again simple, the **adjoint group**, and is the smallest group with the given algebra; the simply connected group is the largest. Examples:

- $\mathfrak{su}(2) \cong \mathfrak{so}(3)$: simply connected group $\mathrm{SU}(2) = S^3$; adjoint group $\mathrm{SO}(3) = \mathrm{SU}(2)/\{\pm I\} = \mathrm{PSU}(2)$. The covering is the two-to-one spin covering.
- $\mathfrak{sl}(2, \mathbb{R})$: the simply connected group has infinite cyclic center; the adjoint group is $\mathrm{PSL}(2, \mathbb{R})$, the connected isometry group of the hyperbolic plane.

Simple algebras are the building blocks of semisimple ones, by the direct-sum decomposition of §18.

---

# Part X: Reductive Lie Groups

## 21. Definition and examples

A Lie algebra is **reductive** if its adjoint representation is completely reducible, equivalently if its radical equals its center, equivalently if

$$
\mathfrak{g} = [\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}] \oplus \mathfrak{z}(\mathfrak{g})
$$

with $[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}]$ semisimple. A connected Lie group is **reductive** if its algebra is, so a reductive algebra is semisimple plus abelian center. Examples are $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$, $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{C})$ and $\mathrm{U}(n)$ (reductive but not semisimple), and every connected compact Lie group. Reductive groups are the natural setting for representation theory, since their finite-dimensional representations are completely reducible. The hierarchy is

$$
\text{simple} \subsetneq \text{semisimple} \subsetneq \text{reductive}.
$$

---

# Part XI: The Classical Matrix Groups

## 22. The four families

The classical groups are the groups preserving a nondegenerate bilinear or sesquilinear form. The simple ones fall into four infinite families, which over $\mathbb{C}$ are

$$
A_n : \mathrm{SL}(n+1, \mathbb{C}), \quad
B_n : \mathrm{SO}(2n+1, \mathbb{C}), \quad
C_n : \mathrm{Sp}(2n, \mathbb{C}), \quad
D_n : \mathrm{SO}(2n, \mathbb{C}).
$$

| Family | Complex group | $\dim_{\mathbb{C}}$ | Compact real form | $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $A_{n-1}$ | $\mathrm{SL}(n, \mathbb{C})$ | $n^2 - 1$ | $\mathrm{SU}(n)$ | $n^2 - 1$ |
| $B_n$ | $\mathrm{SO}(2n+1, \mathbb{C})$ | $n(2n+1)$ | $\mathrm{SO}(2n+1)$ | $n(2n+1)$ |
| $C_n$ | $\mathrm{Sp}(2n, \mathbb{C})$ | $n(2n+1)$ | $\mathrm{Sp}(n)$ | $n(2n+1)$ |
| $D_n$ | $\mathrm{SO}(2n, \mathbb{C})$ | $n(2n-1)$ | $\mathrm{SO}(2n)$ | $n(2n-1)$ |

The compact symplectic group $\mathrm{Sp}(n)$, of quaternionic unitary matrices, is the compact real form of $\mathrm{Sp}(2n, \mathbb{C})$; it differs from the non-compact real group $\mathrm{Sp}(2n, \mathbb{R})$.

## 23. The real groups with their dimensions

The real dimensions of the standard groups are $\dim \mathrm{SO}(n) = n(n-1)/2$, $\dim \mathrm{SU}(n) = n^2 - 1$, $\dim \mathrm{Sp}(n) = n(2n+1)$, $\dim \mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R}) = n^2$, $\dim \mathrm{SL}(n, \mathbb{R}) = n^2 - 1$ and $\dim \mathrm{Sp}(2n, \mathbb{R}) = n(2n+1)$; the first three groups are compact, the last three are not.

# Part XII: The Exceptional Groups

## 24. The five exceptions

The classification of simple Lie algebras over $\mathbb{C}$ by root systems and Dynkin diagrams yields four infinite families and exactly five exceptions $G_2, F_4, E_6, E_7, E_8$. Each has a unique compact real form, hence a simply connected compact simple Lie group, and also non-compact real forms. Their dimensions are:

| Exceptional group | $G_2$ | $F_4$ | $E_6$ | $E_7$ | $E_8$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dimension | $14$ | $52$ | $78$ | $133$ | $248$ |

The exceptional groups follow the general pattern: each is simple, hence semisimple and reductive; the compact forms are compact, connected and simply connected, the non-compact forms are not. All are matrix groups, but they do not preserve a single classical form as the families $A$–$D$ do.

---

# Part XIII: Real versus Complex Lie Groups

## 25. Complex Lie groups

A **complex Lie group** is a complex manifold whose group operations are holomorphic. A complex Lie group of complex dimension $d$ is a real Lie group of real dimension $2d$. Examples: $\mathbb{C}^n$; $\mathbb{C}^\times$; $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{C})$ and $\mathrm{SL}(n, \mathbb{C})$; $\mathrm{SO}(n, \mathbb{C})$; $\mathrm{Sp}(2n, \mathbb{C})$; and the **complex tori** $\mathbb{C}^n/\Lambda$, compact connected abelian complex Lie groups.

## 26. Real forms

A **real form** of a complex Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is a real Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}_0$ with $\mathfrak{g}_0 \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C} \cong \mathfrak{g}$.

Real forms can be topologically very different: $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ and $\mathfrak{sl}(2, \mathbb{R})$ are real forms of $\mathfrak{sl}(2, \mathbb{C})$, the first compact, the second non-compact (the split form), and $\mathfrak{so}(n+1)$ and $\mathfrak{so}(1, n)$ are real forms of $\mathfrak{so}(n+1, \mathbb{C})$.

**Compact real form theorem.** Every semisimple complex Lie algebra has a compact real form, unique up to conjugacy; this is the algebraic basis of Weyl's unitary trick. In the series, complexification relates $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ to $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{C})$, the group of unit biquaternions.

# Part XIV: Lie Groups as Matrix Groups

## 27. Ado's theorem and Lie's third theorem

A **matrix group** (linear Lie group) is a closed embedded Lie subgroup of some $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ or $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{C})$; by Cartan's theorem every closed subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ is a Lie group. The classical and exceptional groups are matrix groups.

**Ado's theorem.** Every finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a field of characteristic zero — in particular every real or complex Lie algebra — is isomorphic to a Lie subalgebra of $\mathfrak{gl}(n, k)$ for some $n$; that is, every such algebra is a **matrix Lie algebra**.

**Lie's third theorem.** Every finite-dimensional real Lie algebra is the Lie algebra of a Lie group, and hence (by §9) of a unique simply connected one.

Combining them: Ado gives a faithful representation $\mathfrak{g} \hookrightarrow \mathfrak{gl}(n, \mathbb{R})$, and Lie's third theorem gives the simply connected $\widetilde{G}$ with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$; integrating the representation produces a matrix group with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$. Therefore:

> Every finite-dimensional real Lie algebra is the Lie algebra of some matrix Lie group.

## 28. Not every Lie group is a matrix group

The statement above concerns Lie algebras, not groups. A Lie group is **linear** if it admits a faithful finite-dimensional representation. The algebra $\mathfrak{sl}(2, \mathbb{R})$ is linear and integrates to $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{R})$ and $\mathrm{PSL}(2, \mathbb{R})$, but its universal cover $\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}}(2, \mathbb{R})$ is **not** linear — it has no faithful finite-dimensional representation, the standard example of a non-matrix connected Lie group. Every connected compact Lie group is linear (§12), and every simply connected nilpotent Lie group is linear.

# Part XV: The Invariants that Separate the Classes

## 29. Dimension, topology, and the Lie algebra

**Dimension** is the crudest invariant: it separates the discrete groups ($\dim G = 0$) from the positive-dimensional ones, is additive, and for a connected group equals $\dim \mathfrak{g}$.

**Topology** supplies the invariants invisible to the Lie algebra. The component group $\pi_0(G) = G/G^0$ is finite for compact $G$; the abelian $\pi_1(G)$ records the failure of simple connectivity and, for connected compact $G$, has the form $\mathbb{Z}^k \oplus F$ with $F$ finite. Compactness is not determined by the complex Lie algebra: $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ and $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{R})$ share the complexification $\mathfrak{sl}(2, \mathbb{C})$ but only the first is compact; over $\mathbb{R}$, a Lie algebra is that of a compact group exactly when it is reductive and its Killing form is negative semidefinite.

**The Lie algebra** determines, for connected groups, the whole algebraic classification: dimension, and whether the group is abelian, nilpotent, solvable, semisimple, simple, or reductive. It does not determine the group: a fixed $\mathfrak{g}$ integrates to $\widetilde{G}$ and to its central quotients, and $G \cong \widetilde{G}/\pi_1(G)$. Classifying Lie groups thus splits into classifying Lie algebras (companion article) and classifying discrete central subgroups of the simply connected groups.

# Summary

## 30. The hierarchy of classes

**Dimensional.** Finite $\subsetneq$ discrete (zero-dimensional) $\subsetneq$ all Lie groups.

**Algebraic** (for connected groups):

$$
\text{abelian} \subsetneq \text{nilpotent} \subsetneq \text{solvable} \subsetneq \text{all},
$$

$$
\text{simple} \subsetneq \text{semisimple} \subsetneq \text{reductive} \subsetneq \text{all}.
$$

The solvable and semisimple branches meet only in the trivial group, and the Levi decomposition builds every finite-dimensional real Lie algebra from a solvable and a semisimple part.

**Topological.** Compact connected $\subsetneq$ connected $\subsetneq$ all; the simply connected groups are the opposite extreme of the covering relation. A connected compact group is a finite quotient of $T^k \times S$ with $S$ simply connected compact semisimple.

## 31. Summary table

| Lie group | $\dim$ | Compact | Connected | Simply conn. | Abelian | Nilpotent | Solvable | Semisimple |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\mathrm{U}(1) = T^1$ | $1$ | yes | yes | no | yes | yes | yes | no |
| $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ | $n^2$ | no | no | no | no | no | no | no (reductive) |
| $\mathrm{SO}(n)$, $n \ge 3$ | $n(n-1)/2$ | yes | yes | no | no | no | no | yes |
| $\mathrm{SU}(n)$, $n \ge 2$ | $n^2 - 1$ | yes | yes | yes | no | no | no | yes |
| $E_8$ (compact form) | $248$ | yes | yes | yes | no | no | no | yes |

## 32. Standard examples of the family

- **The unit quaternions** $\mathrm{SU}(2) \cong \mathrm{Sp}(1) \cong S^3$, of dimension $3$: compact, connected, simple, simply connected; they double-cover the rotation group, $\mathrm{SU}(2) \to \mathrm{SO}(3)$.
- **The special linear group** $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{C})$, of real dimension $6$: the group of unit biquaternions, isomorphic to the norm-one elements of the biquaternion algebra $\mathbb{B} \cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$, and the complexification of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$.
- **The Lorentz group** $\mathrm{SO}^+(1, 3) \cong \mathrm{PSL}(2, \mathbb{C})$, of real dimension $6$: non-compact, simple, not simply connected, with universal cover $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{C})$.
- **The biquaternion units** $\mathbb{B}^\times \cong \mathrm{GL}(2, \mathbb{C})$, of real dimension $8$: reductive, non-compact, connected; $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{C})$ is the norm-one subgroup.

The passage from the biquaternion algebra to its groups of units and norm-one elements, and the exponential map that realizes it, is treated in the companion article *Biquaternion Exponential and Lie Group Structure*.
