
# __Exterior Algebras categorization__

## Introduction

This article classifies the algebras of the exterior algebra family by their structural properties: the exterior algebra $\Lambda(M)$, the exterior powers $\Lambda^k(M)$ as functors, and the neighbouring quotients of the tensor algebra — the symmetric algebra, the Clifford algebra, and the universal enveloping algebra — together with the graded algebra attached to a filtered one.

The organizing idea is that $T(M)$ is free and every class in the family is a quotient of a tensor algebra by relations of a definite shape; the exterior algebra is the quotient in which every generator squares to zero. It is simultaneously

- the universal quotient of $T(M)$ with $v \wedge v = 0$;
- the free graded-commutative algebra on $M$;
- the Clifford algebra of the zero quadratic form, $\Lambda(M) = \mathrm{Cl}(M, 0)$;
- the associated graded algebra of every Clifford algebra, $\operatorname{gr}\mathrm{Cl}(M, Q) \cong \Lambda(M)$.

Two dichotomies organize the classification.

**Homogeneous versus inhomogeneous relations.** Relations generated by homogeneous elements give graded algebras: the symmetric and exterior algebras. Relations that mix degrees give only filtered quotients, whose associated graded algebras are graded: the Clifford and enveloping algebras.

**Commutative, graded-commutative, free.** The symmetric algebra is the free commutative algebra; the exterior algebra is the free graded-commutative algebra; neither is the tensor algebra, which imposes no relations at all. The exterior algebra is the unique member of the trio that is finite-dimensional when the underlying module is a finite-dimensional vector space.

Conventions. Throughout, $R$ is a commutative ring with identity and $M$ is an $R$-module, free of finite rank $n$ when a rank is mentioned. We use **rank** over a general commutative ring and **dimension** over a field; $V$ denotes a module over a field, that is, a vector space. The general theory of the tensor algebra is in *Tensor Algebras*, and the classification of its quotients is in *Tensor Algebras: Categorization*; the siblings *Symmetric Algebras* and *Clifford Algebras* treat the other quotients of the family.

# Part I: The Exterior Algebra

## 1. Definition and Defining Relation

The **exterior algebra** of $M$ is the quotient of the tensor algebra

$$
\Lambda(M) = T(M) \Big/ \big( v \otimes v : v \in M \big),
$$

the two-sided ideal being generated by all $v \otimes v$. The product is written $\wedge$, so the class of $m_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes m_k$ is $m_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge m_k$. The defining relation is

$$
v \wedge v = 0, \qquad v \in M,
$$

the **alternating** condition. Substituting $v = u + w$ and using bilinearity gives $u \wedge w + w \wedge u = 0$, hence

$$
u \wedge w = - w \wedge u
$$

holds in every exterior algebra. Conversely, antisymmetry implies $2 (v \wedge v) = 0$, and this recovers $v \wedge v = 0$ only when $2$ is invertible in $R$. The exterior algebra is therefore defined by the alternating condition, which over a general commutative ring is strictly stronger than antisymmetry; the two agree over a field of characteristic not $2$ (§13).

The relation is homogeneous of degree two, so the ideal is graded and the quotient inherits the grading of $T(M)$:

$$
\Lambda(M) = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} \Lambda^k(M), \qquad \Lambda^0(M) = R, \qquad \Lambda^1(M) = M,
$$

with $\Lambda^k(M) \wedge \Lambda^l(M) \subseteq \Lambda^{k+l}(M)$. The module $\Lambda^k(M)$ is the **$k$-th exterior power** of $M$. Thus $\Lambda(M)$ is connected graded, generated by its degree-one part $M$.

## 2. Universal Property

**Universal property.** Let $A$ be a unital associative $R$-algebra and let $f : M \to A$ be an $R$-linear map with $f(v)^2 = 0$ for all $v \in M$. Then there is a unique unital algebra homomorphism $F : \Lambda(M) \to A$ with $F(v) = f(v)$ on $M$.

Equivalently, for every $R$-module $P$ there is a natural isomorphism

$$
\operatorname{Hom}_R(\Lambda^k(M), P) \cong \operatorname{Alt}_R(M^k, P),
$$

where the right-hand side is the module of alternating $R$-multilinear maps, those vanishing whenever two arguments are equal. This is the functorial form of the universal property, and it is the reason alternating maps, not merely antisymmetric ones, are the natural maps out of exterior powers.

## 3. Grading and Graded-Commutativity

The exterior algebra is **graded-commutative**, also called supercommutative: for homogeneous $\alpha, \beta$,

$$
\alpha \wedge \beta = (-1)^{|\alpha| |\beta|} \beta \wedge \alpha.
$$

For odd elements this reads $u \wedge w = - w \wedge u$; for even elements it is ordinary commutation. For $M$ of rank at least $2$, $\Lambda(M)$ is not commutative, since $e_1 \wedge e_2 = - e_2 \wedge e_1 \neq e_2 \wedge e_1$ when $2$ is invertible. In characteristic $2$ the sign disappears and the exterior algebra is commutative (§13).

The grading is essential: the sign in the commutation rule is a function of the degrees, so $\Lambda(M)$ is an example of a graded-commutative algebra that is not commutative when $2$ is invertible. Supercommutativity is the sharpest single invariant separating it from the symmetric algebra when $2$ is invertible (§14).

## 4. Size: Rank, Basis, Hilbert Series

If $M$ is free with basis $(e_i)_{i \in I}$, then $\Lambda(M)$ is free with basis the products

$$
e_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{i_k}, \qquad i_1 < \cdots < i_k, \quad k \geq 0,
$$

the empty product being $1$. Hence $\Lambda^k(M)$ has a basis indexed by the $k$-element subsets of $I$. When $|I| = n$ is finite,

$$
\operatorname{rank} \Lambda^k(M) = \binom{n}{k}, \qquad \Lambda^k(M) = 0 \text{ for } k > n, \qquad \operatorname{rank} \Lambda(M) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} = 2^n,
$$

and $\Lambda^n(M)$ is free of rank one. Over a field this reads $\dim_F \Lambda(V) = 2^n$, recorded by the Hilbert–Poincaré series

$$
\sum_{k \geq 0} \dim_F \Lambda^k(V)\, t^k = (1 + t)^n.
$$

The finiteness is a structural dividing line of the family: for $V$ of dimension $n \geq 1$, $\Lambda(V)$ is finite-dimensional of dimension $2^n$, while $T(V)$ and $\operatorname{Sym}(V)$ are infinite-dimensional (§19).

## 5. Nilpotence, Zero Divisors, Local Structure

When $M$ is free of rank $n$, the ideal $\Lambda^+(M) = \bigoplus_{k \geq 1} \Lambda^k(M)$ is nilpotent, because any product of $n+1$ elements of positive degree has degree at least $n+1$ and therefore vanishes:

$$
\big( \Lambda^+(M) \big)^{n+1} = 0.
$$

In particular every element of positive degree is nilpotent, and, since $0 \neq e_1 \in M$ satisfies $e_1 \wedge e_1 = 0$, the exterior algebra of a nonzero free module of finite rank is never a domain. Over a field and for $V$ finite-dimensional, $\Lambda^+(V)$ is the unique maximal ideal, so $\Lambda(V)$ is a **local ring** with residue field $F$; the nilpotence of $\Lambda^+(V)$ is what makes the algebra finite-dimensional.

These properties fail for general modules: the vanishing of $\Lambda^k(M)$ above the rank, and hence the nilpotence of $\Lambda^+$, uses that $M$ is free of finite rank (§12).

## 6. First Examples

- $\Lambda(R^0) = R$.
- For $M = R$ with generator $t$, $\Lambda(R) = R \oplus Rt$ with $t^2 = 0$: the algebra of **dual numbers**, of rank $2$.
- For $M = R^2$ with basis $e_1, e_2$, the products $1, e_1, e_2, e_1 \wedge e_2$ form a basis, so $\Lambda(R^2)$ has rank $4$; and $(e_1 \wedge e_2)^2 = 0$, since it has degree $4 > 2$. When $2$ is invertible it is not commutative: $e_1 \wedge e_2 = - e_2 \wedge e_1$.
- For $V = \mathbb{R}^3$, $\Lambda(V)$ is $8$-dimensional, with $\Lambda^1(V)$ and $\Lambda^2(V)$ both of dimension $3$; the composite $\mathbb{R}^3 \times \mathbb{R}^3 \to \Lambda^2(\mathbb{R}^3) \cong \mathbb{R}^3$ is the cross product, and the identification is the Hodge star. Dually, $\Lambda^k(V^*)$ is the space of alternating $k$-forms on $V$.

# Part II: The Exterior Powers as Functors

## 7. Functoriality

For an $R$-linear map $f : M \to N$, the alternating multilinear map

$$
(m_1, \ldots, m_k) \mapsto f(m_1) \wedge \cdots \wedge f(m_k)
$$

induces, by the universal property of §2, a unique $R$-linear map

$$
\Lambda^k(f) : \Lambda^k(M) \to \Lambda^k(N).
$$

The assignments $f \mapsto \Lambda^k(f)$ satisfy $\Lambda^k(\operatorname{id}_M) = \operatorname{id}_{\Lambda^k(M)}$ and $\Lambda^k(g \circ f) = \Lambda^k(g) \circ \Lambda^k(f)$, so $\Lambda^k$ is a functor from $R$-modules to $R$-modules. Here $\Lambda^0$ is the constant functor $R$ and $\Lambda^1$ is the identity functor. The full exterior algebra is functorial as an algebra: $\Lambda$ is a functor to graded-commutative algebras.

## 8. Exactness and Non-Additivity

The functor $\Lambda^k$ is **right exact**: if $M' \to M \to M'' \to 0$ is exact, then so is

$$
\Lambda^k(M') \to \Lambda^k(M) \to \Lambda^k(M'') \to 0.
$$

This holds because $\Lambda^k$ is a quotient of the right exact functor $M \mapsto M^{\otimes k}$. It is not additive: the exterior powers are polynomial functors of degree $k$, and it is $\Lambda(M)$ as a whole, not each $\Lambda^k$ separately, that turns direct sums into tensor products. The correct additivity is the direct-sum formula

$$
\Lambda^k(M \oplus N) \cong \bigoplus_{i+j=k} \Lambda^i(M) \otimes_R \Lambda^j(N),
$$

and at the level of algebras the sharper statement

$$
\Lambda(M \oplus N) \cong \Lambda(M) \mathbin{\hat{\otimes}} \Lambda(N),
$$

the **graded** tensor product, since an odd element of $\Lambda(M)$ and an odd element of $\Lambda(N)$ anticommute in $\Lambda(M \oplus N)$ but would commute in the ordinary tensor product. The symmetric analogue uses the ordinary tensor product,

$$
\operatorname{Sym}(M \oplus N) \cong \operatorname{Sym}(M) \otimes_R \operatorname{Sym}(N);
$$

the Koszul sign is the exterior algebra's characteristic feature.

## 9. Base Change and Local Rank

Exterior powers commute with base change: for a commutative $R$-algebra $S$ there is a natural isomorphism

$$
\Lambda^k(M \otimes_R S) \cong \Lambda^k(M) \otimes_R S.
$$

Consequently the formation of exterior powers is compatible with localization. If $M$ is projective of finite rank $n$, then each $\Lambda^k(M)$ is projective and locally free of rank $\binom{n}{k}$, while $\Lambda(M)$ is projective of rank $2^n$. This local-rank description is the module-theoretic replacement for the dimension formula of §4.

## 10. Duality and the Top Power

For $M$ free of rank $n$ there is a pairing

$$
\Lambda^k(M) \times \Lambda^{n-k}(M) \to \Lambda^n(M), \qquad (\alpha, \beta) \mapsto \alpha \wedge \beta.
$$

Over a field this pairing is nondegenerate, yielding the duality $\Lambda^k(V) \cong \Lambda^{n-k}(V)^* \otimes \Lambda^n(V)$ and the symmetry $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-k}$. The top power is the home of the determinant: for $f : M \to M$ with $M$ free of rank $n$, the induced map $\Lambda^n(f)$ is multiplication by an element $\det(f) \in R$, and $f \mapsto \Lambda^n(f)$ is multiplicative, $\det(g \circ f) = \det(g) \det(f)$.

# Part III: Vector Spaces and Modules

## 11. The Field Case

Over a field $F$ every module is free, so $\Lambda(V)$ has dimension $2^n$ for $\dim_F V = n$, with basis the ordered wedge products, and the theory is uniform. Moreover, when $F$ has characteristic $0$, the exterior powers admit a second description. The symmetric group $S_k$ acts on $V^{\otimes k}$ by permuting the factors; its trivial and sign representations cut out the symmetric and antisymmetric parts,

$$
S^k(V) = \operatorname{im}\Big( \tfrac{1}{k!} \textstyle\sum_{\sigma \in S_k} \sigma \Big), \qquad \Lambda^k(V) = \operatorname{im}\Big( \tfrac{1}{k!} \textstyle\sum_{\sigma \in S_k} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\, \sigma \Big),
$$

and these are the two extreme isotypic components of $V^{\otimes k}$. This identifies the exterior algebra with the antisymmetric part of the tensor algebra, and the symmetric algebra with the symmetric part.

## 12. The Module Case

Over a general commutative ring the identification of §11 fails, and the quotient definition must be used. The differences are structural.

- **No rank.** A module need not be free, and when it is not, $\operatorname{rank} \Lambda(M)$ is not defined; $\Lambda^k(M)$ need not be free, and $\Lambda(M)$ need not have finite rank. For $M = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, $\Lambda^0(M) = \mathbb{Z}$, $\Lambda^1(M) = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, and $\Lambda^k(M) = 0$ for $k \geq 2$, because the single generator $e$ has $e \wedge e = 0$. Hence

$$
\Lambda(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z},
$$

a ring that is neither free nor finite of rank $2^1$ in the sense of §4. For $M = R/I$ cyclic one likewise finds $\Lambda(R/I) = R \oplus R/I$, with $\Lambda^k = 0$ for $k \geq 2$.
- **No division by $k!$.** The idempotent description of §11 requires $k!$ invertible. Over $\mathbb{Z}$, or over any ring of positive characteristic, the symmetric and alternating quotients are the correct objects; the symmetric and antisymmetric *parts* of $M^{\otimes k}$ can be different modules.
- **Projective modules.** If $M$ is projective of finite rank $n$, the rank formula survives locally, by §9: $\Lambda^k(M)$ is projective of local rank $\binom{n}{k}$.

## 13. Characteristic 2 and Non-Invertibility of 2

The alternating condition and antisymmetry differ precisely when $2$ is not invertible. Over a field of characteristic $2$ the sign $(-1)^{|\alpha||\beta|}$ is always $+1$, so the graded-commutative law becomes the commutative law and **$\Lambda(V)$ is commutative**; the basis of ordered wedge products is unchanged, so $\dim \Lambda(V) = 2^n$, and only the commutation type changes. In general, the exterior algebra is defined by $v \wedge v = 0$, while the antisymmetric quotient $T(M)/(u \otimes w + w \otimes u)$ is larger whenever $2$ is not invertible; the two coincide over a field of characteristic not $2$. Statements identifying the exterior algebra with the antisymmetric part of the tensor algebra are therefore characteristic-$0$ statements.

# Part IV: The Neighbouring Quotients

The exterior algebra is one of a small set of quotients of the tensor algebra; the others are obtained by changing the defining relation. The classification is the classification of these relations.

## 14. The Symmetric Algebra: The Commutative Counterpart

The **symmetric algebra** is

$$
\operatorname{Sym}(M) = T(M) \Big/ \big( v \otimes w - w \otimes v : v, w \in M \big),
$$

with defining relation $v w = w v$. It is commutative, and it is the **free commutative $R$-algebra** on $M$: every $R$-linear map $M \to A$ into a commutative unital algebra extends uniquely. For $M$ free of rank $n$, $\operatorname{Sym}(M) \cong R[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, with

$$
\operatorname{rank} \operatorname{Sym}^k(M) = \binom{n+k-1}{k}, \qquad \sum_{k \geq 0} \operatorname{rank} \operatorname{Sym}^k(M)\, t^k = \frac{1}{(1-t)^n}.
$$

The contrast with the exterior algebra is the classification (§19): $\operatorname{Sym}(M)$ is commutative, infinite-dimensional for $n \geq 1$, and a domain when $R$ is a domain, whereas $\Lambda(M)$ is graded-commutative but not commutative when $2$ is invertible, finite-dimensional of dimension $2^n$, and nilpotent in positive degrees. Both are quotients of $T(M)$ generated by $M$ in degree one, but by different homogeneous quadratic relations: $v w - w v$ for the symmetric algebra and $v \wedge v$ for the exterior algebra. Equivalently, $\operatorname{Sym}(M)$ is the free commutative algebra, and $\Lambda(M)$ is the free graded-commutative algebra with all generators odd.

## 15. The Clifford Algebra: An Inhomogeneous Deformation

Let $Q : M \to R$ be a quadratic form with polar form $B$, so $Q(v) = B(v, v)$. The **Clifford algebra** is

$$
\mathrm{Cl}(M, Q) = T(M) \Big/ \big( v \otimes v - Q(v) \cdot 1 : v \in M \big),
$$

with defining relation $v^2 = Q(v) \cdot 1$, whose linearization is $u v + v u = 2 B(u, v) \cdot 1$ when $2$ is invertible. The relation is inhomogeneous as soon as $Q \neq 0$, so the ideal is not graded and $\mathrm{Cl}(M, Q)$ is not graded in the sense of §1; it is filtered, and it retains only a $\mathbb{Z}/2$-grading by parity. Two membership statements link it to the exterior algebra:

$$
\mathrm{Cl}(M, 0) = \Lambda(M), \qquad \operatorname{gr} \mathrm{Cl}(M, Q) \cong \Lambda(M) \text{ for every } Q.
$$

Thus the exterior algebra is both a member of the Clifford family, namely the zero form, and the associated graded algebra of every member, by the PBW basis theorem for Clifford algebras. The Clifford algebra is not supercommutative for $Q \neq 0$ when $2$ is invertible, since $u v + v u = 2 B(u, v) \neq 0$ in general, whereas the exterior algebra is; this separates $\mathrm{Cl}(M, Q)$ from $\Lambda(M)$ even when the two have the same rank (in characteristic $2$ both algebras are commutative, §13). For example, over $\mathbb{R}$ with $Q(e_1) = Q(e_2) = 1$ and $B(e_1, e_2) = 0$, one has $\mathrm{Cl}_{2,0}(\mathbb{R}) \cong M_2(\mathbb{R})$, of dimension $4$, with associated graded isomorphic to $\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^2)$ but $M_2(\mathbb{R})$ not isomorphic to it, since $M_2(\mathbb{R})$ is simple while $\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^2)$ has the nonzero nilpotent ideal $\Lambda^+$.

## 16. The Universal Enveloping Algebra

For a Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ over $R$, the **universal enveloping algebra** is

$$
U(\mathfrak{g}) = T(\mathfrak{g}) \Big/ \big( x \otimes y - y \otimes x - [x, y] : x, y \in \mathfrak{g} \big),
$$

with defining relation $x y - y x = [x, y]$. The relation is inhomogeneous, so $U(\mathfrak{g})$ is filtered, not graded; by the Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt theorem its associated graded algebra is the symmetric algebra,

$$
\operatorname{gr} U(\mathfrak{g}) \cong \operatorname{Sym}(\mathfrak{g}),
$$

and $U(\mathfrak{g}) \cong \operatorname{Sym}(\mathfrak{g})$ exactly when $\mathfrak{g}$ is abelian. The pattern is the mirror image of the Clifford case: an inhomogeneous relation whose associated graded algebra is one of the two homogeneous quotients, exterior for the quadratic relation $v^2 = Q(v)$ and symmetric for the linear commutator relation. In particular neither $U(\mathfrak{g})$ nor $\mathrm{Cl}(M, Q)$ for $Q \neq 0$ is a quotient of $\Lambda(M)$; all of them are quotients of $T(M)$, and the exterior and symmetric algebras appear as associated graded algebras rather than as quotients of the filtered ones.

The Weyl algebra $A_n(R)$, defined by $x_i y_j - y_j x_i = \delta_{ij} \cdot 1$, is a further quotient of the same kind, with $\operatorname{gr} A_n(R) \cong \operatorname{Sym}(V \oplus V^*)$; it is treated in *Tensor Algebras: Categorization*.

## 17. The Graded Algebra of a Filtered Algebra

A **filtered algebra** is an algebra $A$ with an increasing chain

$$
0 \subseteq F^0 A \subseteq F^1 A \subseteq \cdots, \qquad \bigcup_n F^n A = A, \qquad F^m A \cdot F^n A \subseteq F^{m+n} A,
$$

and its **associated graded algebra** is

$$
\operatorname{gr} A = \bigoplus_{n \geq 0} F^n A / F^{n-1} A,
$$

a graded algebra generated by its degree-one part. Every graded algebra with $A_n = 0$ for $n < 0$ is filtered by $F^n A = \bigoplus_{k \leq n} A_k$, and then $\operatorname{gr} A \cong A$; so graded algebras are the special case of filtered ones in which the filtration splits. The two computations that concern this family are

$$
\operatorname{gr} \mathrm{Cl}(M, Q) \cong \Lambda(M), \qquad \operatorname{gr} U(\mathfrak{g}) \cong \operatorname{Sym}(\mathfrak{g}).
$$

The associated graded algebra records the leading terms of products and does not determine the algebra: $U(\mathfrak{g})$, which is noncommutative for nonabelian $\mathfrak{g}$, and the commutative $\operatorname{Sym}(\mathfrak{g})$ have isomorphic associated graded algebras. The exterior algebra therefore occupies two positions at once: it is the homogeneous quotient of the tensor algebra by $v \wedge v = 0$, and it is the graded shadow of the inhomogeneous Clifford quotients.

# Part V: The Hierarchy and the Separating Invariants

## 18. The Hierarchy

Every class is a quotient of a tensor algebra by relations of a definite shape:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(M) &\to \operatorname{Sym}(M) &&\text{by } v w - w v, \\
T(M) &\to \Lambda(M) &&\text{by } v \wedge v, \\
T(M) &\to \mathrm{Cl}(M, Q) &&\text{by } v^2 - Q(v) \cdot 1, \\
T(\mathfrak{g}) &\to U(\mathfrak{g}) &&\text{by } x y - y x - [x, y].
\end{aligned}
$$

Homogeneous relations give graded quotients; inhomogeneous relations give filtered quotients whose associated graded algebras are graded quotients. Within the graded quotients, $\operatorname{Sym}(M)$ and $\Lambda(M)$ are the two extremes: the universal commutative quotient and the universal quotient with all generators square-zero. The Clifford and enveloping algebras sit one level away, as filtered deformations whose associated graded algebras are those two extremes:

$$
\Lambda(M) \xrightarrow{\ \operatorname{gr}\ } \mathrm{Cl}(M, Q), \qquad \operatorname{Sym}(\mathfrak{g}) \xrightarrow{\ \operatorname{gr}\ } U(\mathfrak{g}).
$$

The exterior algebra has a further place internal to the graded quotients: it is the free graded-commutative algebra on $M$ placed in odd degree, and it is the Clifford algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(M, 0)$.

## 19. The Separating Invariants

The classes are distinguished by the following structural invariants; taken together they determine which class a given algebra belongs to among the ones considered here.

- **Grading.** $T(M)$, $\operatorname{Sym}(M)$, and $\Lambda(M)$ are connected $\mathbb{Z}$-graded, generated in degree one. $\mathrm{Cl}(M, Q)$ is $\mathbb{Z}/2$-graded and, for $Q \neq 0$, not $\mathbb{Z}$-graded; $U(\mathfrak{g})$ is filtered and, for nonabelian $\mathfrak{g}$, not graded. The exterior algebra's grading is the one compatible with the supercommutative sign rule.
- **Commutativity type.** $T(M)$ is free, imposing no commutation relation; $\operatorname{Sym}(M)$ is commutative; $\Lambda(M)$ is graded-commutative but not commutative for $\operatorname{rank} M \geq 2$ when $2$ is invertible; $\mathrm{Cl}(M, Q)$ is neither commutative nor supercommutative for $Q \neq 0$ when $2$ is invertible; $U(\mathfrak{g})$ is noncommutative for nonabelian $\mathfrak{g}$. Supercommutativity is the invariant that isolates the exterior algebra when $2$ is invertible.
- **Dimension and rank.** For $M$ free of rank $n \geq 1$, $\Lambda(M)$ has rank $2^n$ and $\mathrm{Cl}(M, Q)$ has rank $2^n$, whereas $T(M)$, $\operatorname{Sym}(M)$, and $U(\mathfrak{g})$ have infinite rank. Finiteness therefore separates the square-zero and Clifford classes from the free and polynomial ones.
- **Freeness.** $\Lambda(M)$ is free of rank $2^n$ exactly when $M$ is free of finite rank; for general $M$ it need not be free. Freeness as an *algebra* (the universal property of the free graded-commutative algebra) is distinct from freeness as a *module* over $R$, and the two must not be conflated.
- **Nilpotence and domain properties.** $\Lambda^+(M)$ is nilpotent for $M$ free of finite rank $n \geq 1$, so $\Lambda(M)$ has zero divisors and is not a domain; $\operatorname{Sym}(M)$ is a domain when $R$ is, and has no nonzero nilpotent elements; $\mathrm{Cl}(M, Q)$ may or may not be a domain, depending on $Q$ and on $n$.
- **Hilbert–Poincaré series.** For $V$ of dimension $n$, the graded dimensions are $(1+t)^n$ for $\Lambda(V)$, $(1-t)^{-n}$ for $\operatorname{Sym}(V)$, and $(1-nt)^{-1}$ for $T(V)$.
- **Defining relations.** The organizing invariant is the relation itself: $v w = w v$ (symmetry), $v \wedge v = 0$ (alternation), $v^2 = Q(v) \cdot 1$ (quadratic deformation), and $x y - y x = [x, y]$ (linear deformation). Homogeneity of the relation is equivalent to the algebra being graded; inhomogeneity forces the filtered description.

## 20. Summary

| Class | Defining relation | Homogeneous | Grading | Commutativity | Rank for $M$ free of rank $n$ | Hilbert series |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $T(M)$ | none | — | $\mathbb{Z}$, connected | free | $\infty$ | $(1-nt)^{-1}$ |
| $\operatorname{Sym}(M)$ | $v w - w v = 0$ | yes | $\mathbb{Z}$, connected | commutative | $\infty$ | $(1-t)^{-n}$ |
| $\Lambda(M)$ | $v \wedge v = 0$ | yes | $\mathbb{Z}$, connected | graded-commutative | $2^n$ | $(1+t)^n$ |
| $\mathrm{Cl}(M, Q)$ | $v^2 = Q(v) \cdot 1$ | no, if $Q \neq 0$ | $\mathbb{Z}/2$ | neither, if $Q \neq 0$ and $2$ is invertible | $2^n$ | $(1+t)^n$ on $\operatorname{gr}$ |
| $U(\mathfrak{g})$ | $x y - y x = [x, y]$ | no | filtered | not commutative | $\infty$ | $(1-t)^{-n}$ on $\operatorname{gr}$ |

The exterior algebra is the connected graded, graded-commutative quotient of the tensor algebra by the homogeneous relation $v \wedge v = 0$; it is finite-dimensional of dimension $2^n$ in the field case, free as a module exactly when the underlying module is free of finite rank, nilpotent in positive degrees, and local over a field. The symmetric algebra is its commutative counterpart, the free commutative algebra. The Clifford and enveloping algebras are the inhomogeneous deformations, with $\mathrm{Cl}(M, 0) = \Lambda(M)$ and $\operatorname{gr} \mathrm{Cl}(M, Q) \cong \Lambda(M)$ on the one hand, and $\operatorname{gr} U(\mathfrak{g}) \cong \operatorname{Sym}(\mathfrak{g})$ on the other.

# Standard Examples of the Family

- **The ground ring.** $\Lambda(R^0) = R$, the rank-zero case.
- **Dual numbers.** $\Lambda(R) = R \oplus R\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon^2 = 0$, the exterior algebra of a free module of rank one and the smallest nonzero case.
- **The plane.** $\Lambda(R^2)$ has basis $1, e_1, e_2, e_1 \wedge e_2$ and rank $4$; the top element $e_1 \wedge e_2$ squares to zero.
- **Space and the Hodge star.** $\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^3)$ is $8$-dimensional, with $\Lambda^1 \cong \Lambda^2$ via the star operator; the cross product is the $\Lambda^1 \times \Lambda^1 \to \Lambda^2 \cong \Lambda^1$ product.
- **Top power and determinant.** For free $M$ of rank $n$, $\Lambda^n(M)$ is free of rank one and $\Lambda^n(f) = \det(f)$ for $f : M \to M$.
- **Arithmetic case.** $\Lambda(\mathbb{Z}^n)$ is free abelian of rank $2^n$; $\Lambda(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is the standard non-free example.
- **Characteristic 2.** Over a field of characteristic $2$, $\Lambda(V)$ is commutative and still of dimension $2^n$.
- **Symmetric counterpart.** $\operatorname{Sym}(V) \cong F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, with $\operatorname{gr} U(\mathfrak{g}) \cong \operatorname{Sym}(\mathfrak{g})$ by PBW.
- **Clifford relatives.** $\mathrm{Cl}(V, 0) = \Lambda(V)$; $\mathrm{Cl}_{2,0}(\mathbb{R}) \cong M_2(\mathbb{R})$ and $\mathrm{Cl}_{0,2}(\mathbb{R}) \cong \mathbb{H}$, both of rank $4$ over $\mathbb{R}$ with associated graded $\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^2)$.
- **Enveloping relatives.** $U(\mathfrak{g}) \cong \operatorname{Sym}(\mathfrak{g})$ for abelian $\mathfrak{g}$; $U(\mathfrak{sl}_2)$ is infinite-dimensional with $\operatorname{gr} U(\mathfrak{sl}_2) \cong \operatorname{Sym}(\mathfrak{sl}_2)$.
