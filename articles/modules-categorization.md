# __Modules categorization__

## Introduction

This article gives a classification of modules, organized by their structural properties. For each class of modules we state the defining property, give concrete examples, and describe the position of the class in the hierarchy of module-theoretic structures.

The general theory of modules is in the preceding article, *Modules*. We assume familiarity with the definitions given there.

Throughout this article, $R$ is a commutative ring with identity $1 \neq 0$, and a **module** means a unital $R$-module. We write $M$ for a module, and submodules, quotient modules, direct sums, and tensor products are understood over $R$.

Most of the classes below make sense over an arbitrary ring. We work over a commutative ring to remain consistent with the rest of this series, in which modules were introduced over a commutative ring; the definitions and the standard examples are not affected by this restriction.

We classify modules by the following properties:

- Whether the module has a **basis** (free).
- Whether it is generated by finitely many elements (**finitely generated**).
- Whether it has a finite presentation by generators and relations (**finitely presented**).
- Whether its submodules satisfy the **ascending chain condition** (Noetherian).
- Whether its submodules satisfy the **descending chain condition** (Artinian).
- Whether it has a **composition series** (finite length).
- Whether it has no nontrivial submodules (**simple**).
- Whether every submodule is a **direct summand** (**semisimple**).
- Whether it cannot be split as a direct sum (**indecomposable**).
- Whether it is a **direct summand of a free module** (**projective**).
- Whether it is a **direct summand of every module that contains it** (**injective**).
- Whether tensoring with it **preserves exactness** (**flat**).
- Whether every element is **annihilated by a nonzero scalar**, or none is (**torsion**, **torsion-free**).

Throughout this article, the standard examples are:

- $\mathbb{Z}$ and its quotients $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, as $\mathbb{Z}$-modules.
- $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$, as $\mathbb{Z}$-modules, with the $p$-primary part $\mathbb{Z}[1/p]/\mathbb{Z}$.
- A vector space $V$ over a field $k$, as a $k$-module.
- The polynomial ring $k[x]$ and its quotients $k[x]/(f)$, as $k[x]$-modules.
- The polynomial ring $k[x, y]$ and its maximal ideal $(x, y)$.
- The polynomial ring $k[x_1, x_2, \ldots]$ in infinitely many variables.
- The ring $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{F}_2 \times \mathbb{F}_3$, a product of two fields.
- The free module $R^n$ and its direct summands.

# Part I: Classification by Generation and Size

## 1. Free modules

A module $M$ is **free** if it has a **basis**: a subset $\{e_i\}_{i \in I} \subseteq M$ such that every element of $M$ can be written uniquely as a finite linear combination

$$
m = \sum_{i \in I} r_i e_i, \qquad r_i \in R,
$$

in which only finitely many of the $r_i$ are nonzero. Free modules are the modules that behave like vector spaces. Every module is a quotient of a free module: if $M$ is generated by a set $S$, then the free module on $S$ maps onto $M$.

If $R \neq 0$ is commutative, the cardinality of a basis of a free module does not depend on the choice of basis, so a free module has a well-defined **rank**.

**Examples.**

- $R$ itself, with basis $\{1\}$, and the free modules $R^n$ of rank $n$.
- Every vector space over a field, by the existence of bases.
- The free abelian groups $\mathbb{Z}^n$ and, more generally, direct sums of copies of $\mathbb{Z}$.

**Non-examples.**

- $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ with $n \geq 2$: every element has finite additive order, whereas a free abelian group has no nonzero element of finite order.
- $\mathbb{Q}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module: it is divisible, but a nonzero free abelian group has no nonzero divisible element, so $\mathbb{Q}$ is not free.
- The maximal ideal $(x, y) \subseteq k[x, y]$: it is a submodule of the free module $k[x, y]$, but it is not free, because it is not principal and its rank is one.

A submodule of a free module need not be free, as the last example shows. Over a principal ideal domain, however, every submodule of a free module is free; this is part of the structure theory discussed in §13.

## 2. Finitely generated modules

A module $M$ is **finitely generated** if there exist finitely many elements $m_1, \ldots, m_n \in M$ such that every element of $M$ is of the form

$$
m = \sum_{i=1}^{n} r_i m_i, \qquad r_i \in R.
$$

A module generated by a single element is **cyclic**; every cyclic module is a quotient $R/I$ by an ideal $I$.

**Examples.**

- Every free module of finite rank, in particular $R^n$.
- Every quotient of a finitely generated module.
- $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, and every finitely generated abelian group.
- Every simple module, since it is generated by any nonzero element.

**Non-examples.**

- $\mathbb{Q}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module: every finitely generated subgroup of $\mathbb{Q}$ is contained in some $\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}$, a proper subgroup.
- $R[x]$ as an $R$-module: the submodule generated by finitely many polynomials has bounded degree, so it cannot contain $x^{N}$ for arbitrarily large $N$.
- The $p$-primary Prüfer group $\mathbb{Z}[1/p]/\mathbb{Z}$: every finitely generated subgroup is finite.

A submodule of a finitely generated module need not be finitely generated. Over $R = k[x_1, x_2, \ldots]$, the module $R$ is cyclic, hence finitely generated, but its submodule $(x_1, x_2, \ldots)$ is not finitely generated.

## 3. Finitely presented modules

A module $M$ is **finitely presented** if there is an exact sequence

$$
R^m \to R^n \to M \to 0
$$

with $m$ and $n$ finite. Equivalently, $M$ is finitely generated, and for some surjection $R^n \to M$ with $n$ finite the kernel is finitely generated; the condition is independent of the chosen finite generating set. A finite presentation records generators $e_1, \ldots, e_n$ together with finitely many relations among them.

**Examples.**

- Every finitely generated module over a Noetherian ring. Indeed, the kernel of $R^n \to M$ is a submodule of the Noetherian module $R^n$, hence finitely generated.
- Every finitely generated module over $\mathbb{Z}$ or over $k[x]$, since both rings are Noetherian.
- Every finitely generated free module, and every quotient $R^n/I$ with $I$ finitely generated.

**Non-example.**

- Let $R = k[x_1, x_2, \ldots]$ and let $I = (x_1, x_2, \ldots)$. The quotient $M = R/I \cong k$ is cyclic, hence finitely generated, but it is not finitely presented: a finite presentation would exhibit the kernel of $R \to k$, namely $I$, as finitely generated, and $I$ is not. This module $M$ is even simple, so finite presentability is not a chain-condition property; it is a coherence property of the pair $(R, M)$, governed by the ring.

Every finitely presented module is finitely generated, but the converse fails, as the example shows. Over a Noetherian ring the two notions coincide.

## 4. Noetherian modules

A module $M$ is **Noetherian** if every ascending chain of submodules

$$
M_1 \subseteq M_2 \subseteq M_3 \subseteq \cdots
$$

stabilizes: there is an index $N$ with $M_n = M_N$ for all $n \geq N$. Equivalently, every submodule of $M$ is finitely generated.

**Examples.**

- Every vector space of finite dimension over a field.
- $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ as $\mathbb{Z}$-modules.
- Every finitely generated module over a Noetherian ring $R$. In particular, every finitely generated module over $\mathbb{Z}$ or over $k[x]$.

**Non-examples.**

- $\mathbb{Q}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module: it is not finitely generated, and a Noetherian module is its own submodule, hence is finitely generated.
- The Prüfer group $\mathbb{Z}[1/p]/\mathbb{Z}$: the subgroups generated by $1/p^n$ form the strictly increasing chain
$$
0 \subset \langle 1/p \rangle \subset \langle 1/p^2 \rangle \subset \cdots
$$
of finite cyclic subgroups.

Submodules and quotients of a Noetherian module are Noetherian, and an extension of two Noetherian modules is Noetherian. Every Noetherian module is finitely generated, but not conversely: over $R = k[x_1, x_2, \ldots]$ the module $R$ is cyclic, while its submodule $(x_1, x_2, \ldots)$ is not finitely generated.

## 5. Artinian modules

A module $M$ is **Artinian** if every descending chain of submodules

$$
M_1 \supseteq M_2 \supseteq M_3 \supseteq \cdots
$$

stabilizes.

**Examples.**

- Every vector space of finite dimension over a field.
- $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, and every finite module.
- The Prüfer group $\mathbb{Z}[1/p]/\mathbb{Z}$: its proper subgroups are the finite cyclic subgroups $\langle 1/p^n \rangle$, which form a single chain, so every descending chain stabilizes.

**Non-examples.**

- $\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module: the chain of principal ideals
$$
(2) \supseteq (4) \supseteq (8) \supseteq \cdots
$$
does not stabilize.
- $\mathbb{Q}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module: the subgroups $\mathbb{Z}, 2\mathbb{Z}, 4\mathbb{Z}, \ldots$ form a strictly descending chain.

Submodules and quotients of an Artinian module are Artinian, and an extension of two Artinian modules is Artinian. Unlike the case of rings, an Artinian module need not be Noetherian. The Prüfer group is Artinian but not Noetherian, and it is not even finitely generated; conversely, $\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian but not Artinian. For rings, by the Hopkins–Levitzki theorem, every Artinian ring is Noetherian, so the module case is genuinely different.

## 6. Modules of finite length

A **composition series** of a module $M$ is a finite chain of submodules

$$
0 = M_0 \subset M_1 \subset \cdots \subset M_n = M
$$

such that each quotient $M_i/M_{i-1}$ is simple. The integer $n$ is the **length** of $M$; by the Jordan–Hölder theorem it depends only on $M$, not on the composition series. The quotients $M_i/M_{i-1}$ are the **composition factors**.

A module has finite length if and only if it is both Noetherian and Artinian. This is the module-theoretic form of the existence of composition series.

**Examples.**

- A vector space of dimension $d$ over a field, of length $d$; its composition factors are $d$ copies of the simple module $k$.
- $\mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, of length $n$.
- $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, of length equal to the number of prime factors of $n$ counted with multiplicity.
- Every simple module, of length one.

**Non-examples.**

- $\mathbb{Z}$, of infinite length: it is Noetherian but not Artinian.
- The Prüfer group, of infinite length: it is Artinian but not Noetherian.
- $\mathbb{Q}$, which is neither Noetherian nor Artinian.

Every module of finite length is a finite direct sum of indecomposable modules, and the summands are unique up to isomorphism and order (the Krull–Schmidt theorem for finite length). Over a principal ideal domain that is not a field, the indecomposable modules of finite length are exactly the quotients $R/(p^n)$ for irreducible elements $p$ and $n \geq 1$.

# Part II: Classification by Decomposition

## 7. Simple modules

A module $M$ is **simple** (or **irreducible**) if $M \neq 0$ and its only submodules are $0$ and $M$.

Over a commutative ring $R$, the simple modules are exactly the quotients

$$
M \cong R/\mathfrak{m}
$$

for maximal ideals $\mathfrak{m} \subseteq R$. Indeed, a submodule of $R/I$ corresponds to an ideal lying between $I$ and $R$, so $R/I$ is simple precisely when $I$ is maximal.

**Examples.**

- $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ for a prime $p$, as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module.
- Every one-dimensional vector space over a field $k$, and no vector space of higher dimension.
- Any quotient $R/\mathfrak{m}$ by a maximal ideal, for instance $k[x]/(x)$ over $k[x]$.

**Non-examples.**

- The zero module, which is excluded by the condition $M \neq 0$.
- $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, whose submodule $2\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ is nonzero and proper.
- A vector space of dimension at least two.

Every simple module is cyclic, generated by any nonzero element, and hence finitely generated. Every simple module has length one, so it is both Noetherian and Artinian, and it is indecomposable. By Schur's lemma, every nonzero homomorphism between simple modules is an isomorphism; over a commutative ring the endomorphism ring of $R/\mathfrak{m}$ is the field $R/\mathfrak{m}$.

## 8. Semisimple modules

A module $M$ is **semisimple** (or **completely reducible**) if it is a direct sum of simple submodules. Equivalently, $M$ is the sum of its simple submodules, that is, $M$ equals its socle; equivalently, every submodule of $M$ is a direct summand of $M$.

**Examples.**

- Every vector space over a field: a vector space is the direct sum of the one-dimensional subspaces spanned by a basis.
- $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, a direct sum of two simple modules.
- Every direct sum of copies of the modules $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, with $p$ prime.
- Every module over a commutative semisimple ring; for a commutative ring this means every module over a finite product of fields, such as $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$.

**Non-examples.**

- $\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module: the submodule $2\mathbb{Z}$ has no complementary submodule, since a complement would meet $2\mathbb{Z}$ trivially, while two nonzero subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}$ always intersect nontrivially.
- $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, which is indecomposable and not simple.
- $k[x]/(x^2)$ as a $k[x]$-module, whose submodule $(x)/(x^2)$ is not a direct summand.

A direct sum of simple modules is semisimple, and a submodule or a quotient of a semisimple module is semisimple. Every simple module is semisimple, but an infinite direct sum of simple modules is semisimple without being of finite length. A finitely generated semisimple module is a finite direct sum of simple modules, hence of finite length.

Over a commutative ring $R$, the regular module $R$ is semisimple if and only if $R$ is a finite direct product of fields; equivalently, every $R$-module is semisimple, if and only if every $R$-module is projective, if and only if every $R$-module is injective. For example, $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{F}_2 \times \mathbb{F}_3$ has this property, while $\mathbb{Z}$ does not.

## 9. Indecomposable modules

A module $M$ is **indecomposable** if $M \neq 0$ and there is no decomposition

$$
M = M_1 \oplus M_2
$$

with $M_1$ and $M_2$ both nonzero. A module that is not indecomposable is **decomposable**.

**Examples.**

- Every simple module, since a splitting would exhibit a nonzero proper submodule.
- $\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, because two nonzero subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}$ always intersect nontrivially.
- $\mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$, whose submodules form the chain $0 \subset p^{n-1}\mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z} \subset \cdots \subset \mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$.
- $\mathbb{Q}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, by the same intersection argument as for $\mathbb{Z}$.
- Every one-dimensional vector space over a field.

**Non-examples.**

- $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$.
- A vector space of dimension at least two, which is the direct sum of a line and a complementary hyperplane.
- $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ for $n$ with at least two distinct prime factors, by the Chinese remainder theorem.

Every simple module is indecomposable, but not conversely: $\mathbb{Z}/p^2\mathbb{Z}$ is indecomposable and not simple. Indecomposability does not require finite generation: $\mathbb{Q}$ is indecomposable as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module and is not finitely generated. Every module of finite length is a finite direct sum of indecomposable modules, and the summands are unique up to isomorphism and order (Krull–Schmidt).

# Part III: Classification by Homological Properties

## 10. Projective modules

A module $P$ is **projective** if for every surjective homomorphism $f : N \to M$ and every homomorphism $g : P \to M$ there exists a homomorphism $h : P \to N$ with $f \circ h = g$. Equivalently, the functor $\operatorname{Hom}_R(P, -)$ is exact; equivalently, $P$ is a direct summand of a free module.

**Examples.**

- Every free module.
- Every module over a field, since every vector space is free.
- Over $\mathbb{Z}$, the projective modules are exactly the free abelian groups.
- Over $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{F}_2 \times \mathbb{F}_3$, the ideal $(2) = 2\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ is projective, because $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \cong (2) \oplus (3)$ exhibits it as a direct summand of the regular module. It is not free: it has three elements, while a nonzero free $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$-module has at least six.

**Non-examples.**

- $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module for $n \geq 2$, since over $\mathbb{Z}$ the projective modules are free and $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is not.
- $\mathbb{Q}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, for the same reason.
- The maximal ideal $(x, y) \subseteq k[x, y]$, which is not projective; see §12.

Every free module is projective, but not conversely, as the ideal $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ inside $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ shows. Over a principal ideal domain the two notions coincide, and a finitely generated projective module over a local commutative ring is free. A finitely generated projective module over a commutative ring is locally free of finite rank. Every projective module is flat, and over an integral domain every projective module is torsion-free, since it is a direct summand of a free module.

## 11. Injective modules

A module $I$ is **injective** if for every injective homomorphism $f : M \to N$ and every homomorphism $g : M \to I$ there exists a homomorphism $h : N \to I$ with $h \circ f = g$. Equivalently, the functor $\operatorname{Hom}_R(-, I)$ is exact; equivalently, $I$ is a direct summand of every module that contains it. By Baer's criterion, it suffices to test the extension property for inclusions of ideals $I_0 \to R$.

**Examples.**

- Every module over a field, since every subspace of a vector space is a direct summand.
- $\mathbb{Q}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module: it is divisible, and over $\mathbb{Z}$ the injective modules are exactly the divisible abelian groups.
- $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ and the Prüfer groups $\mathbb{Z}[1/p]/\mathbb{Z}$, which are divisible and torsion.
- Every direct product of injective modules.

**Non-examples.**

- $\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module: it is not divisible, since $2x = 1$ has no solution, so the inclusion $2\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ together with the map $2\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ sending $2$ to $1$ cannot be extended.
- $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ for $n \geq 2$: it is not divisible, so not injective.

Every injective module over $\mathbb{Z}$ is divisible, and every module embeds in an injective module; the minimal such module is the injective hull. Injective modules are not in general projective: $\mathbb{Q}$ is injective over $\mathbb{Z}$ but not projective. Injective modules are not in general flat either: $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ is a nonzero torsion module over the domain $\mathbb{Z}$, and such a module is never flat.

## 12. Flat modules

A module $M$ is **flat** if the functor $-\otimes_R M$ is exact: for every injective homomorphism $N' \to N$ the induced map $N' \otimes_R M \to N \otimes_R M$ is injective. Equivalently, $\operatorname{Tor}_1^R(M, N) = 0$ for every module $N$.

**Examples.**

- Every free module and every projective module.
- Every module over a field.
- Over $\mathbb{Z}$, the flat modules are exactly the torsion-free abelian groups: $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, and all subgroups of $\mathbb{Q}$ are flat.
- Every torsion-free module over a Dedekind domain, for instance over a principal ideal domain.

**Non-examples.**

- $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ for $n \geq 2$: it is torsion, and a nonzero torsion module over an integral domain is never flat.
- $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ and the Prüfer groups, again because they are torsion.
- The maximal ideal $(x, y) \subseteq k[x, y]$, which is torsion-free but not flat; see below.

Every free module is projective, and every projective module is flat. Neither implication reverses in general. The module $\mathbb{Q}$ is flat but not projective over $\mathbb{Z}$, since over $\mathbb{Z}$ the projective modules are free and $\mathbb{Q}$ is not free; in particular a flat module need not be free.

Over an integral domain, every flat module is torsion-free, and the converse holds over a Dedekind domain but fails in general. The ideal $(x, y) \subseteq k[x, y]$ is torsion-free, as a submodule of the free module $k[x, y]$, but it is not flat. Indeed, if it were flat, then being finitely generated it would be projective, because the ring $k[x, y]$ is Noetherian; a finitely generated projective module over a commutative ring is locally free of finite rank, so $(x, y)_{\mathfrak{m}}$ would be free of rank one over the local ring $k[x, y]_{\mathfrak{m}}$, where $\mathfrak{m} = (x, y)$, hence principal. But $\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$ has dimension two, so $\mathfrak{m}$ is not principal, a contradiction.

A finitely presented flat module is projective; over a Noetherian ring, where finitely generated modules are finitely presented, the same holds for finitely generated flat modules.

# Part IV: Torsion

## 13. Torsion and torsion-free modules

In this section $R$ is an integral domain, so that torsion and torsion-free have their classical form.

An element $m \in M$ is a **torsion element** if $r m = 0$ for some nonzero $r \in R$. The module $M$ is **torsion** if every element is a torsion element, and **torsion-free** if $0$ is its only torsion element, that is, if

$$
r m = 0 \implies r = 0 \text{ or } m = 0
$$

for all $r \in R$ and $m \in M$. The torsion elements of $M$ form a submodule, the **torsion submodule**, and $M$ is torsion-free precisely when this submodule is zero.

**Examples.**

- $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ are torsion $\mathbb{Z}$-modules, as is every finite abelian group.
- $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, and $\mathbb{C}$ are torsion-free $\mathbb{Z}$-modules.
- Every free module is torsion-free, and every submodule of a torsion-free module is torsion-free.
- Every vector space over a field is torsion-free.

**Non-examples.**

- The quotient of a torsion-free module by a submodule need not be torsion-free: $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a quotient of the torsion-free module $\mathbb{Z}$.
- Torsion modules need not be finite or finitely generated: $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ and the Prüfer groups are infinite and torsion.

Over an integral domain every free module, every projective module, and every flat module is torsion-free. Over a principal ideal domain the converse holds for flatness but not for freeness: a module over a principal ideal domain is flat if and only if it is torsion-free, and a finitely generated torsion-free module over a principal ideal domain is free. Finite generation cannot be dropped: $\mathbb{Q}$ is a torsion-free $\mathbb{Z}$-module that is not free. Torsion-free does not imply flat over a general integral domain, as the ideal $(x, y) \subseteq k[x, y]$ shows; over a Dedekind domain the two notions do coincide.

For finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain, the structure theorem gives a complete classification:

$$
M \cong R^r \oplus R/(p_1^{a_1}) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(p_s^{a_s}),
$$

where the $p_i$ are irreducible elements of $R$ and $R^r$ is the free part. The torsion submodule is the direct sum of the modules $R/(p_i^{a_i})$, and each of these is indecomposable. When $R = \mathbb{Z}$, this is exactly the classification of finitely generated abelian groups.

# Part V: The Hierarchy

## 14. Inclusions among the classes

Over an integral domain the four classical homological classes satisfy

$$
\text{free} \subset \text{projective} \subset \text{flat} \subset \text{torsion-free},
$$

and each inclusion is strict:

- **free $\subsetneq$ projective**: in a Dedekind domain with nontrivial ideal class group, for instance $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, a non-principal ideal is projective but not free, since a rank-one projective module over a domain is free if and only if it is principal.
- **projective $\subsetneq$ flat**: $\mathbb{Q}$ is flat but not projective over $\mathbb{Z}$.
- **flat $\subsetneq$ torsion-free**: $(x, y)$ is torsion-free but not flat over $k[x, y]$.

For generation and chain conditions,

$$
\text{finitely presented} \subset \text{finitely generated}, \qquad \text{Noetherian} \subset \text{finitely generated},
$$

and a free module of finite rank is finitely presented, hence finitely generated. Every finitely presented module is finitely generated, and over a Noetherian ring the two notions coincide; over $k[x_1, x_2, \ldots]$ the simple module $R/(x_1, x_2, \ldots)$ is finitely generated but not finitely presented. Every Noetherian module is finitely generated; the converse fails over $k[x_1, x_2, \ldots]$, where the regular module $R$ is cyclic but not Noetherian.

For length and decomposition,

$$
\text{finite length} = \text{Noetherian} \cap \text{Artinian}, \qquad \text{simple} \subset \text{semisimple}, \qquad \text{simple} \subset \text{indecomposable}, \qquad \text{simple} \subset \text{finite length}.
$$

A module of finite length is Noetherian and Artinian, and conversely. A finitely generated semisimple module has finite length, but a semisimple module of infinite length exists: any infinite direct sum of simple modules. An indecomposable module need not be finitely generated, as $\mathbb{Q}$ over $\mathbb{Z}$ shows, nor of finite length: the Prüfer group is indecomposable and Artinian without being Noetherian.

The two families of conditions meet in the following way: a finitely presented flat module is projective, and over a Noetherian ring a finitely generated flat module is projective. So for finitely presented modules over a commutative ring, flatness and projectivity are equivalent.

## 15. Reduction to the standard rings

The hierarchy simplifies drastically over the rings that appear most often.

**Over a field $k$.** Every $k$-module is a vector space, and

$$
\text{free} = \text{projective} = \text{injective} = \text{flat} = \text{semisimple},
$$

while

$$
\text{Noetherian} = \text{Artinian} = \text{finite length} = \text{finite-dimensional}.
$$

Every module is torsion-free, and the only torsion module is the zero module. The simple modules are the one-dimensional vector spaces, and every vector space is a direct sum of them.

**Over $\mathbb{Z}$.** The classification of finitely generated modules is the structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups. The classes collapse as follows:

$$
\text{free} = \text{projective}, \qquad \text{flat} = \text{torsion-free}, \qquad \text{injective} = \text{divisible}.
$$

A finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-module is free if and only if it is torsion-free. The injective modules are the divisible groups, of which $\mathbb{Q}$ and the Prüfer groups are the basic examples.

**Over a principal ideal domain $R$.** The same three identities hold:

$$
\text{projective} = \text{free}, \qquad \text{flat} = \text{torsion-free}, \qquad \text{injective} = \text{divisible}.
$$

The structure theorem classifies the finitely generated modules as a free part together with a direct sum of modules $R/(p_i^{a_i})$.

**Over a Dedekind domain that is not a principal ideal domain.** The identities $\text{flat} = \text{torsion-free}$ and $\text{injective} = \text{divisible}$ still hold, but $\text{projective} = \text{free}$ fails: a nonzero ideal is projective but need not be principal. The obstruction is measured by the ideal class group. The Dedekind domain $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ has class group $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, and its non-principal ideals are the standard non-free projective modules.

**Over a commutative semisimple ring.** If $R$ is a finite direct product of fields, then every $R$-module is semisimple, projective, and injective, and the regular module is a direct sum of simple modules. The ring $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{F}_2 \times \mathbb{F}_3$ is the standard example.

**Over a general commutative ring.** None of the accidental identities above holds, and the classes are genuinely distinct, as the examples of this article show.

## 16. The standard examples in the hierarchy

The following table places the standard examples in the hierarchy. Here $\Omega(n)$ denotes the number of prime factors of $n$ counted with multiplicity, and $k$ is a field.

| Module | free | f.g. | Noetherian | Artinian | length | semisimple | indecomposable | projective | injective | flat | torsion-free |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{Z}$ | yes | yes | yes | no | $\infty$ | no | yes | yes | no | yes | yes |
| $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ | no | yes | yes | yes | 1 | yes | yes | no | no | no | no |
| $\mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$, $n \geq 2$ | no | yes | yes | yes | $n$ | no | yes | no | no | no | no |
| $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ | no | yes | yes | yes | 2 | yes | no | no | no | no | no |
| $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ | no | yes | yes | yes | 3 | no | no | no | no | no | no |
| $\mathbb{Q}$ | no | no | no | no | $\infty$ | no | yes | no | yes | yes | yes |
| $\mathbb{Z}[1/p]/\mathbb{Z}$ | no | no | no | yes | $\infty$ | no | yes | no | yes | no | no |
| $k^d$, $d \geq 2$ | yes | yes | yes | yes | $d$ | yes | no | yes | yes | yes | yes |

The pattern is clear. Over a field every module is free, projective, injective, flat, and semisimple, and the chain conditions reduce to finite dimension. Over $\mathbb{Z}$ the questions of freeness for finitely generated modules, of flatness, and of injectivity reduce to torsion-freeness and divisibility, and the classification of finitely generated modules is the structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups. Over the polynomial ring $k[x]$ the same structure theorem gives the classification of finitely generated modules by rational canonical form, and over a Dedekind domain that is not principal the ideal class group measures the failure of projective modules to be free. Over a general commutative ring, all of the classes above are distinct, and the examples of this article separate them.
