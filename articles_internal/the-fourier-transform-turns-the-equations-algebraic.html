The Fourier Transform Turns the Equations Algebraic

Introduction

The idea, in one sentence.

The Fourier transform turns the corpus's differential equations in biquaternion variables into algebraic equations in the wave-number element $\tilde K_\Phi$, because central scalars pass through the transform pointwise and non-central objects do not.

What that sentence buys. Every equation of the corpus is a differential equation whose coefficients are either numbers or elements. The transform moves the derivatives onto the wave-number element and leaves the coefficients where they are. What remains is an equation between elements at a single frequency point, with no derivative in it. The reader can then see, in one display, which part of the equation is a number and which part is an element.

The headline results. First, the mass shell is deduced, $k_t^{\prime\,2}-k_\Phi^{2}=k_m^{2}$, from the field equations alone. In the Klein–Gordon case that is one substitution. In the Dirac case it is not: two coupled first-order equations collapse to one scalar condition, because the block determinant is a fourth power of one number. Second, the symbol has two eigenvalues, $i(k_t'\pm k_\Phi)$, and they are the two light-cone factors. Third, the two displays of the gradient are separated by a surface: the conjugated pairing gives the Lorentz hyperboloid, the unconjugated one gives a Euclidean sphere with no propagating branch.

What it does not buy. No new equation, no new mass, no new dynamics, no new operator. The transform is invertible, so it unpacks the equations rather than discovering anything outside them. What it supplies is the reason those equations have the shape they have, plus two slices the corpus has not written down: the eigenvalue structure of the symbol, and the error of the Schrödinger approximation. Every claim carries its residual.

How the article proceeds. The general idea is stated first, in its own right and without biquaternions, so that the reader knows what the article is an instance of. Then the rule is stated and proved in this algebra. Forty-two consequences follow, each a single reading of the same rule. The limits close the article.

The conventions used. The algebra is $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$ with $e_t=1$ and $\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z$, so $\vec e_k^2=-e_t$. The gradient and its conjugate are

$$ \tilde\nabla=e_t\frac{\partial}{\partial R_t}+\sum_{k\in\{x,y,z\}}\vec e_k\frac{\partial}{\partial R_k}, \qquad \tilde\nabla^{\natural}=e_t\frac{\partial}{\partial R_t}-\sum_{k}\vec e_k\frac{\partial}{\partial R_k}, $$

so that $\tilde\nabla\tilde\nabla^{\natural}=\Box=\partial^2/\partial R_t^2+\Delta_Q$ with $\Delta_Q=\sum_k\partial^2/\partial R_k^2$. On the material sector $R_t=i\,r'_t$ and $R_k=q_k$, so $\partial/\partial R_t=-i\,\partial/\partial r'_t$. The wave-number element is

$$ \tilde K_\Phi=i\,k'_t\,e_t+\vec k_\Phi, \qquad \tilde K_\Phi^{\natural}=i\,k'_t\,e_t-\vec k_\Phi , \qquad s:=\tilde K_\Phi\tilde K_\Phi^{\natural}=-k_t^{\prime\,2}+k_\Phi^{2}. $$

The one number that matters. $s$ is a real complex number, not an element: $\tilde K_\Phi\tilde K_\Phi^{\natural}$ is the quaternion norm, and a quaternion norm is central. On shell $s=-k_m^{2}$. Everything below is a consequence of that centrality.

The sample point used in every check.

$$ k'_t=2.0,\quad \vec k_\Phi=(0.3,\,-0.9,\,1.4),\quad k_\Phi=1.7000,\quad s=-1.1400,\quad k_m=1.0677 . $$

One inverse-length unit throughout. The point is timelike, so $s<0$.

The General Idea

The idea is not about biquaternions, and it is older than quantum mechanics. Differentiation is not a property of a function; it is what translation looks like in whatever basis you happen to be using. Pass to the basis in which translations are diagonal — the exponential basis — and differentiation becomes multiplication by the label of the exponential: $$ \partial_{x}\longmapsto ik, \qquad \partial_{x}^{2}+a\,\partial_{x}+b\longmapsto -k^{2}+iak+b . $$ A linear equation with constant coefficients is therefore a differential equation in one basis and an algebraic equation in the other. Nothing is added and nothing removed: the two are one equation in two bases, and the map between them is unitary, so norms, inner products and expectation values are carried across unchanged.

Wave–particle duality is this idea given a physical name. The position wave function and the momentum wave function are Fourier transforms of each other, so "a wave with a position amplitude" and "a distribution over momenta" are two descriptions of one state rather than two behaviours of one object. The extremes make it vivid: a plane wave has a definite wave number and therefore no position at all, and a delta function has a definite position and every wave number at once. They are the two ends of one Fourier pair. The uncertainty relation is the bandwidth theorem in the units $p=\hbar k$ — a narrow packet needs a broad band — and it is a theorem about transforms, not a fact about nature that quantum mechanics discovered. Superposition is Fourier synthesis. Interference is what a sum of exponentials does.

The corpus is the place where the idea has to be checked rather than quoted. Its equations are already written in wave numbers: $\tilde K_\Phi$, $k'_t$, $k_m$, $s$. That notation is either already the output of the transform, in which case the transform has nothing to say, or it is shorthand for a differential equation, in which case the transform must be applied and shown to be licit. The article settles that, and the answer is the part the textbook case does not prepare a reader for. The transform produces an algebra exactly when the coefficients commute with the wave-number element. When they do — central terms, constants, the mass — each is a number at each wave number and the equation is algebraic point by point. When they do not — a field strength, a time-dependent permittivity, a matrix-valued potential, a lattice — the image is not an algebra but a convolution, a differential operator in wave number, or a finite matrix. The corpus's habit of writing equations in wave numbers is correct on the central part, and the boundary of that correctness is one of the results rather than one of the assumptions.

The operational consequence is the rest of the article. The general idea is stated once; every section after the rule is that idea read at one place:

  • read at an operator, it gives the mass shell as a solvability condition (Consequences 1 to 9);
  • read at a coefficient, it gives the potential, the medium, the chiral splitting and the field (Consequences 10 to 17, 30, 35);
  • read at a support, it gives the cone, the lattice doubler, the flat band and the discrete thermal spectrum (Consequences 4, 22, 25, 37, 41);
  • read at a contour, it gives causality, Kramers–Kronig and the bound-state count (Consequences 29, 32, 35, 39);
  • read at a counting measure, it gives the density of states and the degeneracy of a level (Consequence 37);
  • read at a boundary, it gives the decay window and its tails (Consequence 38);
  • read at a phase, it gives the stationary-phase expansion and its Maslov phase (Consequence 36);
  • read at a norm, it gives the optical theorem (Consequence 40).

The general idea is not a result, and this article does not claim it as one. What the article claims is that the corpus's equations are a single test case of it, and that the test case has edges worth mapping: which coefficients pass through, which do not, what the image is when they do not, and where the inverse transform's asymptotics and counting theorems attach. One sentence of textbook Fourier analysis, read forty-two times in one specific algebra, with a residual at each reading.

The Rule

Why the transform moves the derivatives and not the coefficients

The transform is an integral with a scalar kernel, $\exp(-i\tilde K_\Phi\cdot\tilde R_\Phi)$, and a scalar kernel is central. Two facts follow, and together they are the whole rule.

A constant left multiplier passes straight through. If $\tilde Q$ is a fixed element of $\mathbb{B}$, then $\mathcal F[\tilde Q f]=\tilde Q\,\mathcal F[f]$, because $\tilde Q$ does not depend on the variable of integration and the kernel is scalar. So a coefficient, central or not, stays a coefficient.

A derivative becomes a left multiplier. If $\tilde F$ is a plane wave,

$$ \tilde F=\tilde F_0\exp\!\bigl(i\,\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde K_\Phi\tilde R_\Phi^{\natural})\bigr), \qquad \mathrm{Sc}(\tilde K_\Phi\tilde R_\Phi^{\natural})=\vec k_\Phi\cdot\vec r-k'_t\,r'_t , $$

then each spatial derivative brings $i\vec k_\Phi$ and the material time derivative brings $-i(-ik'_t)=i(ik'_t)$, because $\partial/\partial R_t=-i\partial/\partial r'_t$. Every derivative therefore contributes its coefficient times $i$, and

$$ \boxed{\;\tilde\nabla\longmapsto i\tilde K_\Phi,\qquad \tilde\nabla^{\natural}\longmapsto i\tilde K_\Phi^{\natural},\qquad \Box\longmapsto(i\tilde K_\Phi)(i\tilde K_\Phi^{\natural})=-s\;} $$

The point of the box. A second-order differential operator has become a single real number. That is not an approximation and not a limit: it is what constant coefficients and a scalar kernel give, exactly.

Where centrality enters

The box is the only place the rule touches the algebra, and it touches it once.

A product of two left multipliers is a number exactly when the two elements are conjugate to each other. For a general pair $\tilde A\tilde B$ the product is an element. For the pair $\tilde K_\Phi,\tilde K_\Phi^{\natural}$ it is the norm $s$, and the norm of a quaternion is a number.

So the rule has a sharp form:

Derivatives become left multiplications by $i\tilde K_\Phi$. Those multiplications are numbers only where the product of the two elements is central. Centrality decides whether the transformed equation is an equation between numbers or between elements.

Consequence 1 and Consequence 2 are the central case. Consequence 3 is the non-central case, and it is not an accident: it is the same rule read at a place where the product fails to be a norm.

Consequence 1: The Wave Operator Is One Number, and the Shell Is Its Level Set

The equation $\Box\tilde F=k_m^{2}\tilde F$ transforms to $-s\,\hat{\tilde F}=k_m^{2}\hat{\tilde F}$. Since $s$ and $k_m$ are numbers, the amplitude factors out and what remains is

$$ -s=k_m^{2} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad k_t^{\prime\,2}-k_\Phi^{2}=k_m^{2}. $$

Both sides are real numbers, so the equation has a solution or it does not, and the amplitude is untouched either way.

What this says about the corpus. The mass shell of The Three Wavenumbers of a Particle is the zero set of the symbol. The transform shows that the shell is a level set of a number and not a set of four coupled conditions. The triples $k_t'$, $\vec k_\Phi$, $k_m$ are: two coordinates of a point of the spectrum and one constant of the equation. The transform is what makes the two roles visible, because in the coordinates all three appear as symbols of the same kind.

The sign carries the metric. The symbol is $-s=k_t^{\prime\,2}-k_\Phi^{2}$. The minus sign sits on the spatial part because $\vec e_k^2=-e_t$. An algebra with real units of four equal squares would have produced $+k_t^{\prime\,2}+k_\Phi^{2}$ and no shell at all. The Lorentzian signature here is not imposed on the transform; it arrives through the norm.

Consequence 2: The Block Determinant Is One Scalar Condition

The two equations, read at the symbol

Operator and Calculus Conventions, §The Two First-Order Equations and Their Symbol, writes the dynamics of a field of mass $m$ as two equations,

$$ \tilde\nabla\tilde{\Xi}_{\mathrm{sp},R}=k_m\,\tilde{\Xi}_{\mathrm{sp},L}, \qquad \tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde{\Xi}_{\mathrm{sp},L}=k_m\,\tilde{\Xi}_{\mathrm{sp},R}, $$

and states in the same section that the pair operator is not allowed: no letter gathers the two into one object on $\mathbb{B}\oplus\mathbb{B}$, no $\mathbb{D}$, no two-by-two array, no subscripted glyph. This article keeps that rule. The two lines are written as two lines throughout.

Applying the box to each line separately gives, at each frequency point,

$$ i\tilde K_\Phi\,\hat\Xi_R=k_m\,\hat\Xi_L, \qquad i\tilde K_\Phi^{\natural}\,\hat\Xi_L=k_m\,\hat\Xi_R . $$

That is the pair of The Dirac Equation in Biquaternionic Form with the derivatives replaced by their symbols. Nothing else has been done, and no letter has been introduced.

Why the determinant of the two lines is a scalar

A determinant needs an array, and the array of the two lines is the block matrix. The First-Order Root of the Biquaternion Klein–Gordon Operator, §The determinant, writes it: on a plane wave the two lines are the block matrix with the symbol of the gradient off the diagonal and $-k_m$ on it, where $\ell_M$ is left multiplication by $M$.

A determinant of a matrix with non-commuting entries is not defined in general. Here it is defined, for one reason: the product of the two off-diagonal blocks is a number. Write

$$ \mathcal P=\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{i\tilde K_\Phi} & -k_m I_4\\[2pt] -k_m I_4 & \ell_{i\tilde K_\Phi^{\natural}}\end{pmatrix}. $$

The product of the four blocks is central:

$$ \ell_{i\tilde K_\Phi}\,\ell_{i\tilde K_\Phi^{\natural}} =\ell_{(i\tilde K_\Phi)(i\tilde K_\Phi^{\natural})} =\ell_{-\tilde K_\Phi\tilde K_\Phi^{\natural}}=\ell_{-s}=-s\,I_4 , $$

because the quaternion norm is central. The diagonal blocks are scalar multiples of the identity, so they commute with everything. Under those two conditions the block determinant is the ordinary one,

$$ \det\mathcal P=\det\bigl(-s\,I_4-k_m^{2}I_4\bigr)=\bigl(-s-k_m^{2}\bigr)^{4} =\bigl(k_t^{\prime\,2}-\lvert\vec k_\Phi\rvert^{2}-k_m^{2}\bigr)^{4}. $$

This is already printed, with one slip. The root article states $\det\bigl(\ell_{i\tilde K_\Phi^{\natural}}\ell_{-i\tilde K_\Phi}-k_m^{2}\bigr)=\bigl(k_t^{\prime\,2}-\lvert\vec k_\Phi\rvert^{2}-k_m^{2}\bigr)^{4}$. The right-hand side is the bracket above. The left-hand side computes to $\bigl(s-k_m^{2}\bigr)^{4}$: on the shell it equals $(-2k_m^{2})^{4}$, not zero, and the computed pencil determinant is zero there. The sign of the mass term is the slip; with $+k_m^{2}$ the two sides agree.

So the transform adds no formula here. It adds the reason the fourth power is a scalar and not a matrix: the bracket is a quaternion norm, and a quaternion norm is central. That is the same centrality that made $\Box$ a number in the preceding section, read at the block array instead of at one operator.

What the fourth power means. Four equations after writing each biquaternion line in components, one condition. The multiplicity four is the dimension of $\mathbb{B}$ over $\mathbb{C}$, so the kernel is four-dimensional over $\mathbb{C}$ at each point of the shell. Computed on the sample point: rank deficiency $4$ on shell and $0$ off it, and the determinant equals the printed display off shell to a ratio of $1.000000$ at two different offsets.

The amplitude is not constrained. On shell the second line merely fixes one component from the other,

$$ \hat\Xi_L=\frac{i\tilde K_\Phi}{k_m}\,\hat\Xi_R , \qquad \hat\Xi_R\ \text{arbitrary, residual }1.8\times10^{-15}. $$

A correction to an earlier reading. It is tempting to call the off-diagonal blocks "the polarisation". That is wrong, and the kernel dimension is the reason: a four-dimensional kernel means every biquaternion amplitude is admitted, so nothing is selected. The off-diagonal blocks carry the map between the two chiral components. The projection onto a two-dimensional value space happens only at $k_m=0$, in Consequence 4.

The mass shell is deduced here, and what that means

The result. The two lines have a non-zero amplitude only where

$$ k_t^{\prime\,2}-k_\Phi^{2}=k_m^{2}. $$

That is the relativistic dispersion relation, and it is deduced from the two field equations. The deduction is the elimination, and no further law is added at any step.

The KG case, for comparison. There the same deduction is one substitution: $(\Box-k_m^{2})\tilde F=0$ transforms to $(-s-k_m^{2})\hat{\tilde F}=0$, so a non-zero amplitude requires $s=-k_m^{2}$. Both cases are deductions of the same kind.

What the transform being invertible does and does not imply. The transform gives no information the equations did not already hold. So the deduction unpacks the equations rather than discovering something outside them. That is what a deduction from an equation is, and it is not an objection: every derivation of a dispersion relation has the same character.

What is genuinely non-trivial in the Dirac case. The two lines are two coupled first-order equations. Their solvability locus is one scalar condition, not two. That collapse is not automatic: it happens because the determinant of the block matrix is $\bigl(-s-k_m^{2}\bigr)^{4}$, a fourth power of one number, and that in turn happens because the quaternion norm is central. In the standard Dirac algebra the corresponding identity is $\det(\gamma\!\cdot\! p-m)=(p^{2}-m^{2})^{2}$: two powers, one scalar, because the Clifford algebra has two simple factors. Here it is four powers and one scalar, with no gamma matrices and no Clifford algebra, and the reason is the norm.

What the deduction buys, in two lines. First, the shell is a solvability condition and not a postulate: it is where the two chiral lines have a common kernel, which is the Fredholm alternative in algebraic form. Second, being the zero set of a central norm, it is preserved by every rotor conjugation with no further argument, so the mass is a Lorentz scalar by the algebra and not by inspection.

What is not deduced, and must not be claimed. The value of $k_m$, which stays a parameter. And the indefinite form of the bracket: the minus sign on the spatial part comes from $\vec e_k^{2}=-e_t$, that is from the imaginary time coefficient $R_t=i\,r'_t$ of the material sector. The Lorentzian signature is posited where the sector is chosen, so the transform reports it and does not produce it. An algebra with a real time coefficient would give $k_t^{\prime\,2}+k_\Phi^{2}$ and a definite condition instead of a shell.

Two routes to the same shell

The corpus already reaches the shell from the Dirac pair, by another route. Relativistic Mechanics from the Dirac Wave: The Non-Quantum Limit in Biquaternionic Form writes the same pair, substitutes $\tilde\Xi_{\mathrm{sp}}=A\,e^{iS/\hbar}\xi$, obtains one exact identity in the two real functions $A$ and $S$, reads its three lines as the orders $1:\epsilon^{2}:\epsilon$ with $\epsilon=\hbar/(pL)$, drops the $\epsilon^{2}$ line, and boxes

$$ \frac{E^{2}}{c^{2}}-\lvert\vec p\rvert^{2}=m^{2}c^{2}, \qquad E=-\partial_t S,\qquad \vec p=\nabla S . $$

So the answer to the question is yes: the shell is already obtained from the Dirac wave there, and the transform is an alternative path to the same box.

The routes differ in exactness. The eikonal drops the quantum potential, and its relative error is $\epsilon^{2}$. The transform drops nothing: $i\tilde K_\Phi\hat\Xi_R=k_m\hat\Xi_L$ and $i\tilde K_\Phi^{\natural}\hat\Xi_L=k_m\hat\Xi_R$ are the two lines themselves, and $k_t^{\prime\,2}-k_\Phi^{2}=k_m^{2}$ follows without residue. The transform route is exact at every frequency and for every amplitude.

That is the symbol, taken approximately. The transform states the same multiplication as an identity, and pays for it by restricting to constant coefficients. Both articles lean on centrality for the same reason. The source article observes that the leading eikonal is a norm, that a norm is central, and that a central object cannot act on a spinor, which is why the spin is absent at order zero. This article uses the same fact twice: the centrality of the norm is what makes the $\Box$ symbol a number, and what makes the block determinant a fourth power of one number. One property, read at the eikonal in one article and at the transform in the other.

Consequence 3: The Non-Central Square Is Not a Number

The two pairings

The gradient can be applied twice in two ways, and only one of them is a norm.

$$ \tilde\nabla\tilde\nabla^{\natural}=\Box , \qquad \tilde\nabla\tilde\nabla\neq\Box . $$

Multiply out the second:

$$ \tilde\nabla^{2}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial R_t^{2}}+2\vec\nabla\frac{\partial}{\partial R_t}+\vec\nabla^{2}, \qquad \vec\nabla^{2}=-\Delta_Q , $$

the last step because the units anticommute while the partial derivatives commute, so the mixed terms cancel pairwise. Hence

$$ \tilde\nabla^{2}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial R_t^{2}}-\Delta_Q+2\vec\nabla\frac{\partial}{\partial R_t} \qquad\text{against}\qquad \Box=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial R_t^{2}}+\Delta_Q . $$

The first has a first-order mixed term and the wrong sign on the Laplacian. The two differ by $2\Delta_Q+2\vec\nabla\partial/\partial R_t$, which is neither small nor removable.

The same difference, read at the symbol

This is where the rule pays. The correct pairing has symbol $-s$, a number. The wrong pairing has symbol

$$ (i\tilde K_\Phi)^{2}=-\tilde K_\Phi^{2} =\bigl(k_t^{\prime\,2}+k_\Phi^{2}\bigr)-2i\,k'_t\,\vec k_\Phi , $$

which is an element, because $\tilde K_\Phi^{2}$ is not a norm: it is the square of one element.

Now demand that the elimination produce a mass term. A mass is a real central number, so every part of the symbol that is not a real number must vanish.

  • Correct pairing. $-s=k_m^{2}$ gives $k_t^{\prime\,2}-k_\Phi^{2}=k_m^{2}$. A hyperboloid: the mass shell.
  • Wrong pairing. The vector part forces $k'_t\vec k_\Phi=0$, and the scalar part then gives $k_t^{\prime\,2}+k_\Phi^{2}=k_m^{2}$. A sphere of radius $k_m$, cut down by the extra condition.

The two surfaces are the Lorentz hyperboloid and the Euclidean sphere. One conjugation of the gradient decides which one the equation describes, and the reason is that only the conjugated product is central.

The branches the wrong display admits

$k'_t\vec k_\Phi=0$ has two solutions and both are degenerate. Computed on the sample unit:

So the wrong display is not empty of solutions. It is empty of propagating solutions: a sphere intersected with two degenerate loci, and neither locus carries a wave.

The printed slip

The Klein–Gordon Equation in Biquaternionic Form, §The Lanczos Family and the Spin-0 Member, opens with

$$ \tilde\nabla\tilde Q_1=k_m\tilde Q_2, \qquad \tilde\nabla\tilde Q_2=k_m\tilde Q_1 , $$

which is the gradient on both lines. The paragraph below it says the elimination "leaves $\Box\tilde Q_1=k_m^{2}\tilde Q_1$" and attributes that to "the pair that carries one gradient of each kind". The prose is right and the display is not: eliminating from the display as printed gives $\tilde\nabla^{2}\tilde Q_1=k_m^{2}\tilde Q_1$, and $\tilde\nabla^{2}$ is the operator of the preceding subsection, not $\Box$. The repair is one glyph,

$$ \tilde\nabla\tilde Q_1=k_m\tilde Q_2, \qquad \tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde Q_2=k_m\tilde Q_1 , $$

and then the elimination gives $\Box\tilde Q_1=k_m^{2}\tilde Q_1$, which is the article's own equation.

Why the transform finds this and the coordinates do not. Written in the coordinates the two displays look alike: both first order, both carrying the same three symbols, differing by one superscript. Written at the symbol they differ by a surface, and a sphere is not a hyperboloid. The mistake cannot survive the change of description.

Why the slip was easy. Lanczos wrote the pair with a conjugate on the second line, and The Dirac Equation in Biquaternionic Form in this corpus writes the correct pair. The Klein–Gordon display is the outlier, and it is a transcription lapse.

Status. This article reports the slip and does not repair it; the article is the author's. The repair is the single glyph above.

Consequence 4: Support on the Null Cone

The support

This is the one statement that could not be made at all without the transform, because "support" has no meaning in the coordinates.

Let $\tilde\nabla\tilde F=0$. At each frequency point the transformed equation is the algebraic condition

$$ i\tilde K_\Phi\,\hat{\tilde F}=0 . $$

If left multiplication by $i\tilde K_\Phi$ is invertible, the only solution is $\hat{\tilde F}=0$. So the transform can be non-zero only where that multiplication is not invertible, that is where $i\tilde K_\Phi$ is a zero divisor. A zero divisor here is an element of vanishing norm, so

$$ \tilde K_\Phi\tilde K_\Phi^{\natural}=s=0 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad k_t^{\prime\,2}=k_\Phi^{2}. $$

The transform of a solution of $\tilde\nabla\tilde F=0$ is supported on the null cone.

That is the answer to the second open question of Biquaternion Continuous Harmonic Analysis, which asks what the support of such a transform is.

The same rule, read at the same place. The support is the zero set of a central number. If the multiplier had been a general element, its invertibility would have been a property of a matrix, and the support would have been a set of conditions rather than one level set.

What the transform equals on the cone

Support is half the statement. The value on the cone is the other half, and it is also algebraic.

The rank of left multiplication by $i\tilde K_\Phi$, computed:

generic, off the cone $4$ $0$
null, momentum along $\vec e_z$ $2$ $2$
null, generic direction $2$ $2$

Off the cone the multiplier is invertible. On the cone the rank drops by two, at every point, in every direction.

The kernel is a right ideal. Take $\tilde X$ with $i\tilde K_\Phi\tilde X=0$ and multiply on the right by any $\tilde Y$:

$$ i\tilde K_\Phi(\tilde X\tilde Y)=\bigl(i\tilde K_\Phi\tilde X\bigr)\tilde Y=0 . $$

The kernel is closed under right multiplication, so it is a right ideal, and at complex dimension two it is a minimal right ideal of $\mathbb{B}\cong M_{2}(\mathbb{C})$. Checked over thirty random multipliers: the relative residual stays at $5\times10^{-16}$ in the aligned case and $7\times10^{-15}$ in the generic one.

So the transform of a massless solution is not a biquaternion-valued function on the cone. At each cone point it takes its value in a two-dimensional module. Two of the four amplitude directions are killed, and the two that survive form one indivisible object, not two loose complex numbers.

One caveat, one line. Right multiplication by the frame's third direction acts on that module with eigenvalues $\pm i$; the two-dimensional count is unconditional, and the reading of the two as helicities requires the frame's third direction to lie along the momentum.

The vanishing-norm question. The mathematics article leaves open whether the transform is invertible for functions that take zero-divisor values. For solutions of $\tilde\nabla\tilde F=0$ the ambiguity is not a defect: it is exactly the freedom to add a component from the killed subspace, which is the polarisation count. The general problem stays open for arbitrary zero-divisor-valued functions.

Consequence 5: The Symbol Is Quadratic, and It Has Two Eigenvalues

One identity. Multiplying out $\tilde K_\Phi=ik_t'e_t+\vec k_\Phi$ gives

$$ \tilde K_\Phi^{2}-2ik_t'\,\tilde K_\Phi+s=0 , $$

with $s=\tilde K_\Phi\tilde K_\Phi^{\natural}=-(k_t^{\prime\,2}-k_\Phi^{2})$. The residual is zero, not small. So the wave-number element is quadratic over the centre, and its minimal polynomial is that one.

The two roots. They are $i(k_t'\pm k_\Phi)$, each of multiplicity two, and their product is $s$. Verified: the four eigenvalues of left multiplication by $\tilde K_\Phi$ are $i(k_t'-k_\Phi)$ twice and $i(k_t'+k_\Phi)$ twice, and the product of the two distinct values equals the norm.

The two roots are the two light-cone factors. $k_t'-k_\Phi$ and $k_t'+k_\Phi$ are the two factors of the hyperboloid. The mass shell is their product. Nothing in this display refers to a spinor, a gamma matrix or a chiral projector.

Consequence 22: A Doubler Is a Zero That the Circle Forces

The symbol stops being a polynomial and becomes a trigonometric polynomial. In the continuum the symbol of $\partial/\partial x_\mu$ is $k_\mu$, and its zero set is one point. On a lattice the symmetric difference has symbol $i\sin(k_\mu a)/a$, which is a trigonometric polynomial of degree one. That change of class is the whole of the difference, and it is a statement about Fourier series rather than about operators.

On a circle the wave number is not free to be odd. The points $k$ and $-k$ are distinct, but $k=-\pi/a$ and $k=+\pi/a$ are the same point. A real odd function therefore satisfies $f(\pi/a)=f(-\pi/a)=-f(\pi/a)$, hence $f(\pi/a)=0$. The Nyquist wave number is a forced zero of every real odd symbol. Measured on five unrelated odd functions — $\sin k$, $\sin k+0.3\sin 3k$, $\sin k\,(2+\cos k)$, $\sin k/(2-\cos k)$, $\sin 2k-0.1\sin 4k$ — the value at $\pi/a$ is below $3\times10^{-16}$ in every case. Every one vanishes, whatever its shape.

That single vanishing is the whole of the doubling. Each of the four directions contributes the same two zeros, $0$ and $\pi/a$, and the element symbol vanishes only where all four do. So the pole set is the sixteen corners $2^{4}=16$, and the temporal direction reproduces each spatial one twice. The corpus counts sixteen and calls the fifteen non-origin corners doublers. They are not fifteen independent accidents: they are what the oddness of one real function on one circle forces, raised to the fourth power.

Hermiticity is the price of the single zero, and the corpus's own article records the price without naming it. Measured on the periodic chain: the forward difference has $\lVert D+D^{\dagger}\rVert=2$, so it is not anti-Hermitian, and its symbol $(e^{ika}-1)/a$ has a real part $(\cos ka-1)/a$ that is even and reaches $-2/a$ at the Nyquist point. The backward difference gives $+2/a$. The symmetric difference is anti-Hermitian with an even part identically zero. So a symbol that is not odd carries an even part, and an even part in $\vec k$ is a mass. Escape from doubling costs either hermiticity or a $k$-dependent mass.

The Wilson term is exactly that even part, chosen to annihilate one zero and spare the other. Its symbol is $r(1-\cos ka)/a$: even, value $0$ at $k=0$ and $2r/a$ at $k=\pi/a$. So the physical pole stays at $s^2=m^2$ and the doubler moves to $s^2=(m+2r/a)^{2}$. Measured for $r=0.25$, $0.5$ and $1$: the shift is $0.5/a$, $1/a$ and $2/a$. The term does not remove a zero of the determinant. It converts one zero into a mass, and it does so by being the only parity the original symbol lacked.

The two-to-one map is a fold, and the fold sits at a quarter of the zone. Measured: $\sin(ka)$ has derivative $\cos(ka)$, which vanishes at $ka=\pi/2$. The two preimages of any symbol value are symmetric about $\pi/(2a)$, so the map from the zone to the symbol is two-to-one everywhere and folds at $\pi/(2a)$. The group velocity $d\omega/dk$ vanishes at that fold. A wave packet placed there does not move, and this is the lattice statement that the two branches meet.

The reading. In the continuum, distinct wave numbers give distinct symbols, so one momentum is one state. A lattice cannot keep that: its symbols are periodic, and a periodic odd real function has at least two zeros. Doubling is therefore not a defect of a particular discretisation. It is aliasing — the same symbol standing for two wave numbers — and Consequence 11 already said what aliasing does to an invariant. What the corpus's Nielsen–Ninomiya section calls the interplay of hermiticity, locality and chirality reduces, for the free symbol, to one line: a real odd function on a circle vanishes at the Nyquist point.

Consequence 27: The Mass Is the One Wave Number That Is a Value, Not a Variable

Of the corpus's three wave numbers, exactly two are symbols of derivatives. The Three Wave Numbers of a Particle writes $k_t'^{2}=k_\Phi^{2}+k_m^{2}$ for the energy, momentum and mass wave numbers. A derivative returns a symbol: $\partial/\partial R_k$ returns $ik_\Phi$ and $\partial/\partial r_t'$ returns $ik_t'$. There is no coordinate whose derivative returns $k_m$. The mass is not a Fourier variable, and this is not a convention: it is that no derivative exists for it to be the symbol of. The corpus's phrase "mass as a modulus" is therefore exact, and the transform says why.

The mass is the value of the dispersion at zero momentum, which is the gap. Measured at four masses: $\varpi(0)=m$ to machine precision, residual $0.00\times10^{0}$. The three are one point on a hyperbola, so there is one variable and not three. The count of three is what a partial transform produces, which ties this consequence to Consequence 24. Transform in all four and the count drops to two variables and one constraint; transform in one and the count is one variable and two parameters. The reading. A wave number is a symbol, and the corpus's three are treated as though they were three of a kind. This is why the corpus's loop integrals run over $\vec p$ and $p^{0}$ and never over $m$, and it is why a mass is a label on a field rather than a coordinate of it. The distinction is not semantic. Consequence 28: The Order in Wave Number Is the Degree in Position, and Both Are a Ladder of Regularity

Multiplication by a power of position is differentiation in wave number. For any field, $$ \mathcal F\{x^{n}f\}(k)=i^{n}\frac{d^{n}}{dk^{n}}\hat f(k). $$ Measured against the closed forms on $f=e^{-x^{2}/2}$, where the ratios are known exactly: the computed ratios match $-ik$, $1-k^{2}$, $i(k^{3}-3k)$ and $k^{4}-6k^{2}+3$ for $n=1,2,3,4$ with errors $7.1\times10^{-14}$, $2.1\times10^{-13}$, $6.3\times10^{-13}$ and $2.0\times10^{-12}$. The identity is exact and the errors are the grid.

So the class of equations the transform algebraises is graded, and the grade is the degree. A constant coefficient gives order zero: a number, and the equation is algebraic — Consequences 1 to 29. A linear coefficient gives order one. A quadratic one gives order two. The dividing line the article has used so far, "constant coefficients only", is the first rung of a ladder and not a wall. The exact statement is: a convolution kernel supported at the single point $k=0$ is a finite sum of derivatives of the delta, hence a finite-order differential operator, and such a kernel is the transform of a polynomial and of nothing else. Polynomial in position equals finite order in wave number, and the order is the degree.

Each rung is one of the corpus's solvable problems. Order zero is the free equation, and the whole of this article. Order one is the Dirac oscillator, and its operator is $\partial_{k}$ — which generates translations, measured to $9.1\times10^{-16}$ in the identity $e^{a\partial_{k}}\hat f(k)=\hat f(k+a)$. That is the exact counterpart of a constant potential, which translates the symbol: a constant potential translates the symbol and a linear potential translates the state, and the two facts are one derivative apart. A rigid translation of a spectrum is why a linear potential shifts every level by the same amount and none of the spacings. Order two is the harmonic oscillator, whose second-order equation in $k$ closes a three-term recursion and whose solutions are the Hermite functions — the corpus's ladder is exactly this, and its termination is the finiteness of the order.

Off the ladder, and the corpus knows it. The step potential of the Klein paradox has a transform that decays as $1/k$: measured, $|k\hat f|$ saturates at $2.0000$, the sum of its two jump sizes, and then falls. A decaying kernel is not the transform of a polynomial, so the $k$-space equation is an integral equation with no finite order, and no recursion terminates. The Coulomb potential is worse and more interesting: its transform decays as a power, measured by shell average over six shells as $|k|^{-2.08}$ with $|\hat f|\,|k|^{2}$ between $12.4$ and $11.2$ against the exact $4\pi=12.566$, the drift being the truncation of the box. So the Coulomb term in $k$-space is convolution with $1/|k|^{2}$, that is multiplication by $(-\Delta)^{-1}$: an operator of infinite order, the inverse of the Laplacian. This is why the corpus solves the Coulomb problem by the Duru–Kleinert transformation and not by Fourier. There is no polynomial to stand on: $1/r$ is not a polynomial, its image is nonlocal, and the algebraic method has nothing to say.

And the same ladder governs what an operator can do to regularity, which is the shock question. The order of decay of a transform counts the derivatives that fail. Measured: a pulse, whose value jumps, has $|k\hat f|$ bounded at $2.0000$ and $|k^{2}\hat f|$ growing; a tent, whose slope jumps, has $|k^{2}\hat f|$ bounded at $4.000$ and $|k^{3}\hat f|$ growing; the transform of the delta, which is the derivative of the step, has $|\hat f|$ bounded at $2.000$ with $|k\hat f|$ growing. The tail order is the order of the first broken derivative, exactly.

A polynomial symbol can only lower that order, never raise it. Multiplying by $(ik)^{m}$ maps a tail of order $p$ to one of order $p-m$, because $k^{p-m}(ik)^{m}\hat f=i^{m}k^{p}\hat f$ stays bounded. So an operator with a polynomial symbol makes a field rougher by exactly its degree, and cannot make it smoother by any amount. Smooth data has a tail of unbounded order, and unbounded order minus a finite degree is still unbounded. Smooth data stays smooth under every linear constant-coefficient equation, for all time, in every dimension. A shock is the appearance of a $1/k$ tail, so it needs an operation that is not multiplication by a polynomial: a convolution, that is a product of fields. Consequence 20 says a product has no symbol; this says the same thing at the tail, and between them they are the transform's answer to the question Shock Electromagnetic Waves opens with. Its three regimes — linear vacuum, nonlinear medium, plasma — are the three cases of one classification: polynomial symbol, no shock ever; convolution, shock possible; convolution hidden inside a constitutive law, the same.

The reading. The transform turns position into a derivative and a distribution into a rate of decay, and both are counted by one number. Degree in position is order in wave number; broken derivative in position is inverse power of wave number. An equation's solvability and its smoothing are read off the same ladder from opposite ends: from below, the degree decides how many terms a recursion needs, and from above, it decides how much regularity the operator can destroy. The corpus's four closed-form potentials — none, linear, quadratic, and the step — sit at rungs zero, one, two, and off the ladder, and its choice of method in each case is the rung it stands on.

Consequence 29: The Wick Rotation Is a Rotation of the Frequency, and Its Content Is Two Numbers That It Moves

The rotation acts on the wave number exactly as it acts on the time. Interpretation of the Wick Rotation in the Biquaternion Universe substitutes $r'_t\mapsto-i\tau$ and insists that this is a rotation of the contour. At the transform the two statements are one. A component $e^{i\omega t}$ evaluated at $t=-i\tau$ becomes $e^{\omega\tau}=e^{i\omega_4\tau}$ with $\omega=i\omega_4$: the frequency is multiplied by $i$, by the same factor and in the same direction as the time. Applied twice it is $\omega\mapsto-\omega$, the symbol of time reversal, so the article's $W^{2}=T$ is here the arithmetic $i^{2}=-1$ acting on the frequency. Checked as matrices, $W=\mathrm{diag}(-i,1,1,1)$ and $W^{2}=\mathrm{diag}(-1,1,1,1)$.

That is what a rotation of a contour is. A contour rotation changes the path of integration, and therefore changes the variable conjugate to the one transformed. The two operations the article finds sharing one name separate cleanly here: the substitution $r'_t\mapsto-i\tau$ is a statement about the contour of the transform, and the relabelling $ir'_t\mapsto c\tau$ is a statement about the reality condition on the coefficients, which the transform never sees. The article reaches that distinction by algebra. The transform states it in a line, and supplies the reason the first operation matters: it is the only one of the two that can move a singularity.

Consequence 5 meets the same hyperboloid-and-sphere pair, where a choice of conjugation decides between them; here the deciding operation is a rotation, and the difference is that a rotation is not an automorphism of the algebra. Nothing is being said about the algebra. Something is being said about the point at which the transform is evaluated.

The propagator's denominator is $\omega^{2}-k_\Phi^{2}-k_m^{2}$, with zeros at $\omega=\pm\sqrt{k_\Phi^{2}+k_m^{2}}$ on the real axis: distance zero, at every wave number. The reading. The Wick rotation is not a change of physics and not a change of algebra. Every exponential rate in the corpus is a distance from the real axis, and the rotation is the operation that creates one out of nothing.

Consequence 30: A Field Strength Is a Failure of Two Wave Numbers to Commute, and the States in a Landau Level Are the Flux

The corpus already prints the commutator, in position space. Landau Levels in Biquaternionic Form opens with the fact that the magnetic field enters as a central commutator: the two transverse mechanical momenta do not commute, their commutator is a pure imaginary multiple of $e_t$, and the flux density is recovered from it as a fixed multiple of $[\tilde\pi_x,\tilde\pi_y]$, verified there to $5\times10^{-11}$ on a smooth test function. That article also derives the oscillator algebra from it and counts the degeneracy in its fifth section. The consequence below is therefore an identification, not a new computation, and it is worth making because the transform states the same commutator in the conjugate variable, where a geometric content appears that position space hides.

In the Fourier plane the two operators are translation generators. With $\vec\pi=\vec k-e\vec A$ and a uniform $\vec B$, the potential is linear in position, so the transform sends $\vec A$ to $i\partial_{\vec k}$ and $\vec\pi$ to a pair of first-order operators in wave number: $\pi_x=k_x$ and $\pi_y=k_y+ieB\,\partial_{k_x}$. Then $$ [\pi_x,\pi_y]=ieB , $$ measured in this representation as $\bigl\|[\pi_x,\pi_y]f+ieBf\bigr\|/\|f\|=1.3\times10^{-9}$ on a smooth test field. The corpus verifies the same identity as an operator on position space; here the two operators are derivatives with respect to wave number, so the statement becomes: two translations of the Fourier plane fail to commute, and their failure is the field strength.

The consequence is a geometry, and it is the geometry of a non-commutative plane. A pair of coordinates that fail to commute by $ieB$ has a smallest cell, of area $2\pi/eB$. Everything the corpus computes follows from that area. The symbol $\pi_x^{2}+\pi_y^{2}$ is, after the similarity transform $e^{ik_yk_x/eB}$, exactly $-e^{2}B^{2}\partial_{k_x}^{2}+k_x^{2}$ — an oscillator in wave number whose eigenvalues are $eB(2n+1)$: measured $0.449996$, $1.349982$, $2.249954$, $3.149912$, $4.049856$ against $0.450$, $1.350$, $2.250$, $3.150$, $4.050$, first gap $0.899986$ against $2eB=0.900000$. No eigenvalue depends on $k_y$, which the similarity transform shows exactly and the numbers bear out — the corpus's "the energy is independent of $k$", obtained here without solving a boundary-value problem. The spacing is twice the field strength because the cell area is finite.

The degeneracy is then a ratio of areas. The magnetic translations obey $T_xT_y=e^{-ieBa^{2}}T_yT_x$, computed as $0.900447102353-0.434965534111i$ against $\exp(-ieBa^{2})$ to twelve decimals. A loop enclosing $N^{2}$ cells multiplies the field by a unit-modulus phase, measured $|{\cdot}|=1.000000000000$, whose angle is $N^{2}eBa^{2}$ modulo $2\pi$. Single-valuedness on a closed surface forces the total flux to be $2\pi$ times an integer, and the integer is the number of cells the plane can be tiled with: measured on an $L\times L$ torus with flux $2\pi/L$ per plaquette, the lowest level's multiplicity is $3$, $4$, $5$, $6$ for $L=3,4,5,6$. The number of states in a Landau level is the number of flux quanta, which is the area of the torus divided by the area of one cell. The corpus reaches that count through the boundary conditions of the Landau gauge; the transform reaches it as an area ratio, and the two must agree because they are counting the same cells.

The contrast with the mass in the duality article is the part the transform supplies. Dual Equivalence of the Dirac and Topologically Massive Gauge Fields records that the mass and the charge both enter as shifts of the covariant derivative, and that the mass is a non-integrable phase. At the transform the two are not alike, and the difference is a curvature. A shift by a closed one-form — that article's mass direction $K_\mu$, or a pure gauge $A_\mu=\partial_\mu\Lambda$ — is a translation of the symbol with zero curvature: the dispersion moves and the spectrum stays continuous, which is Consequence 8's constant-potential result. A shift by a form of non-zero curvature is a non-commutativity, and every discrete feature follows from it, including the quantization of its own strength. The mass moves the shell; the charge quantises the spectrum; the difference is the curvature of the shift. That statement is not in the duality article, which treats the two shifts as alike, and it is the reason a mass has no flux quantum.

Consequence 8 is right, and this is where its boundary lies. A field strength produces nothing the symbol can see, and a varying field strength produces nothing at all: it is a convolution and there is no multiplier. A uniform one does produce something, and what it produces is not a number but a failure of two wave numbers to commute. The Lorentz force needs a field varying in space, which is order two or worse; a uniform field is order one, and order one is exactly soluble.

The reading. The corpus's Limits section places Landau levels outside the method because they need a varying $\vec A$. The transform sharpens that into a positive statement. A uniform field strength sits on the ladder, at the same rung as the Dirac oscillator — but its first-order equation is an oscillator in wave number, and it is discrete because the wave-number plane has a smallest cell. The magnetic field is not a force in this picture; it is the curvature of the translation group of the Fourier plane, and the flux quantum is the area of that cell.

Consequence 31: A Repeated Root Is a Pole of Order Two, and Its Propagator Grows with the Time

The exceptional point is a statement about pole order, and the transform is where pole order lives. Exceptional Points and the Nilpotent Regime in the Biquaternion Spectrum proves the normal form $Q_tI_2+N$ with $N^{2}=0$, and separates it from the diabolic normal form $Q_tI_2$. The two have the same spectrum. At the transform the difference appears in the resolvent, where it is exact:

$$ (zI-J)^{-1}=\frac{1}{z-Q_t}I+\frac{1}{(z-Q_t)^{2}}N , $$

verified to $5.6\times10^{-17}$ at three complex $z$. The coefficient of the double pole is the nilpotent. At a diabolic point $N=0$ and the second term is absent, so the same eigenvalue coincidence produces two simple poles in one case and one double pole in the other. The spectrum cannot tell the two apart; the pole order can, and the pole order is what a transform reads.

A pole of order $m$ is a factor of $t^{m-1}$. Measured as the ratio of the inverse transform to $t^{m-1}e^{-\gamma t}$, constant in $t$ for every $m$: $6.281648$, $6.281538$ and $3.140683$ for $m=1,2,3$ — that is, $2\pi$ and $\pi$, the normalisations, with no residual dependence on $t$. The exceptional point sits exactly on the rung of Consequence 28 where the solution is a polynomial in time, and the size of the Jordan block is its degree.

Where it does not apply, and the transform agrees with the article. Three descriptions of one integer. The corpus computes the normal form, the transform reads the pole, and a detector sees a response that grows linearly instead of oscillating.

Consequence 32: The Causal Barrier Is a Growth Rate, and the Propagation Speed Is Its Slope

A support radius is read off the transform as an exponential rate. By the Paley–Wiener theorem, a function supported in $[-R,R]$ has a transform that is entire of exponential type $R$, and $$ R=\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\log\bigl|\hat f(-ib)\bigr| . $$ Measured on a $C^{\infty}$ bump of radius $1$: $0.39795817$, $0.58025586$, $0.71150692$, $0.80287960$, $0.86541704$ at $b=10,20,40,80,160$. The values rise towards $1$ and approach it slowly, and the slowness is itself informative: the type is a limit at infinite imaginary wave number, so the smoother the bump the longer the approach. The radius of a support is the distance from the real axis at which the transform stops being bounded by any exponential of smaller rate.

The wave equation adds the time to that radius, exactly. Take $u(x,t)=f(x-t)$, a solution of $\partial_t^{2}u=\partial_x^{2}u$. Its transform is $e^{ikt}\hat f(k)$, so the type at time $t$ exceeds the type at time $0$ by exactly $t$ — measured as $0.86541704$, $1.36541704$, $1.86541704$, $2.86541704$ at $t=0,\,0.5,\,1,\,2$, the increments being $0.5$, $1$, $2$ to every printed digit and independently of $b$. The rate of growth of the type is the speed. Finite propagation is not an extra axiom about the equation; it is the statement that the exponential type grows at a finite rate, and the rate is the speed of the front.

The degree in the symbol decides whether that rate is finite. For the heat equation the transform is $e^{-k^{2}t}\hat f(k)$; at $k=-ib$ the factor is $e^{b^{2}t}$, and the measured ratio grows with $b$ without bound — $0.98$, $1.51$, $2.40$ at $b=20,40,80$ for $t=0.02$, and $8.80$ at $b=80$ for $t=0.1$. That is a type that is not finite, which is to say a support that is not compact, which is infinite propagation speed. For the Schrödinger equation the transform is $e^{ik^{2}t}\hat f(k)$; along the ray $k=b(1-i)$ the factor is $e^{2b^{2}t}$ and the ratio grows as $0.94$, $1.61$, $2.82$ at $b=20,40,80$ for $t=0.02$: also infinite. The test is the power of $k$ in the exponent and whether it carries an $i$. An exponent linear in $ik$, multiplied by the time, gives a type growing at a constant rate and a finite speed. An exponent quadratic in $k$, with or without the $i$, gives no finite type at all.

This sharpens what the corpus cites as standard. Causality and the Light Cone as an Information Barrier records finite propagation speed as a theorem of the classical theory of hyperbolic equations, and Consequence 4 records the same shell as the zero set. Both are correct, and neither is quantitative. The transform supplies the number: the barrier is the statement that the analytic continuation of the transform is bounded by $e^{R|k|}$ with $R$ growing at the front speed, so the light cone is the boundary of the region in which the transform of the solution is of finite exponential type. One can measure a front speed by watching where the transform stops being entire of a given type, and that is a statement a numerical solution can be tested against.

The reading. A barrier is a growth rate. The corpus's distinction between the wave equations and the Schrödinger equation is usually stated as one of interpretation, and here it is a statement about a limit in the complex wave-number plane: one family has a finite type that grows linearly in the time, the other has no finite type at all. The equation that is causal is the equation whose symbol is linear in the imaginary wave number times the time; the equation that is not has degree two and pays for it with an infinite type.

Consequence 33: A Symbol That Changes in Time Has No Dispersion Relation, and the Deficit Is a Fourier Transform

The failure of Consequence 8 is measurable, and its measure is a transform. When the coefficient of an equation depends on the time, there is no multiplier in the frequency and the equation becomes a convolution: the corpus's time-dependent problems — Bogoliubov Transformations, the expanding background, the quench — are all of this kind. What the transform adds is that the amount of the failure is not a qualitative matter. For the mode equation $\ddot q+\omega^{2}(t)q=0$, the Bogoliubov coefficient to first order in the change is $$ \beta=\int dt\;\frac{\dot\omega(t)}{2\omega(t)}\,\exp\!\Bigl(-2i\int^{t}\omega\Bigr), $$ so $\beta$ is the Fourier component of the rate of change of the frequency, taken at twice the frequency. Verified against direct integration of the mode equation at $\epsilon=0.05$: exact $|\beta|^{2}$ of $2.919176\times10^{-4}$, $4.646778\times10^{-5}$, $3.486154\times10^{-7}$ against the formula's $2.918551\times10^{-4}$, $4.645688\times10^{-5}$, $3.485057\times10^{-7}$ at $\tau=0.5,\,1,\,2$ — ratios $0.9998$, $0.9998$, $0.9997$. A time-dependent symbol produces exactly what the transform of its own time dependence says it produces, and nothing else.

The adiabatic theorem is Consequence 28's tail theorem, read in the time variable. A smooth change has a transform that falls faster than any power, so $\beta$ is exponentially small; a change with a kink has a transform with a power tail, so $\beta$ is only a power small. Measured with identical endpoints and identical duration $\tau$, at $\tau=4$: $|\beta|^{2}=5.003606\times10^{-12}$ for the analytic profile and $9.648083\times10^{-6}$ for the kinked one. Six orders of magnitude, from smoothness alone. The corpus calls this the adiabatic theorem, and it is the same integer as Consequence 28's: the number of continuous derivatives of the profile at its corners is the power of the exponent, and analyticity is the case of infinitely many.

And the power law carries an interference pattern that the transform predicts. The kinked profile's transform is a $\mathrm{sinc}$, which vanishes when $\omega_0\tau$ is a multiple of $\pi$. Measured: $|\beta|^{2}$ falls from $2.269002\times10^{-5}$ at $\tau=2$ to $1.455708\times10^{-6}$ at $\tau=3$ and back up to $9.648083\times10^{-6}$ at $\tau=4$, a dip at the first zero $\tau=\pi/\omega_0=3.14$. The non-monotonicity is not noise; it is the zero of a sinc, and its position is a prediction of the transform that a direct integration of the equation confirms. This is the clearest case in the article where the transform says something a numerical solution could not have been trusted to reveal.

The reading. Constancy of the symbol is what gives a dispersion relation, and the departure from constancy is not an error but a quantity: the transform of $\dot\omega$. The adiabatic limit is the statement that this transform is small, the adiabatic theorem's exponential smallness is the statement that it is small faster than any power, and the interference dips are its zeros. A corpus that treats the slowly varying case as an approximation is here treating it as a tail, and the tail has a known order.

Consequence 34: A Projection Is an Indicator, so It Cannot Be Local

Idempotence is a pointwise condition on the symbol. Let $P$ be a multiplier with symbol $\chi$. Then $P^{2}=P$ holds if and only if $\chi(k)^{2}=\chi(k)$ for every $k$, that is, if and only if $\chi$ is $0$ or $1$ everywhere. Measured: the residual $\max|\chi^{2}-\chi|$ is exactly $0$ for the indicator of an interval, and $0.25$ for the Gaussian $e^{-k^{2}/2}$ and for the tent $(1-|k|)_{+}$. No smooth symbol is a projection, and no projection is smooth.

And the indicator's kernel is a sinc with a $1/x$ tail. For the band $[-B,B]$, $$ K(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-B}^{B}e^{-ikx}dk=\frac{\sin(Bx)}{\pi x}, $$ whose envelope is exactly $1/(\pi x)$: measured $0.31830989$ at $x=10.996,\,23.562,\,48.695,\,98.960,\,199.491$. An exactly idempotent multiplier is never local, and the tail's order is the order of the symbol's first broken derivative, exactly as in Consequence 28: the indicator gives $1/x$, the tent gives $(1-\cos x)/(\pi x^{2})$, and a symbol with no jump has no power tail — and is not a projection. Measured fitted slopes $-0.92$ and $-2.05$ over two decades.

So sharpness and range are conjugate, and the corpus's question has a second answer. Decoherence as Idempotent Projection establishes that full dephasing is a trace-preserving conditional expectation, an exact projection in the operator algebra, and it is careful to say that this is a statement about the algebra of operators and not about space. The transform adds the spatial half of that statement, and it is a negative one. A projection with a sharp band edge acts in space by convolution with a $1/(\pi x)$ kernel, so it has infinite range; a projection with a graded edge is not a projection at all. There is no operator that both projects exactly and acts locally, and the trade is quantitative: the width of the edge is the width of the range, reciprocally.

The lattice is where the obstruction disappears, and this closes a loop. On a lattice the band is closed by construction, so every symbol is periodic and an idempotent multiplier is available without any tail — which is Consequence 11's fourth-power lattice operator and Consequence 22's forced doubler seen from the other side. The circle closes the band and the line opens it: a finite band and a continuous space cannot both be had, and a projection is exactly what one cannot build without choosing. That is the same obstruction that Consequence 22 states as a zero the circle forces, stated as a tail the line forces.

The reading. The corpus asks whether decoherence is a projection, and answers yes at full strength in the algebra. The transform says that in space the answer is never yes exactly: idempotence forces an indicator, an indicator is a sinc, and a sinc has a $1/x$ tail. A measurement with a sharp resolution and a finite range does not exist; the resolution and the range are one number apart, and the number is the width of the band edge.

Consequence 35: A Medium With Memory Keeps a Symbol, and Causality Is Where Its Poles Are Not

There are two ways to leave the constant-coefficient case, and only one keeps a symbol. Consequence 33 treats a coefficient that changes in time: no multiplier survives and the equation becomes a convolution. A medium with memory is the other case, and it goes the other way. The permittivity becomes a function of the frequency, the symbol stays a symbol, $$ D(\omega,k)=k^{2}-\omega^{2}\epsilon(\omega)\mu(\omega), $$ and the dispersion relation the corpus prints, $k^{2}=\omega^{2}\epsilon(\omega)\mu(\omega)$, is exactly the statement $D=0$. A frequency-dependent response adds no new difficulty to the method: it makes the symbol a function instead of a polynomial. What it costs is that the symbol is no longer finite. It has poles, at the resonances of the response, and everything physical is then a statement about where those poles are.

Causality is the statement that no pole lies above the real axis, and its consequence is a transform. The response cannot precede its cause, so $\chi(t)=0$ for $t<0$, so its transform is analytic in the upper half plane, so the Cauchy integral of $\epsilon-\epsilon_0$ over a closed contour there vanishes. Split that vanishing into its real and imaginary parts and it becomes the pair $$ \mathrm{Re}\,\epsilon(\omega)=\epsilon_0+\frac{1}{\pi}\,\mathrm{PV}\!\int\frac{\mathrm{Im}\,\epsilon(\omega')}{\omega'-\omega}\,d\omega', \qquad \mathrm{Im}\,\epsilon(\omega)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\,\mathrm{PV}\!\int\frac{\mathrm{Re}\,\epsilon(\omega')-\epsilon_0}{\omega'-\omega}\,d\omega'. $$ Verified on a Lorentz oscillator with $\omega_p^{2}=1$, $\omega_0=1$, $\gamma=0.2$: the Hilbert transform of $\mathrm{Re}\,\epsilon-1$, computed over a grid of $2^{14}$ points on $[-40,40]$, reproduces $\mathrm{Im}\,\epsilon$ with a maximum relative deviation of $8.13\times10^{-6}$ on $|\omega|<6$. At $\omega=1.001$ the two values are $4.994279876$ and $4.994273045$. Absorption is not an independent property of a medium. It is fixed by the dispersion, point by point, and the fixing is a transform.

And the control fails when the pole moves. Flip the sign of the damping term, which puts the two poles at $0.99498744+0.1i$, and the same computation returns the negative of $\mathrm{Im}\,\epsilon$: relative deviation $2.0$, not $10^{-5}$. The causal model's measured poles are $0.99498744-0.1i$ and $-0.99498744-0.1i$. The difference between a medium that absorbs and one that emits before it is asked is the sign of one imaginary part, which is the side of the axis on which two poles sit.

The group velocity is a derivative of the symbol, and the corpus already prints it as one. The relation $D=0$ defines $k(\omega)$, so $$ v_g=\frac{d\omega}{dk}=-\frac{\partial D/\partial k}{\partial D/\partial\omega}, $$ which for $D=k^{2}-\omega^{2}\epsilon\mu$ is $1/(n+\omega\,dn/d\omega)$ — the formula the media article prints, read now as the derivative of the dispersion relation rather than as a separate piece of kinematics. Verified against a finite-difference derivative of $k(\omega)=\omega n(\omega)$: agreement to $8.5\times10^{-12}$ at every point tested, from $\omega=0.3$ to $\omega=3$.

Which makes the media article's two caveats one caveat. The Brillouin energy density $W=\tfrac12\bigl(\tfrac{d(\omega\epsilon)}{d\omega}\vec E^{2}+\tfrac{d(\omega\mu)}{d\omega}\vec H^{2}\bigr)$ contains the same derivative $\tfrac{d(\omega\epsilon)}{d\omega}$ that appears in the denominator of $v_g$. Both are $\partial_\omega$ of the symbol, and both depart from the non-dispersive expression at the same order. While the coefficient is constant the symbol and its derivative agree and the distinction is invisible; at the first resonance they separate, and the energy and the velocity separate with them.

What the derivative costs, measured. Near the resonance the same model gives $\mathrm{Re}\,v_g=-0.05438276$ at $\omega=0.99$ and $-0.13557074$ at $\omega=1.10$, with $|\mathrm{Im}\,v_g|$ equal to $0.076$ and $0.070$ there. The group velocity has gone negative and complex exactly where the medium absorbs hardest. A negative group velocity is not a signal travelling backwards; it is the statement that at that frequency the peak of a pulse has stopped meaning anything. The speed that survives there is the front speed of Consequence 32, which is an exponential type and is set by the pole nearest the real axis — the same poles, read for a different purpose.

The reading. The media article treats dispersion as a boundary of the framework and flags that its energy density is the non-dispersive one. The transform says the boundary is thinner than that. A frequency-dependent medium keeps an algebraic equation, keeps a symbol, keeps a dispersion relation, and gains a pair: real and imaginary parts that determine each other. What it loses is not the symbol but its polynomiality, and the price of a non-polynomial symbol is a pole, which is where the physics moves.

Consequence 36: The WKB Expansion Is the Stationary-Phase Expansion of the Inverse Transform

The corpus's semiclassical method is an asymptotic evaluation of a Fourier integral, and the transform says so term by term. Write a state as an inverse transform with a phase, $$ \psi(x)=\int\hat\psi(k)\,e^{ikx}\,dk=\int A(k)\,e^{iS(k)/\varepsilon}\,dk , $$ with $\varepsilon$ the small parameter. A semiclassical state is one whose transform is concentrated, and the integral is then dominated by the wave numbers where the phase is stationary. The whole WKB series is the expansion about those points, and each of its orders is one fact about the transform.

The amplitude is the Jacobian of the stationary-point map. At a non-degenerate stationary point $k_0$ with $S''(k_0)\neq0$, $$ \int A\,e^{iS/\varepsilon}\,dk \;=\; A(k_0)\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\varepsilon}{|S''(k_0)|}}\;e^{iS(k_0)/\varepsilon}\;e^{\pm i\pi/4}, $$ so the amplitude carries $1/\sqrt{|S''|}$. That factor is the van Vleck determinant, and it is the Jacobian of the map from stationary wave number to position: where a range of wave numbers focuses onto one point, the amplitude grows as the inverse square root of the focusing. Verified on a compactly supported amplitude with $S=\tfrac{c}{2}x^{2}$: measured $|I|$ against $\sqrt{2\pi\varepsilon/c}$ gives ratios $1.000012$, $1.000000$, $1.000003$, $1.000000$, $1.000000$, $1.000000$ for $c=0.5,\,1,\,4$ at $\varepsilon=10^{-3}$ and $10^{-4}$. The WKB amplitude is not a separate approximation. It is the leading term of the integral, and its square is an inverse Jacobian.

The $\pi/4$ is the sign of the curvature at the stationary point. The same formula carries $\pm\pi/4$ according to the sign of $S''$. Measured: $+0.78540$ for $S''=+1$ and $-0.78540$ for $S''=-1$, against the predicted $\pm\pi/4=0.785398$. The Maslov phase is not an extra rule: it is the argument of $\sqrt{\pm i}$.

With several stationary points the branches interfere, and the beat is fixed by the transform. The leading term is the sum of one contribution per stationary point, and the cross term oscillates in $1/\varepsilon$ with period $2\pi/|\Delta S|$, where $\Delta S$ is the phase difference of the two branches. Verified for $S(x)=x^{3}/3-20x$, whose stationary points are at $\pm\sqrt{20}$ and whose $\Delta S=-119.2569588$: sweeping $1/\varepsilon$ from $20$ to $21$ finds $19$ maxima of $|I|^{2}$ with mean spacing $0.05263889$ against the predicted $2\pi/|\Delta S|=0.05268611$. Two paths interfere with a fringe spacing that the transform fixes, so coherence between branches is a statement about a transform and not about an equation.

At a caustic the exponent itself changes, and that change is the Maslov index. Where $S''=0$ the formula above diverges and the true behaviour is different in kind. At a fold, $S=x^{3}/3+tx$, whose stationary points are at $x=\pm\sqrt{-t}$, the integral is an Airy function and $|I|$ scales as $\varepsilon^{1/3}$ instead of $\varepsilon^{1/2}$. Measured by fitting $\log|I|$ against $\log\varepsilon$ over $\varepsilon=10^{-3}$ to $3\times10^{-5}$, on the same amplitude: exponent $+0.3334$ for $S=x^{3}/3$, where $S''=0$ at the stationary point, against $+0.5000$ for $S=x^{2}/2$, where $S''=1$. The power of the semiclassical parameter is the codimension of the caustic — a half at a smooth point, a third at a fold, a quarter at a cusp — and each step down costs another $\pi/2$, which is the argument of a different special function's leading term.

The reading. The WKB article derives the amplitude–phase split, the transport equation, the Maslov index and the connection formulas inside the algebra. All four are one object here: the asymptotic expansion of a Fourier integral, ordered by the stationary points of the phase, with the integer of the expansion counted by the number of ways the map from wave number to position folds. The semiclassical limit is what a transform looks like when its symbol is concentrated, and the corpus's observation that the phase is central in $\mathbb{B}$ is the statement that this concentration happens on a real vector of the algebra.

Consequence 37: The Density of States Is the Coarea Weight $1/|\nabla\sigma|$

The support of a solution is not enough; the weight on the support is a coarea factor. Consequence 4 says the on-shell set is the zero set of the symbol, and Consequence 21 says the sectors of that set are the regimes of the phase. Neither says how much of a state sits at a given energy. That is fixed by the transform. For a symbol $\sigma(\vec k)$ the density of states is $$ \rho(E)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\delta\bigl(\sigma(\vec k)-E\bigr)d^{d}k =\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\oint_{\sigma=E}\frac{d\ell}{|\nabla_{\vec k}\sigma|}, $$ the second form being the coarea formula. The weight of a wave number on the shell is the reciprocal of the symbol's gradient, so a steep symbol carries little weight and a flat one carries a great deal, and nothing else enters.

Verified against a lattice count and against the shell integral. Binning the histogram of $\sigma=-2(\cos k_x+\cos k_y)$ over the whole Brillouin zone returns a total of $1.0000$ — the zone's area over $(2\pi)^{2}$ — so the counting is calibrated. The two sides of the formula then agree: at $E=-3$ the count gives $0.090758$ and the one-dimensional shell integral $0.091270$; at $E=1$, $0.141210$ and $0.141893$; at $E=2.5$, $0.099114$ and $0.099100$. A histogram and a line integral compute the same number because one is the coarea formula's left side and the other its right.

A linear symbol has a constant gradient, and that is why a Dirac cone has a linear density of states. For $\sigma=v|\vec k|$ the gradient is $v$ on every shell, so $\rho=E/(8\pi v^{2})$ in one quadrant. Measured against $\sigma=v(r)$, in a quadrant, at $E=0.2$ and $0.4$: ratios $0.99990$, $0.99991$ for $v=1$; $1.00016$, $1.00043$ for $v=2$; $1.00178$, $1.00096$ for $v=4$. The $1/v^{2}$ law is confirmed by the ratios of the same quantities across $v$, and the linearity by $\rho(0.4)/\rho(0.2)=2.00002$. The linear density of states of graphene is not a coincidence of the material: it is the statement that a linear symbol has a constant gradient, and the constant is the velocity.

A critical point of the symbol is exactly where the formula fails, and the failure has a known size. Where $\nabla\sigma=0$ the weight is infinite and the integral diverges. On the square lattice that happens at $\sigma=0$, and the divergence is logarithmic: the shell integral gives $0.222005$, $0.257114$, $0.292119$, $0.338514$, $0.373548$ at $E=0.2,\,0.1,\,0.05,\,0.02,\,0.01$, a slope of $-0.050651$, $-0.050503$, $-0.050633$, $-0.050543$ against the $-1/(2\pi^{2})=-0.050661$ of the same integral. The van Hove singularity is a critical point of the symbol, and its strength is the constant in front of the logarithm — the coarea formula's failure mode, quantified, and stable across grids from $3000^{2}$ to $12000^{2}$.

A flat band is the case where the shell stops being a set of measure zero. If $\sigma$ is constant on a surface then every wave number on that surface is on shell, $|\nabla\sigma|=0$ there, and $\rho$ is no longer a function of $E$: it is a delta carrying the weight of the whole surface. That is what a flat band is in the transform's language — a pole of the propagator on a surface instead of at a point, and therefore a degeneracy that no perturbation removes by moving a single wave number.

The reading. The transform gives three properties of a spectrum that the corpus states separately: the support of the state (Consequence 4), the weight on the support (the coarea factor), and the places where that weight is infinite (the critical points). They are one formula, and the third is where the inversion theorem stops being a theorem. A spectrum is a measure; its density is the reciprocal of the gradient of its symbol, and every singularity of the density is a place where that gradient vanishes.

Consequence 38: An Exactly Exponential Decay Is Impossible, and the Tail Is the Threshold's Transform

The survival amplitude is a transform of a positive measure, so every statement about a decay law is a statement about a spectrum. Write $$ A(t)=\int dE\;w(E)\,e^{-iEt}, \qquad w(E)=\rho(E)\,\bigl|\langle 0|E\rangle\bigr|^{2}\ge 0, $$ supported on the spectrum of the Hamiltonian. The three regimes of the decay law are then three regions of this one integral, and each is fixed by one feature of $w$ rather than by the dynamics.

An exact exponential requires a Lorentzian, and a Lorentzian has weight below the ground state. If $A(t)=e^{-iE_0t-\Gamma t/2}$ for all $t>0$, its transform is the Lorentzian $\frac{1}{2\pi}\Gamma\big/\bigl((E-E_0)^{2}+\Gamma^{2}/4\bigr)$, which is positive on the whole real line. The fraction of it lying below $E=0$ is $$ \frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\pi}\arctan\frac{2E_0}{\Gamma}, $$ measured $0.01590225$ at $E_0=1,\Gamma=0.1$; $0.00159154$ at $E_0=1,\Gamma=0.01$; $0.00318299$ at $E_0=5,\Gamma=0.1$. A Hamiltonian bounded below has no weight there at all, so the exponential decay law is excluded, and the size of the exclusion is this number — proportional to $\Gamma/E_0$, vanishing only for an infinitely narrow and infinitely long-lived state, which is the free-particle limit. The corpus states that the pole captures an intermediate window only; the transform says why the window cannot be widened to all times, and gives the deficit.

The short-time law is a statement about positivity, and it has no linear term. Because $w\ge0$ is a measure, its odd moments about the mean converge absolutely and the expansion has no linear term: $$ P(t)=1-(\Delta E)^{2}t^{2}+O(t^{4}), $$ with $(\Delta E)^{2}=\int w\,E^{2}\big/\int w-\bigl(\int w\,E\big/\int w\bigr)^{2}$. Measured on a band $[0,1]$ with $w=\sqrt{E(1-E)}$, where $\Delta E=0.25$: $1-P=6.2499998\times10^{-8}$, $2.4999997\times10^{-7}$, $9.9999958\times10^{-7}$ at $t=10^{-3},\,2\times10^{-3},\,4\times10^{-3}$, against $(\Delta E)^{2}t^{2}=6.25\times10^{-8}$, $2.5\times10^{-7}$, $10^{-6}$ — ratios $1.000000$ to seven digits. The exponential's linear term is wrong at the origin by construction, and the crossover is at $t\sim1/\Delta E$, which is the inverse bandwidth. This is the corpus's "very short times" caveat, with the coefficient supplied.

The long-time tail has three exact numbers, not one. If $w\sim E^{\alpha}$ at the threshold, the transform's endpoint contributes $$ A(t)\sim\Gamma(\alpha+1)\,e^{-i\pi(\alpha+1)/2}\,t^{-(\alpha+1)}, $$ so the exponent, the constant and the phase are all fixed. Verified on half-line spectra $w=E^{\alpha}e^{-E}$ at $t=50$ to $800$: fitted slopes $-0.99994$, $-1.49983$, $-1.99987$ against $-1$, $-1.5$, $-2$ for $\alpha=0,\,\tfrac12,\,1$; the scaled moduli $t^{\alpha+1}|A(t)|$ converge to $0.99999$ and $1.00000$ against $\Gamma(1)=\Gamma(2)=1$, and to $0.88623,\,0.88629,\,0.88642$ against $\Gamma(3/2)=0.88623$; and the phases are $-1.57030,\,-2.35631,\,-3.14059$ against $-\pi/2=-1.570796$, $-3\pi/4=-2.356194$, $-\pi=-3.141593$. The tail is not merely "a power law set by the density of states at the band edge": it is the edge's exponent plus one, weighted by a gamma function and rotated by a fixed angle.

And a band has two edges, so its tail is an interference. For $w=\sqrt{E(1-E)}$ the two edges each contribute a $t^{-3/2}$ term with relative phase $e^{-it}$, and $t^{3/2}|A(t)|$ is not constant but beats: measured $3.54544$, $3.73511$, $3.90047$, $4.17099$, $4.36400$, $4.51339$, $4.34436$ at $t=50,\,75,\,100,\,150,\,200,\,300,\,400$. A power-law tail of a bounded spectrum is a sum over its thresholds, and the sum shows as an oscillation on top of the power.

The reading. The unstable-state article is careful to state its result as an approximation with a window. The transform makes the window quantitative on both sides and explains why there is a window at all: the survival amplitude is a transform of a positive measure, an exponential is the transform of a Lorentzian, and a Lorentzian is not a measure on a half-line. Decay is not a law but a window between a quadratic forced by positivity and a power forced by the threshold, and the pole is what sits in between.

Consequence 39: Levinson's Theorem Is the Argument Principle on a Transform, and It Counts Zeros Rather Than Poles

Consequence 35 read the upper half plane for the absence of poles. Read it again for the presence of zeros and it counts bound states. The $S$-matrix is analytic above the real axis for the same reason the susceptibility is — the outgoing wave is causal — and its zeros there are the bound states, at $k=i\kappa$ with $\kappa>0$. The number of them is therefore a winding number of the transform's phase, $$ n=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint d\arg S(k)=\frac{1}{\pi}\bigl[\delta(0)-\delta(\infty)\bigr], $$ the second form for a single channel with phase shift $\delta$. The count of bound states is a property of the phase of a transform along a contour, and nothing else.

Verified on a square well, over five bound states. With $2m=\hbar=a=1$ and depth $V_0$, counting the solutions of $K\cos K+\sqrt{V_0-K^{2}}\sin K=0$ and separately winding the phase of $S(k)$ computed from the matching condition: $V_0=0.5,\,2,\,5,\,10,\,20,\,40,\,100$ give $n=0,0,1,1,1,2,3$ and $\Delta\delta_0/\pi=-0.00000,\,-0.00008,\,-0.99987,\,-0.99981,\,-0.99968,\,-1.99933,\,-2.99838$. At $V_0=20$ the unwrapped phase runs from $\delta_0(10^{-4})=3.141459$ to $\delta_0(10^{5})=-0.000033$, a total of $3.141492$ against $\pi=3.141593$. Two computations that share no line of code give the same integer.

So one contour yields both of the article's analyticity results, and they are the two halves of the same integral. Consequence 35 asked where the poles are not, and got the Kramers–Kronig pair. This asks where the zeros are, and gets the bound-state count. Both are Cauchy's theorem on the same semicircle: the integral of $d\log(\epsilon-\epsilon_0)$ vanishes because there is nothing inside, and the integral of $d\log S$ is $2\pi i$ times the count because there are $n$ things inside. A causal medium has an empty upper half plane; a confining potential has a populated one; the same contour integral reads both.

And the count fails exactly where the transform stops being integrable, which is $k=0$ and not large $k$. For the Coulomb potential $\delta(0)$ does not exist: the transform $-4\pi Z/k^{2}$ is not integrable at zero wave number, the phase shift diverges logarithmically as $\sigma_0\sim\eta\ln\eta-\eta-\pi/4$ with $\eta=Z/k$ — measured $+12.24$, $+359.73$, $+5906.97$, $+82102.62$ at $k=0.1,\,0.01,\,10^{-3},\,10^{-4}$ — and the spectrum has infinitely many bound states, so no finite winding counts them. Levinson's theorem is a statement about short-range potentials because it is a statement about a transform that is integrable at the origin; this is the same non-integrability that Consequence 28 records for the Coulomb term, seen now as the failure of a count rather than the failure of an algebra.

The reading. A number of states is a winding number, and a winding number is a property of the phase of a transform. The corpus states Levinson's theorem as a quantisation condition on the phase shift; the transform identifies it as the argument principle and locates its hypothesis at the one point where the potential's transform can fail. Binding is a zero in the upper half plane, and counting bound states is counting turns.

Consequence 40: The Optical Theorem Is an Identity of the Transform, and Unitarity Only Makes Its Terms Cross-Sections

The elastic cross-section is a norm, and the norm is preserved by a transform. That is its whole content. For a profile $S(b)$ in the impact-parameter plane, $$ f(\vec q)=\frac{k}{2\pi i}\int d^{2}b\;e^{i\vec q\cdot\vec b}\bigl(S(b)-1\bigr), \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{el}}=\int d\Omega\,|f|^{2}=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\int d^{2}q\,|f|^{2}=\int d^{2}b\,\bigl|1-S\bigr|^{2}, $$ the last equality being Plancherel for the two-dimensional transform, with $d\Omega=2\pi q\,dq/k^{2}$ under $q=2k\sin(\theta/2)$. The statement that a cross-section is the squared modulus of an amplitude is the statement that a transform preserves an $L^{2}$ norm, and it holds for any profile whatsoever.

The total cross-section is the same transform at zero momentum transfer, which is the mean of the profile. Since $f(0)$ is the $\vec q=0$ Fourier component, $$ \sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}=\frac{4\pi}{k}\,\mathrm{Im}\,f(0)=2\int d^{2}b\,\bigl(1-\mathrm{Re}\,S\bigr). $$ And the pointwise identity $$ 2\bigl(1-\mathrm{Re}\,S\bigr)=\bigl|1-S\bigr|^{2}+\bigl(1-|S|^{2}\bigr) $$ holds for every complex $S$, by expansion and nothing else. Verified on a $2048^{2}$ lattice with $L=16$ and $k=1$, for $S=\exp\bigl(i(2\delta_0+2i\,a)e^{-b^{2}/2}\bigr)$: $\sigma_{\mathrm{el}}$ from the $q$-space integral and from the $b$-space integral agree with ratio $1.0000000$ in every case, and $\sigma_{\mathrm{el}}+\sigma_{\mathrm{inel}}$ against $\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}=(4\pi/k)\mathrm{Im}\,f(0)$ agrees to $8\times10^{-15}$. Values: $1.114144$ at $\delta_0=0.3$; $10.648516$ at $\delta_0=1$; $26.445823$ at $\delta_0=2$; and with absorption $a=0.3$ at $\delta_0=1$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}=13.858046$ split as $\sigma_{\mathrm{el}}=8.090424$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{inel}}=5.767622$. The optical theorem is algebra plus Plancherel. No dynamics enters it.

What unitarity adds is that the two terms are counts, not numbers. The condition $|S(b)|\le1$ makes $1-|S|^{2}\ge0$ at every $b$, so the identity becomes a sum rule over non-negative terms and therefore a bound, $\sigma_{\mathrm{el}}\le\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}$. Relax it and the identity survives arithmetically while the interpretation dies: a profile with gain somewhere returns a negative inelastic term, which is a cross-section that subtracts. Unitarity is not what proves the theorem; it is what makes the theorem a statement about probabilities.

Read as moments, the theorem says twice the mean deficit equals the variance plus the incoherence. The left side is a mean of the profile; the elastic term is its $L^{2}$ distance from unity, which is a variance; the inelastic term is the mean of $1-|S|^{2}$, which is the probability of leaving the elastic channel. Measured over $\delta_0$ from $0.2$ to $1.0$ with $|S|=1$ throughout, the inelastic term is $8\times10^{-15}$ and the other two are equal. The total cross-section measures how much the profile differs from unity on average; the elastic one measures how much it differs from unity in square mean; and the identity is the bridge between the two.

The reading. In Consequence 34 a pointwise algebraic condition — idempotence — became a nonlocal tail. Here a pointwise inequality becomes an integral identity. The transform is the machine that moves statements between the pointwise and the integral, and the optical theorem is the cleanest case: it is not a dynamical law that unitarity implies, it is Plancherel applied to a profile, with $|S|\le1$ promoted from an ingredient to a consistency condition. The forward direction knows the total because the forward direction is the mean.

Consequence 41: On a Circle the Transform Is a Geometric Series, and Statistics Is the Sign of Its Ratio

The Matsubara frequencies are a Fourier series, so the discrete sum has a closed form that is one line of algebra. On a circle of circumference $\beta$ the transform of the Green's function is a series, and its inverse is a sum over images separated by $\beta$. Each image is the previous one multiplied by $e^{-\beta E}$, so the series is geometric with ratio $q=e^{-\beta E}$: $$ \frac{1}{\beta}\sum_n\frac{1}{\omega_n^{2}+E^{2}} =\frac{1}{2E}\coth\frac{\beta E}{2} =\frac{1}{2E}\,\frac{1+q}{1-q}, \qquad \omega_n=\frac{2\pi n}{\beta}. $$ Fermions differ by one sign. The antiperiodic identification gives each image the opposite sign, so the ratio is $-q$ and the same series returns $$ \frac{1-q}{1+q}=\tanh\frac{\beta E}{2}. $$

Verified. The sum over images and the closed form agree to $0.00\times10^{0}$ relative at $\beta=2$, $E=0.7$, $\tau=0.4$, and the successive ratios are $0.2465969639$ four times against $e^{-\beta E}=0.2465969639$. The bosonic series against $\coth$ agrees to $6\times10^{-7}$ relative and the fermionic against $\tanh$ to $2\times10^{-7}$, the residual being truncation of the sum at $|n|=2\times10^{6}$. And $(1+q)/(1-q)=1.6546216358$ against $\coth(\beta E/2)=1.6546216358$; $(1-q)/(1+q)=0.6043677771$ against $\tanh(\beta E/2)=0.6043677771$.

The reading. The corpus states that the spin–statistics theorem fixes periodic versus antiperiodic identification, and that the thermal factor is then $\coth$ or $\tanh$. The transform says what the two letters are: one geometric series, and the statistics is the sign of its ratio. $\coth$ and $\tanh$ are the same sum with the images added or alternating, and the only thing that decides which is a sign in the identification. The series converges because $|q|<1$, that is because $\beta E>0$: the thermal sum is well defined because the temperature is positive, and it diverges at $\beta\to0$, which is the classical limit where the circle shrinks to a point and the discrete sum becomes the divergent integral.

Consequence 42: The Count Is the Hermitian Form and Not the Norm, and It Carries One Derivative Fewer Than the Equation's Order

The transform turns a density into a number only if the density is the right bilinear, and the corpus has two. The state module lies on the zero-divisor cone, so the quaternion norm $\langle\tilde\Xi\rangle_{\natural}$ vanishes on every state, while the Hermitian form $\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde\Xi^{\dagger}\tilde\Xi)$ does not. On the idempotent $\tilde\Pi_1$ the determinant is $0$ and the trace is $1$; on $\tilde\Pi_1(1+a\tilde e_x)$ the determinant stays $0$ while the trace runs $1.090$ and $3.890$ at $a=0.3$ and $1.7$. A conserved count built on the quaternion norm would count nothing, so the particle arm of the duality cannot be the norm of the element: it is the Hermitian form on the element.

And the density must carry as many derivatives as the equation has time derivatives, minus one. Transforming $\rho$ alone is not a closed statement: the transform of the continuity equation is linear in the transformed density and current but quadratic in the modes of the state, because $\rho$ is a convolution. The one place the count is a probability rather than a charge is the first-order case, where the density has no derivative and is definite; for the Klein–Gordon equation the density carries one derivative, $\rho=\frac{i}{2k_m}(\tilde\Xi^{*}\partial_{r'_t}\tilde\Xi-\tilde\Xi\partial_{r'_t}\tilde\Xi^{*})$, and the sign follows the frequency, $\pm\frac{\omega}{ck_m}\lvert N\rvert^2$. That indefiniteness is not a defect of the transform; it is the statement that a second-order equation has two initial data, so no single-element form can be its count.

The reading. The two arms of the duality rest on two different quadratic structures — the Hermitian form on the state and the quaternion norm of the gradient — and the reason they must differ is that the state module is degenerate. The transform preserves a number; which number it preserves is a choice of bilinear, and the choice is forced by the order of the equation.

Note: The Mellin Transform

The following is a note and not a finding. It records the one transform that the article's rule does not cover, and says where it would earn its place.

THE MELLIN TRANSFORM

The Fourier transform is diagonal on translation. A dilation is not a translation,
so the Fourier transform is blind to it: multiplying the argument of a function by a
constant leaves no multiplier in the wave number. That is why a homogeneity degree is
invisible in the symbol.

Change the variable. Put u = log k. A dilation becomes a shift, u -> u + log L, and the
transform diagonal on shifts of u is the Fourier transform in u. That is the Mellin
transform,

    M(s) = integral over x > 0 of x^(s-1) f(x) dx .

Two consequences, both elementary.

1. A pure power is a pole. For f(x) = x^a the transform is M(s) = 1/(s-a): the exponent
   is the location of a pole in s, and the coefficient is its residue. A sum of powers
   is a sum of poles. This is the exact analogue of a plane wave being a delta in the
   wave number: the same duality, with the exponent in place of the momentum.

2. It reads scale structure that the Fourier transform throws away. A covariance under
   dilation, a scaling dimension, and the degree of homogeneity of a symbol are all
   statements about poles in s. The massless/massive dichotomy is the cleanest case: a
   massless symbol is homogeneous of degree two, so its Mellin transform is one pole; a
   mass breaks the homogeneity and adds a second.

WHERE IT WOULD BE USEFUL IN THIS CORPUS

- Conformal invariance and the massless limit: the dilation Ward identity is a shift in
  u, so its conservation law is a statement about residues, and the massless fixed point
  is the case of a single pole.
- Dimensional analysis and a running coupling: an anomalous dimension is a displaced
  pole, and a beta function is the motion of that pole with the scale.
- Power-law tails: a decay with exponent alpha is a pole at s = alpha, which is the
  scale-space counterpart of the threshold behaviour already recorded.
- Self-similar profiles: a scale-invariant solution is an eigenfunction of dilation, so
  it is a single Mellin mode, and a fractional operator is a multiplication in s.

WHERE IT DOES NOT APPLY, STATED PLAINLY

The Mellin variable needs a positive real argument, so it acts on |k| or on a radius,
never on the biquaternion wave vector. It is not a second biquaternion Fourier transform,
it does not commute with the rotor, and it does not turn an equation into an algebraic
one. It is an ordinary scalar transform on a scalar variable that the algebra's own
transform leaves untouched. A finding is a statement about a symbol; this is a statement
about a homogeneity degree, and the two are different kinds of statement.

Summary

The mass shell is deduced. In the Klein–Gordon case, $-s-k_m^{2}=0$ gives $k_t^{\prime\,2}-k_\Phi^{2}=k_m^{2}$ in one step. In the Dirac case the deduction is the substance: two coupled first-order equations have a solvability locus of one scalar condition, because the block determinant is $\bigl(-s-k_m^{2}\bigr)^{4}$, a fourth power of one number, and that because the quaternion norm is central. The quaternionic analogue of $\det(\gamma\!\cdot\! p-m)=(p^{2}-m^{2})^{2}$. What the shell gains from this is that it is a solvability condition rather than a postulate, and that it is the zero set of a central norm, hence rotor-invariant with no further argument. What is not deduced is the value of $k_m$, and the indefinite form, which comes from $R_t=i\,r'_t$.

This is a second route to a shell the corpus already has. Relativistic Mechanics from the Dirac Wave reaches the same box by an eikonal expansion, to relative error $\epsilon^{2}$, and at $\epsilon=0$ exactly. The transform route is exact and carries no connection; the eikonal route carries $\phi$ and $\vec A$ and gives the flow and the Lorentz force. Their meeting point is the plane wave, where both are exact and where the first article's leading-order step, "the gradient acts as multiplication by $i\tilde K_\Phi$", becomes this article's identity.

The two pairings are separated by a surface. The unconjugated square becomes $-\tilde K_\Phi^{2}$, which is an element, whose vector part $2ik'_t\vec k_\Phi$ cannot equal a mass. That display therefore forces $k_t^{\prime\,2}+k_\Phi^{2}=k_m^{2}$ together with $k'_t\vec k_\Phi=0$, a Euclidean sphere with no propagating branch.

The massless transform lives on the cone. $\tilde\nabla\tilde F=0$ transforms to $i\tilde K_\Phi\hat{\tilde F}=0$, so the transform is supported on the null cone and takes its values in a minimal right ideal of complex dimension two.

The symbol is quadratic, and its two eigenvalues are the light-cone factors. $\tilde K_\Phi^{2}-2ik_t'\tilde K_\Phi+s=0$, with roots $i(k_t'\pm k_\Phi)$ of multiplicity two and product $s$. Every analytic function of the symbol is therefore linear in it. The cone is the vanishing of one root, and the two-dimensional kernel of Consequence 4 is that root's multiplicity, not a separate count.

The Schrödinger equation is not a norm. Its symbol is affine, so no element of $\mathbb{B}$ has it as a norm. It is the rest clock divided out, and the price is a quartic error $k_\Phi^{4}/(8k_m^{3})$ and a surface that stays strictly inside the cone, at a gap never smaller than $k_m/2$.

A zeroth-order term shifts a level or splits it. The symbol equation becomes an eigenvalue problem for left multiplication by that term. A central term moves the shell; a non-central one splits it in two, with the two-level bound coming from the two-by-two matrix structure of $\mathbb{B}$.

The transform stops at a constant potential. A constant potential translates the symbol and nothing more. A field strength makes the symbol a convolution and then there is none, which is why the Lorentz force belongs to the eikonal and not to this method.

The operator determinant is a product of one number. $\log\det\mathcal O=\sum\log\sigma$ with $\sigma=-s-k_m^{2}$ central, so the spinor trace is replaced by an exponent equal to the dimension of the space. Its imaginary part is $\pi$ times the number of modes beyond the shell, $83$ of $144$ on a $12\times12$ grid, and that is where the $i\epsilon$ comes from.

A source is admissible when it lies in the image of the symbol. Maxwell's field is a pure vector, so its symbol has a one-dimensional cokernel off the cone, and the corpus's integrability condition is exactly that cokernel. The condition is scalar because the domain has codimension one. On the cone the rank drops and the count becomes two.

The fourth power is algebraic and the number of zeros is not. The lattice reproduces $\bigl(m^{2}+\tilde K_\Phi\tilde K_\Phi^{\natural}\bigr)^{4}$ unchanged, so the exponent comes from the centrality of the norm. It does not reproduce the zero count: one in the continuum, sixteen on a lattice. And the lattice bracket is positive definite, so the phase of Consequence 9 is created by the continuation of the temporal component and by nothing else.

A first-order direction gives an eigenvalue, not a frequency. Transform where the equation is second order and you get a constraint. Transform where it is first order and you get a flow, whose rate is a pure vector and whose exponential is a rotor of unit modulus. That is one rule covering Schrödinger, the parabolic Dirac pair and the oscillator.

The mass makes the constraint and buys a polarisation. The Proca operator is the d'Alembertian, so its symbol is the norm and the shell is central: one line, no projection. On that shell the symbol has rank zero, four components are allowed, the Lorenz condition cuts them to three, and Maxwell on its own shell keeps two. Zitterbewegung is the beat between the two roots. The frequency is $c$ times the root separation, $2c\,k_t'$, and the amplitude is the reciprocal of the separation at rest, $1/(2k_m)$. Measured in a one-dimensional Dirac simulation to a relative error of $7\times10^{-5}$ at rest and $8\times10^{-4}$ in motion. Both numbers of the effect are functions of one number.

A resonance is a root that left the real sheet. The harmonic projection has rank $4$, the residual gauge change has rank $4$, and the transverse-traceless space has dimension $2$; the three fit together with zero intersection. The gauge change stays inside the harmonic space only when $K^{2}=0$.

A nonlinear closure has no symbol, and its wave is a Fourier ladder. The transform turns a product into a convolution, so Petiau's closed system has no symbol at all. Its elliptic wave still decomposes: odd harmonics only, in exact geometric ratio $q(1-q^{2n-1})/(1-q^{2n+1})$, with the modulus as the width of the ladder. Wave–particle duality is therefore a spectral width, and the fixed amplitude moves the freedom into that width.

Bound, antibound and resonance are three locations of one zero. The scattering problem is one denominator, unitary on the real axis to $2\times10^{-16}$. Its zeros give the bound state on the positive imaginary axis, the antibound state on the negative one, the resonance in the lower half plane, and propagation on the axis itself. The number of bound states is a count of zeros on a bounded ray.

A doubler is a zero that the circle forces. A lattice symbol is a trigonometric polynomial, and on a circle a real odd one must vanish at the Nyquist wave number, because $\pi/a$ is its own negative. A single direction gives two zeros, so the pole set is $2^{4}=16$ and the doublers are aliases. Escaping them costs hermiticity or an even part, and an even part in $\vec k$ is a mass, which is exactly what the Wilson term is.

A multi-mass field is a partial fraction. Lorentz covariance collapses the determinant of the symbol to a polynomial in $s^{2}$ alone, of degree $N/2$; its roots are the masses. The propagator then splits into one simple pole per mass, with residue the inverse spacing $1/(m_i^{2}-m_j^{2})$, which is not free. Coincident masses make the residue diverge and the decomposition fail.

A factorisation splits the polarisation, not the support. The two first-order factors of $\Box$ have the same zero set, because the cone is irreducible; what they split is the kernel, into two complementary ideals. The two conjugate function theories of the corpus are those two ideals, and no factor is the retarded half.

Statistics is a twisting of the Fourier series on the thermal circle. Periodic and antiperiodic boundary conditions give the two Matsubara ladders, which differ by the zero mode alone; the infrared divergence of the bosonic sum and the decoupling of fermions at high temperature are that one point.

A reality condition is a reflection of the spectrum. Conjugation in space is $k\to-k$ in momentum, so it always pairs a mode with another and never with itself. Its fixed space is exactly half the spectrum, which is Dirac's eight against Majorana's four, and the sign of its square lives in the internal fibre and never in $k$. It survives the flow when it commutes with the derivative and anticommutes with the mass.

The mass is a value, not a variable. Of the corpus's three wave numbers, two are symbols of derivatives and one is not; the mass is the value of the dispersion at zero momentum, which is the gap, and the ratio of the other two is the group velocity. A variable can be integrated over and a parameter cannot, and that is the whole difference.

The degree of a potential is the order of its image. Multiplication by $x^{n}$ is $i^{n}\partial_{k}^{n}$, so the transform of a polynomial potential of degree $n$ is a differential operator of order $n$ and the transform of anything else is an integral equation. The same ladder read from the other end counts regularity: the tail of a transform is $1/k^{p}$ where $p$ is the order of the first broken derivative, so no equation with a polynomial symbol can produce a shock.

The rotation of a contour is the creation of a distance. The Wick rotation multiplies the frequency by $i$ exactly as it multiplies the time, so $W^{2}=T$ is $i^{2}=-1$ read at the wave number. It turns a zero set into a gap, and a pole at distance zero into a pole at distance $\sqrt{k_\Phi^{2}+k_m^{2}}$. Since a distance from the real axis is a decay rate, every exponential rate in the corpus is such a distance, and the rotation is the operation that makes one.

A uniform field strength is two wave numbers that do not commute. $[\pi_x,\pi_y]=ieB$ as a number, so the wave-number plane has cell area $2\pi/eB$ and the first-order image is an oscillator in $k$ with gap $2eB$. Single-valuedness of the magnetic translations on a closed surface quantises the flux, and the number of states in a level is the number of flux quanta: $3,4,5,6$ measured at $L=3,4,5,6$.

A repeated root is a pole of order two. The exceptional normal form $Q_tI_2+N$ has resolvent $(z-Q_t)^{-1}I+(z-Q_t)^{-2}N$, so the nilpotent is the coefficient of the double pole. A pole of order $m$ contributes exactly $t^{m-1}$, measured for $m=1,2,3$, so a wave equation with that symbol grows linearly — $|u|/t=1.000000$ — while its diabolic partner stays bounded. The resonance pole $z_\star$ is simple; the exceptional point is double.

The barrier is a growth rate. By Paley–Wiener the radius of a support is the exponential type of the transform, $R=\lim_{b\to\infty}b^{-1}\log|\hat f(-ib)|$, measured $0.398\ldots0.865\to1$ for a bump of radius one. The wave equation adds exactly $t$ to the type at every $b$, so the front speed is the slope of the type. The heat and Schrödinger equations have no finite type at all, and that is their infinite speed.

A time-dependent symbol has no dispersion relation, and the deficit is a transform. To first order $\beta=\int dt\,(\dot\omega/2\omega)e^{-2i\int\omega}$, verified to $0.02\%$. The adiabatic theorem is the tail ladder in the time variable: at $\tau=4$ an analytic profile gives $5.0\times10^{-12}$ and a kinked one $9.6\times10^{-6}$, and the kink's $\mathrm{sinc}$ zero is the measured dip at $\tau=3$.

A projection is an indicator, and an indicator is not local. $P^{2}=P$ forces the symbol to be $0$ or $1$ pointwise, so every exact multiplier projection is a band indicator, whose kernel is $\sin(Bx)/(\pi x)$ with envelope exactly $1/\pi$; a smooth symbol is never a projection, and a projection is never smooth. Sharpness and range are conjugate: on a lattice the band closes and the obstruction disappears, which is Consequence 22 seen as a tail rather than a zero.

A medium with memory keeps a symbol, and causality is the position of its poles. $D=k^{2}-\omega^{2}\epsilon(\omega)\mu(\omega)$ still vanishes algebraically, and causality makes $\epsilon$ analytic above the real axis, so its real and imaginary parts are a Hilbert pair: verified to $8.1\times10^{-6}$, and failing with a sign flip when the damping changes sign. The group velocity is $-D_k/D_\omega$, which reproduces the corpus's $1/(n+\omega\,dn/d\omega)$ to $10^{-12}$ and makes the two stated caveats one derivative.

The WKB expansion is the stationary-phase expansion of the inverse transform. Amplitude $1/\sqrt{|S''|}$ with ratios $1.000000$; Maslov phase $\pm\pi/4$ measured as $\pm0.78540$; two branches beat with period $2\pi/|\Delta S|$, measured $0.05263889$ against $0.05268611$; and at a fold the exponent drops from $1/2$ to the measured $1/3$, so the Maslov index is the codimension of the caustic.

The density of states is the coarea weight $1/|\nabla\sigma|$. Histogram and shell integral agree to $0.6\%$; a linear symbol has a constant gradient and hence a linear density of states, verified for $v=1,2,4$; a critical point of the symbol gives the measured van Hove slope $-0.050651$ against $-1/(2\pi^{2})=-0.050661$; a flat band is a delta on a surface.

An exactly exponential decay is impossible, and the tail is the threshold's transform. An exponential requires a Lorentzian spectrum, which carries a fraction $\tfrac12-\pi^{-1}\arctan(2E_0/\Gamma)$ of its weight below the ground state, measured $0.01590225$ at $E_0=1,\Gamma=0.1$; the short-time law is $1-(\Delta E)^{2}t^{2}$ with no linear term, ratio $1.000000$; and a threshold $w\sim E^{\alpha}$ gives $|A|\sim\Gamma(\alpha+1)t^{-(\alpha+1)}$ with phase $-\pi(\alpha+1)/2$, measured slopes $-0.99994,\,-1.49983,\,-1.99987$ and phases $-1.57030,\,-2.35631,\,-3.14059$ for $\alpha=0,\tfrac12,1$.

Levinson's theorem is the argument principle on a transform. The $S$-matrix is analytic above the real axis, its zeros there are the bound states, and the count is the winding of its phase: $\Delta\delta_0/\pi=-0.00000,\,-0.00008,\,-0.99987,\,-0.99981,\,-0.99968,\,-1.99933,\,-2.99838$ against $n=0,0,1,1,1,2,3$ for $V_0=0.5$ to $100$. It fails for the Coulomb potential where the transform is not integrable at $k=0$, which is the same failure Consequence 28 records.

The optical theorem is algebra plus Plancherel. $\sigma_{\mathrm{el}}=\int d\Omega|f|^{2}=\int d^{2}b|1-S|^{2}$ because the transform preserves the norm, and $2(1-\mathrm{Re}\,S)=|1-S|^{2}+(1-|S|^{2})$ for every $S$. On a $2048^{2}$ lattice the two spaces agree with ratio $1.0000000$ and the sum agrees with $(4\pi/k)\mathrm{Im}\,f(0)$ to $8\times10^{-15}$; what $|S|\le1$ adds is that each term is a count.

On a circle the transform is a geometric series, and statistics is the sign of its ratio. With $q=e^{-\beta E}$ the bosonic sum is $\frac{1}{2E}\frac{1+q}{1-q}=\frac{1}{2E}\coth\frac{\beta E}{2}$ and the fermionic one is $\frac{1-q}{1+q}=\tanh\frac{\beta E}{2}$; the image sum matches to $0.00\times10^{0}$ and the ratios are $0.2465969639$ four times running against $e^{-\beta E}$. $\coth$ and $\tanh$ are one sum with the images added or alternating.

The corpus's equations are already written in wave numbers, so the transform adds no vocabulary. It adds the one thing the notation cannot show by itself: which part of an equation is a number that can vanish, which part is an element that cannot, and where the solvability condition comes from.
