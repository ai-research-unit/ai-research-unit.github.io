The Gauge-Covariant Derivative: An Inventory by Type

Introduction

One operator is written in nine shapes in this corpus, across thirty-four articles. The shapes are not spellings of one array. They differ in what the connection is (a central scalar, an algebra element, a matrix, a link variable), in which side the field is multiplied from, and in whether the derivative is taken before or after the field is split into two chiral halves. Two articles on the same subject can be written in two different shapes, and a reader who takes the letter for the object will substitute one for the other and be wrong.

The object itself is fixed. The covariant derivative is the gradient with the connection added, and the reason it exists is stated in The Gauge Principle in Biquaternionic Form: requiring $D_2(\lambda\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}})=\lambda D\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}$ for a central phase forces $\tilde A_2=\tilde A-\tilde\nabla\Gamma$, and the connection is the price of that requirement. Everything else in this inventory is a variation on where the connection sits and on which side the multiplication acts.

The count is worth stating at the head, because the figures move. Thirty-seven statements in thirty-four articles. Three articles carry two shapes each: the gauge principle states the abelian derivative and previews the non-abelian one, and the gauge-curvature and Yang–Mills articles each write the non-abelian derivative and its adjoint form. The nine shapes are tabulated below, each with the article, a link, and the equation as the article prints it.

Two findings organise the record, and neither is a matter of taste.

The letter does not fix the object. $D_\mu$ names the covariant derivative in the gauge articles and the gauge propagator in The Stueckelberg Mechanism. $\mathcal D$ names the covariant derivative in the anomaly article and the functional measure in every path-integral article. §Where the Letter Is Not a Covariant Derivative lists the seven collisions with a line reference for each.

The side is carried by position, and it moves the object. In the abelian gauge articles the derivative multiplies the field from the left, $D\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}$. In the chiral articles the charge sits on the right, $D_\mu Q=\partial Q/\partial Q_\mu+\frac{i}{\hbar}A_\mu Qb$. In the spin-connection articles the connection multiplies from both sides, $D_\mu\tilde Q=\partial/\partial x_\mu\tilde Q+\vec\Gamma_\mu\tilde Q+\tilde Q\vec\Gamma_\mu^{*}$. The three are not related by a change of letter.

The Object and Its Layers

The definition. The covariant derivative is the sum of the gradient and the connection,

$$ \tilde D=\tilde\nabla+i\kappa\mathcal A,\qquad \kappa=\frac{ϙ}{\hbar}, $$

with $\tilde\nabla=\sum_\mu e_\mu\partial/\partial Q_\mu$ of Operator and Calculus Conventions, §The Gradient. The assembled operator carries the tilde because its $e_t$ coefficient is $\partial/\partial Q_t+i\kappa\mathcal A_t$, a scalar part; the component carries none.

The four writings, in one place. The Covariant Derivative and Gauge Connection in Biquaternionic Form prints the assembled operator, its expansion, its action, and its component side by side:

$$ D=\tilde{\nabla}+\frac{iϙ}{\hbar}\tilde{A}=\sum_{\mu\in\{t,x,y,z\}}e_\mu\left(\partial/\partial Q_\mu+\frac{iϙ}{\hbar}A_\mu\right)=:\sum_{\mu\in\{t,x,y,z\}}e_\mu D_\mu, $$

$$ D\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}=\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}+\frac{iϙ}{\hbar}\tilde{A}\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}},\qquad D_\mu\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}=\partial/\partial Q_\mu\,\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}+\frac{iϙ}{\hbar}A_\mu\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}. $$

Three properties, and each is a warrant for one of the shapes below. First, it is a derivation on central scalars, $D(f\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}})=fD\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}+(\tilde\nabla f)\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}$; the connection term passes through $f$ because $f$ is central. Second, its commutator with a function is the plain derivative, $[D_\mu,f]=\partial/\partial Q_\mu f$. Third, its commutator with itself closes on the algebra, $[D_\mu,D_\nu]=i\kappa F_{\mu\nu}$, and that is the only one of the three that produces an element rather than an operator.

The square, where the shape is the abelian one. With $\bar D=\sum_\mu\bar e_\mu D_\mu=\tilde\nabla^{\natural}+i\kappa\tilde A^{\natural}$ the quaternion conjugate of the operator, the identity

$$ \bar D D=\sum_{\mu\in\{t,x,y,z\}}D_\mu^{2}+\frac{iϙ}{\hbar}\tilde F $$

separates the covariant d'Alembertian from the curvature. The article states that it was checked on random connections and random fields, on constant and on non-constant potentials. The derivation is three lines: the diagonal of $\sum_{\mu,\nu}\bar e_\mu e_\nu D_\mu D_\nu$ gives $e_t\sum_\mu D_\mu^{2}$ because $\bar e_\mu e_\mu=e_t$, and each off-diagonal pair collapses onto the commutator, $\bar e_\mu e_\nu D_\mu D_\nu+\bar e_\nu e_\mu D_\nu D_\mu=\bar e_\mu e_\nu[D_\mu,D_\nu]$, because the anticommutator coefficient vanishes term by term. I did not re-verify the identity here; I take the article's statement. $\bar D$ is the quaternion conjugate of the operator, not the operator of the opposite charge; the article says so, because $\tilde A^{\natural}\neq-\tilde A$ in general.

The two conventions articles are the anchor, and they differ in scope. Algebra and Element Conventions gives the formula and its use on the curvature. Operator and Calculus Conventions only lists the glyph, and it does so to fix the tilde: the operator is read with its scalar part, and the component $D_\mu$ carries no tilde. An article that writes $\tilde D$ without a formula is therefore following the second, not defining anything.

The pair $\bar D$ belongs to this type and is not a second operator. It is the quaternion conjugate of $D$, and the chiral Dirac pair is written with it. The Minimal Coupling is the article that states the pair and checks its covariance; The Hydrogen Atom and The Electron use it without re-deriving it.

The gauge-principle article appears twice, and the second entry is a preview, not a result. It says so in its own words: the non-abelian case "belongs to the planned companion articles", and what it states is the commutator identity and the gap in the reality condition. One shape, two carriers. In these articles $F_{\mu\nu}$ is an algebra element rotated by the gauge group. Non-Abelian Gauge Fields makes the point its first claim: a component of the field strength is not a number, and every other non-abelian feature is downstream of that.

Type 3: The Adjoint Action

The derivative acts on an element that transforms in the adjoint, so the connection multiplies from both sides and the two multipliers are the connection and its conjugate.

The third line is the three-term form with the two frame multipliers, which is the shape of the next type; the article writes it as the bivector form of the same action.

The same article that introduces this shape states why it is not the abelian one. The chirality basis operator is not central, so the right multiplier does not fold into the connection, and the commutator closes on two multipliers rather than on one element.

Type 6: The Transcribed Standard-Model Derivative

One article writes the derivative of the Standard Model as it stands, with the gauge matrices as matrices and not as algebra elements. It is a different object and it is labelled as such.

article complete equation
The W and Z Bosons in Biquaternionic Form $D_\mu=\partial/\partial Q_\mu+ig\sum_a T_aW_{\mu a}+ig_{\mathrm{Y}}Y_\xi^{\mathrm{scl}} B_\mu$, entering through the kinetic term $\lvert D_\mu\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}\rvert^2$

Why it is not Type 2. The $T_a$ are the generators of the gauge group in the representation carried by $\xi^{\mathrm{scl}}$, and they are not elements of $\mathbb{B}$; $g$ and $g_{\mathrm Y}$ are two couplings, not one. The article uses the abelian shape $D_\mu=\partial/\partial Q_\mu+i\kappa A_\mu$ in its own biquaternionic computation and the transcribed shape only where the two couplings must be displayed separately.

Type 8: The Lattice Derivative

On a lattice there is no derivative, so the covariant derivative is a finite difference and the connection is a link variable.

article complete equation
Lattice Gauge Theory and the Biquaternion Path Integral $(D_\mu\xi)(n)\longmapsto\frac{1}{a}\Big(\tilde U_\mu(n)\xi(n+\vec u_\mu)-\xi(n)\Big)$
Fermion Doubling and the Nielsen–Ninomiya Theorem in Biquaternionic Form $D_\mu\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}_n=\frac{\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}_{n+\vec u_\mu}-\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}_{n-\vec u_\mu}}{2a}$, $\tilde\nabla^{L}=\sum_{\mu}e_\mu D_\mu$

The two articles discretise different things, and the difference is the subject of the pair. The lattice-gauge article keeps the link and drops the difference to first order, which is the gauge-covariant choice; the fermion-doubling article uses the centred difference, which carries no link variable and is therefore not gauge-covariant at nonzero coupling, and that is the situation the doubling theorem is about. A reader who takes $D_\mu$ for one thing across the two will miss that.

The three articles that carry two shapes are the ones to read twice. The Gauge Principle states the abelian derivative and previews the non-abelian one. What is not in the count, and why. Path-integral articles that write only $\mathcal D\tilde A$ as a measure, articles that use $D_\mu$ for a propagator, and articles that name the derivative without an equation are excluded. The last row is the one that matters most for a search. The glyph $\tilde D$ occurs in ten physics articles as the gauge-covariant derivative, in eight mathematics articles as the convective derivative, and in two more mathematics articles as a generic element. The tilde and the position are the same in all three. The mathematics articles use $\bar s_\mu$ and the unbarred $s$ for the multipliers, so the object is a directional derivative in the direction of an element, not a connection. Anything counted by glyph alone will add the two together.

The measure is the easiest collision to avoid, because the letter is calligraphic and the covariant derivative is not. The corpus writes $\mathcal{D}$ for the measure and $D$ or $\tilde D$ for the operator, and it keeps the two apart everywhere except in the Fujikawa display, where both occur in one equation and the context separates them.

Summary

The corpus writes one object in nine shapes and thirty-seven statements, in thirty-four articles. The abelian shape dominates with sixteen articles; the algebra-valued shape follows with eight, of which two also carry the adjoint form. The remaining shapes are few and each belongs to one task: the spin connection to gravity, the chiral charge to the electroweak sector, the transcribed form to the Standard Model comparison, the twisted form to the index theorem, the lattice form to the regularisation articles, and the operator-in-a-determinant form to the anomaly computation.

The shapes differ in three respects, and each is visible in the equation rather than in the letter. The connection is central or algebra-valued. The multiplication is one-sided or two-sided. The carrier is a continuous space or a lattice. The letter $D$ is the same in all of them, and so is the tilde on the assembled operator.

The collisions are seven, and one of them is systematic: in the mathematics articles $\tilde D$ is a convective derivative, so a glyph count over the two corpora is wrong by ten articles. The others are local: a propagator, a measure, a rotor, a central unit, a generic element, a dilation field.
