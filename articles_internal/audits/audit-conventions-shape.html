Audit of the Shape of the Conventions Articles

What this file is. A measurement of the twenty conventions articles — the ten of the mathematical corpus and the ten of the physical corpus — as objects of writing rather than as statements of convention. It measures size, growth, historical residue, physics inside the mathematics, duplication inside each set, and paragraph shape. Written 2026-11-24 on the working tree, then used as the worklist for six passes of repair.

Where it stands. The mathematical set is 53 248 words, from 61 982: 8 734 words out, 13.7 per cent, over nine passes. The physical Operator and Calculus is 11 254, from 11 658, and the physical set is 77 525, from 77 929: the same gunk cut the same way, in its own words, with no pointer to the mathematical copy, as the ruling below requires. The measurement below is the original one and is kept as the record; the pass notes at the end of each set carry the changes.

The ruling this file now follows. The two conventions sets are independent. There are no shared rules, no master article, and no cross-citation of a convention from one set to the other. Each set states its own conventions in full, in its own words, for its own reader. Every finding below is therefore read per set, and no repair may consist of replacing a statement by a pointer to the other corpus.

Scope. articles_maths/*-conventions.md, 10 files, 61 982 words, and articles_physics/*-conventions.md, 10 files, 77 929 words: 20 files, 7 548 lines, 139 911 words. The two menus maths.md and physics.md both declare ten conventions articles, and the ten declared are the ten measured; nothing is missing and nothing extra is included.

Headline. The twenty conventions articles hold 1.26% of the corpus's words, and their mean length is 2.1 times the corpus mean and 2.5 times the median. Eight of the twenty sit among the seventy longest articles of 3 312. The mathematical set spends 12.3% of its prose on physical apparatus that its own scope excludes. In both sets the file has grown by restatement and by change history rather than by accretion of new rules. On the ruling above, the duplication between the two sets is not a defect and is not repaired; it is recorded once, in Finding 2, because it fixes the price of the ruling.

The rest of this file is the measurement behind that sentence, sorted by cause, then the strategy.

Finding 1 — The Conventions Are the Longest Prose in the Corpus

A convention article is read once and consulted often. The corpus has written it as the longest thing it has.

Nine of the twenty exceed the ninetieth percentile of the corpus. Three enter the top twenty-five: Physics Units and Common Elements Conventions is the seventh longest article of 3 312, the physical Matrix Representations Conventions the fourteenth, the physical Operator and Calculus Conventions the twenty-fourth. Their mathematical counterparts rank 52 (Index and Notation), 59 (Matrix Representations) and 62 (Operator and Calculus).

The growth is concentrated in the middle of the file. Physics Units and Common Elements Conventions has six ## sections and 15 582 words: 2 600 words per section. Index and Notation Conventions in mathematics has 179 table rows in one article, more than any other file measured here.

Length alone does not prove bloat; a convention can be long and dense. The remaining findings are what the length is made of.

Finding 2 — Duplication inside Each Set

The two sets are independent by ruling, so their agreement is not a defect. It is recorded once here, with one number, and then dropped: the audit does not ask for reconciliation and no repair below touches it.

Recorded and closed: 157 paragraphs of more than 120 characters are identical in two or more of the twenty files, and 154 of them are shared by the mathematical and the physical article of one pair. Frame and Metric Conventions is the extreme case, with 39 of its 43 mathematical paragraphs recurring in the physical article under the same ten headings. Under the ruling this coincidence is the expected result of two sets that name the same objects, and it is left alone.

What remains a defect is duplication inside one set, because independence gives each set one statement per rule and no second article to defer to.

One paragraph is copied five times in the mathematical set:

No contravariant index appears in this article, and none appears anywhere in the series. The one metric is the unit metric $\delta_{\mu\nu}=(1,1,1,1)$; a metric that is the identity raises nothing, so a raised index would name the same index twice.

It appears in Algebra and Element, Product and Form, Norm and Unitary, Special Element and Peirce and Operator Family Conventions, which is five statements of a rule that Index and Notation Conventions owns. The physical set has the same five copies, in its own five articles.

Each set therefore has one rule stated six times, and the six statements must agree by hand. That is the internal cost of independence, and it is the only duplication the strategy below repairs.

Finding 3 — Change History Written into the Rule

A convention states a rule. A change log states what a rule used to be. The corpus has put the second into the first, in four places, and the first of them is a whole section.

1. The legacy sections. Three articles carry a section titled The Legacy Pauli Gamma Formulation:

article lines words
Matrix Representations Conventions (maths) 751–844 702
Matrix Representations Conventions (physics) 842–965 1 364
Operator and Calculus Conventions (physics) 577–602 347

Each opens by declaring its own subject obsolete — "The Pauli and gamma formulation is legacy" — and then prints the five gamma arrays in three frames, the sixteen products, the comparison map, the contractions and the trace identities. The mathematical version declares that gamma matrices are not used, and then writes them in 228 display blocks, a quarter of the article's lines.

The constraint on repairing this is severe and must be stated before any suggestion: 91 files cite §The Legacy Pauli Gamma Formulation by name, 26 in the mathematical corpus and 65 in the physical one. The section is the designated house of the arrays, and both corpora have been told they can send readers there. It cannot be deleted. It can be shortened in place, in each set separately: the ruling forbids moving the arrays into one article that both sets cite.

2. The migration of a rule not yet done. Reserved Letters Conventions (maths), line 9:

The corpus is being brought to the rule, so a spectral article that writes $\lambda$ for the eigenvalue, roughly $21\,000$ occurrences against $1\,500$ of $\eta$, is read here as the wavelength, and the older usage is named rather than hidden.

That is a progress report on an editorial migration, with a count of the files still to be fixed. It will be false as soon as the migration ends, and it is not a convention.

3. A retirement table. Index and Notation Conventions (physics), line 108:

What the letter η used to carry. The letter η is retired from every scalar sense and kept for the spectrum.

followed by a four-row table of replacements. Three of the four rows state a current rule; the heading is the only historical thing in them.

4. A rule against a word. Frame and Metric Conventions (maths and physics) carried, as rule 9 of nine, "The word signature is not used, and the metric has no signature to carry."

A rule that exists because a word was once used is a change-log entry. The content behind it — the inertia $(n,m,r)$ and the excess $\sigma=n-m$ — is a convention and survives; the prohibition is history.

Ruled by the author, 2026-11-24. The rule stands as written, in both sets. It is a guard, not a change-log entry, and it is not to be rewritten.

What that ruling does to the sentence. Operator and Calculus (maths), §The Gradient, used the banned word: "that is the $(-,+,+,+)$ signature on the material parameters, the signature the series works in throughout". It is a pre-existing contradiction inside the set, not a product of these passes. It is now "and the four coefficients on the material parameters are $(-,+,+,+)$, the tuple the series works in throughout". The mathematical set no longer contains the word at all.

The physical twin still does. Operator and Calculus (physics), §The Gradient, line 151 of the original, carries the same sentence, and Frame and Metric (physics) rule 9 bans the same word in the same set. That contradiction is untouched here and needs one decision: the physics sentence is cut the same way, or the physics rule is narrowed to the metric alone.

Smaller traces of the same kind, each removable without loss of a rule:

  • Matrix Representations Conventions (maths), line 11: "The order renames nothing. The corpus calls the Peirce frame the canonical frame, and this article keeps the name". A reader who never knew another name does not need the reassurance.
  • Index and Notation Conventions (maths), line 134: "the norm the earlier notation called the Frobenius or the Hilbert–Schmidt norm … and no longer as a bar". Two names that "survive as names of the same object and never as symbols" are names the reader will never meet.
  • Matrix Representations Conventions, §The Rule on Pauli and Gamma Matrices: "A reference to a legacy section stays, and so does a historical remark inside one." That is a rule permitting history, written in a conventions article.

One near miss, worth recording. $\mathrm{orb}$ looked used: $\pi_{\mathrm{orb},1}$ appears at The Classification of Involutions on Surfaces. It is the orbifold fundamental group there, not the orbital generators the row described, and the orbifold already has its own row, $\mathrm{obf}$. The row was deleted for the sense it stated, not for the label. $\mathrm{sp}$ nearly went the same way twice: $\mathrm{sp}(n,\mathbb{R})$ at Hermitian Lie Groups is the symplectic algebra and $\lVert f\rVert_{\mathrm{sp}}$ at Rigid Analytic Functions is the spectral norm, both documented elsewhere in the same table. Only the third occurrence, the spinor field, is the sense the row carries, and one genuine use is enough. - Operator and Calculus Conventions (maths) states the Dirac pair with a mass $m$, the Maxwell equation $\tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde F=-\sqrt{\mu}\,\tilde J$ with $\tilde J=ic\rho\,e_t+\vec J$, the field strength and the Poynting flux. It spends 1 853 words in four subsections — The Plain Bilinear Product, Its Form and Its Norm and its three companions — restating the products and the norms of two other articles. - Special Element and Peirce Conventions (maths) uses gauge eleven times, builds the Peirce example "With a star, $\lvert t_i\rvert=1$ and the gauge is a torus of phases", and carries the Dirac spinor. - Reserved Letters Conventions (maths) illustrates $\nu$ and $\lambda$ with "a particle has three of them", $\varrho$ with "the charge density", and the fallback $\digamma$ with a coupling. - Frame and Metric Conventions (maths), lines 5 and 139, define the subject with a physics word: "a frame means a spacetime frame". The object defined is a choice of coordinates on the biquaternion space; the word adds a subject the corpus excludes.

Two inversions, and they are now the sharpest defect in the file. The mathematics does not merely borrow physical vocabulary. In two places it defers to the physical corpus for its own rule, which independence forbids outright.

  • Index and Notation Conventions (maths), §The Subscripts: "the rule is fixed in Operator and Calculus Conventions, §Conventions for Derivation." This one was read wrong and is withdrawn. The mathematical Operator and Calculus Conventions has a §Conventions for Derivation of its own, so the reference is inside the mathematical set and correct. A citation between two articles of the same set is not a deferral to the other corpus.
  • Matrix Representations Conventions (maths), §Further Reading: a link to articles_physics/... "for the reasons the AXF matrices replace the Pauli and gamma matrices". Gone from the conventions article. The link survives in twenty-six other mathematical articles, which are outside the ten files of this audit; it is a corpus habit, not a conventions-set defect, and it needs one decision for the corpus rather than ten edits here.

The count was one true and one false. No link from the mathematical conventions set into the physical corpus remains.

What is not leakage. The shared alphabet is not the problem. A scale $\varrho$, a wave number $\nu$, an amplitude, a phase, an eigenvalue, a spinor and a spin are mathematical objects that physics also uses. The test that separates the two is this: does the sentence need the physical world to be true, or only the algebra? "A particle has three wave numbers" needs the physical world; "an element has three wave numbers, $\nu_m$, $\nu_\Phi$ and $\nu_t'$" does not. The first is leakage, the second is a convention.

Finding 8 — Content Held in the Wrong Article

Six pieces of text sit in the wrong one of the ten articles. Each is a move, not a cut, so the ruling on independence is untouched: no piece crosses between the two sets.

1. The marks that are not derivatives. Operator and Calculus Conventions, §Conventions for Derivation, lines 149–155, holds four rules that name a mark and no derivative: the prime marks the imaginary coordinate; the bar on an index marks the conjugate spinor; the underline is the scope mark of geometric calculus; $\partial_{\mathrm{nil}}$ is a construction written in the caret. Index and Notation Conventions is the article of the marks, and it states none of the four. Move the four there, and leave in §Conventions for Derivation the rules that are about differentiation: no dot, the fraction, the binding to the whole argument.

2. The prime, stated three times and never where it belongs. It is stated at Algebra and Element Conventions line 200 for the primed element, at Operator and Calculus Conventions line 149 for the imaginary coordinate, and again in that article's Summary at line 397. It is stated zero times in Index and Notation. One statement, in the marks article, is the whole rule; the two other sites become uses of it.

3. $h_{\mathrm{def}}$ contradicts Reserved Letters Conventions. Index and Notation line 396 fixes the deformation parameter as $h_{\mathrm{def}}$. Reserved Letters fixes eight glyphs and says that a scalar which is none of the eight takes $\digamma$ with a subscript naming its sense. $h_{\mathrm{def}}$ is a ninth glyph that takes no $\digamma$, so one of the two rules is wrong and the two articles never meet. Either the glyph is added to the eight, or it is written $\digamma_{\mathrm{def}}$. This is a contradiction inside the mathematical set, and it is the only one found.

Closed, 2026-11-24. Neither option offered here was taken, because both were wrong about the rule. The eight are the letters that stand alone; $h$ is never written bare, and it already carries two senses told apart by subscript, $h_{\mathrm{def}}$ and $h_{\mathrm{tpl}}$. Reserved Letters now says so in one paragraph. $h_{\mathrm{def}}$ is not a ninth glyph and does not become $\digamma_{\mathrm{def}}$; the contradiction was in the audit's reading of "spent once", which the corpus applies to undecorated letters.

4. The Planck rule was a cross-set duplicate, and the check settles it. Physics Units and Common Elements Conventions holds the Planck constant rule, and so did the mathematical Index and Notation. The copy in the mathematical set is removed in pass four. The independence ruling did not require the removal — a duplicate would have been tolerable — but the exclusion of physics from the mathematical set did.

5. The gamma arrays: premise checked, and it fails. This item was written on the assumption that Frame and Metric Conventions displays the four arrays. It displays none: begin{pmatrix} returns 0 hits in that file. It holds $\mathsf{K}(e_\mu)=\gamma_\mu$ and the anticommutator, which is the map, and the arrays are held once, in Matrix Representations. There is no duplication, and no move is needed. What the check did find in the same section is a sentence of change history — "Earlier versions called the five images the gamma frame" — now cut to the naming rule.

6. Two of the eight rules of Frame and Metric carried a duplicate tail, checked. The claim that three rules are "not frame rules" was too strong, and reading them settled it. Rule 5 (the $i$ is in the coordinate or in the signs, never in the metric) is one sentence and is the article's thesis. Rules 2 and 8 each ended with a sentence already stated twice elsewhere in the same article: rule 2 restated the one metric and the frame-free matrices, and rule 8 restated that the weight is not the negative of a metric, which is the paragraph at line 132. Those two tails are gone. All eight rules stand.

What the moves cost, and the mistake in them. The moves were run as a move-and-keep-a-pointer, and that shape grows the set. Pass five added 32 words, the only pass that did, and the shape was wrong: a canonical statement plus a pointer in the old home is two copies where there was one, and the point of the exercise is fewer words. The pointers are gone, the canonical statements are compressed, and the moves now pay for themselves. Rule for the rest of this audit: a move must be a move, not a copy. The old site loses the rule and gains nothing, or the rule does not move.

What must not move. The two first-order equations stay in Operator and Calculus Conventions, because the symbol rule of that article is read off them. The gamma arrays stay in Matrix Representations, because the rule that the gamma index is low and the frame is bracketed is checked against them. The four product sections stay in Operator and Calculus Conventions, because no other article reads a product on the gradient.

Residual physics, for a decision rather than a move. Operator and Calculus Conventions §The Two First-Order Equations and Their Symbol still speaks of a field of mass $m$, of chiral parts, of the Klein–Gordon equation, of a plane wave, of a momentum and of a Green's function. The Maxwell equation and the Poynting flux are gone; this vocabulary remains. Two readings are possible. Either the section is algebraic — two linear equations in $\Psi_R$ and $\Psi_L$ with a real central parameter $m$ — and the words change and nothing moves, or the section is physical and belongs to the other set, which the independence ruling forbids. The first reading is the one consistent with the ruling, and it is a wording job, about 40 words.

What Is Not Bloat

Four things must be named as innocent, or the remedy will remove them.

The reserved-letters articles. They are summaries by design, they say so in the introduction, and at 2 765 and 3 757 words they are near the corpus median. Their repetition of the eight and the eleven glyphs is the point of the article.

The tables that are the only statement of a rule. The twelve products, the six layer tables of the norms, the Peirce multiplication rule and the four adjoints exist nowhere else in the same form. A table of 60 rows carrying 60 distinct rules is not bloat. The 115-label index is different: it is an inventory, and an inventory can live in one place and be referenced.

The cross-references inside a set. An article that cites another article of its own set instead of restating it does what the corpus asks, and independence leaves that route open. What independence closes is the other direction, a citation from one set to the other. The problem is the reverse of both: 91 files cite one legacy section by name, so the three legacy headings stay wherever else their contents go.

The duplication between the two sets. It is the consequence of the ruling, not a fault. Two sets that name the same objects with the same alphabet will agree in about 154 paragraphs of 157, and the agreement is left as it is.

The Suggested Strategy

The ruling removes the largest saving the first version of this audit proposed. Under independence the physical set keeps its own full statement, so no article of it can be shortened by pointing at a mathematical one. The reduction falls from about 70 000 words to about 20 000, and the corpus keeps roughly 120 000 words of conventions. That is the price of the ruling, and it is paid deliberately.

What remains is a list of authored edits inside each set. No scripted pass is proposed. Every item is a decision about a paragraph, taken in review mode, with the author's permission for each one, and reported as it is made.

The mathematical set: 61 982 words

Ordered by words removed per decision.

  1. Operator and Calculus Conventions, 9 363 words. The worst offender, and the clearest breach of the exclusion. Premise checked 2026-11-24, and the first plan for this article was wrong.

The four subsections at lines 252–416, 1 853 words, do two things at once, and only one of them is duplication:

  • They restate the general definitions of the four forms and their norms, which Product and Form Conventions §The Form Convention states in full, as $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2)$ and its three companions. That part is duplication and goes.
  • They read the four products on the gradient and record the result: divergence, gradient and curl in one object; the plain norm $-\partial^2/\partial {r'_t}^2-\Delta_\Phi$; the quaternionic norm $\Box_\Phi$; the Hermitian norm also $\Box_\Phi$; the Krein norm equal to the plain one, indefinite, of inertia $(1,3)$. No other article owns this, and the §Summary of the article depends on it. The plan to delete the block would have deleted a rule.

So the edit is a compression, not a deletion: one section, the four readings kept, the general definitions dropped, the physics dropped.

  • Keep, per product, the one line that says what the product gives on the gradient, and the operator each norm produces. Four rows become one table.
