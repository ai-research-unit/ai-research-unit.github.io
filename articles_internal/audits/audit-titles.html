Audit of Titles and File Names

What this file is. A measurement of the agreement between the title of each article and the name of its file, over both published collections. Written 2026-11-23 on the working tree. The rule the repair follows: when a title and a file name disagree, the title wins and the file is renamed (§The rule: the file name follows the title). It lists what differs, sorts the differences by cause, separates the tolerated spellings from the defects, and closes with the options. §The Repair records the repair that followed, so the measurement above §The Repair is the state before it.

The rule it measures. The corpus states the rule twice, in Index and Notation Conventions:

a mathematical article is a file articles_maths/<slug>.md and a physical article is a file articles_physics/<slug>.md, where the slug is the title in lower case with hyphens in place of spaces and punctuation.

and again in the Summary of Notation of the same article:

articles_maths/<slug>.md — the file of a mathematical article; the slug is the title in lower case with hyphens.

There is no second rule. Nothing prescribes how mathematics inside a title is written into a slug, nothing says whether a subtitle after a colon belongs to the slug, and nothing says what to do when two articles are the same subject at two levels of generality.

Scope. articles_maths/*.md, 2 633 files, and articles_physics/*.md, 679 files: 3 312 articles. Every one of the 3 312 opens with its title on the first line, in the form # __Title__. No file was skipped. One of the 3 312 was an orphan and has since been deleted (§What the repair left alone), so the tree holds 3 311 — 2 632 mathematical and 679 physical.

Headline. 3 143 file names are their title letter for letter. 168 are a tolerated spelling of their title. 0 contradict their title. That is the whole finding, and the rest of this file is the working.

The Measure

The title is the text between the double underscores. The file name is the stem of the file. The two are compared as bags of content words.

A word counts when it is not a stop word (the, a, of, and, in, at, to, for, …). Accents are folded, so Möbius and mobius are one word. A plural and a possessive are the same word as their stem, so bispinors and bispinor agree, and Chaitin's and chaitins agree. A word inside $…$ in the title is credited to the slug when the slug contains its letters: the title $M_4(\mathbb{C})$ credits m4c. A slug word inside the concatenation of the whole slug is credited too, so 2x2 and 2×2 agree.

The strict reading of the rule — the slug is the title transliterated — cannot be applied by machine, because 496 titles carry mathematics and the corpus uses no fixed transliteration. It writes $M_4(\mathbb{C})$ as m4c, $\mathbb{R}$ as r, $\tfrac32$ as three-halves, $\operatorname{Sym}$ as sym, $2\times2$ as 2x2, C^* as c-star, and $\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}$ as c-over-r. A strict count would measure the transliteration table of the checker, not the corpus. The content-word count measures the corpus.

Two things the measure cannot see. It cannot see a subtitle that repeats the main clause in different words, and it cannot see whether the title or the file name is the one that is out of date. Where that question matters, this audit answers it from the body of the article, and says so.

What the Corpus Tolerates, and What It Does Not

The rule of the corpus is one sentence: the slug is the title in lower case, with hyphens. The corpus has never applied it literally, because a title is prose and a slug is an address. Three deviations are therefore habitual and are treated here as compliant:

  1. The subtitle after a colon is not carried into the slug, nor a trailing clause. 308 titles carry a colon. Of the 95 names of kind A, 72 stop at the colon and 23 drop a closing clause such as including the Lie groups.
  2. A formula is written as its letters. $M_4(\mathbb{C})$ → m4c, $\mathbb{R}$ → r, $C^*$ → c-star, $\operatorname{Sym}$ → sym.
  3. A formula may instead be written as its words. $\oplus$ → plus, $\otimes$ → tensor, $\tfrac32$ → three-halves. This is rarer, and it is what makes 30 rows look like a paraphrase when they are a spelling.

Two other deviations are not tolerated, because each makes the name say something the title does not:

  • a qualifier in the name that the title lacks — the-positive-cone-and-its-boundary-over-r against The Positive Cone and Its Boundary. In the first run there were 48 such rows.
  • a name that says more, or other, than the title — the-gluon-an-octet-outside-the-biquaternion-algebra against The Gluon: An Octet Inside the Carrier's Group. In the first run there were 31 such rows.

Those 79 rows, and the 26 title collisions behind them, were the defect this audit was made to find. They are repaired or renamed; none survives. The first run as a whole, for the record:

The rule: the file name follows the title

This is the rule that settles every disagreement below, and the one to reuse in the next audit of this corpus.

When a title and a file name disagree, the title is the authority. The file is renamed.

The title is what a reader sees and what the menu repeats. The file name is an address derived from it. A stale name is a stale address, and an address is cheap to change: the old one is kept alive by LEGACY_COPIES. A stale title, by contrast, is visible on every page and in every menu.

So the sequence is: fix the title first, then make the name follow it.

situation does the file get renamed?
the title was wrong and the file name was right no — the name already matches
the title was fixed, and the name no longer matches it yes
two files of one collection carry one title one is retitled, then renamed if its old name described the old title
the name says something the title does not say yes, unless the extra words are a formula in letters or a plural
the title carries a subtitle the name omits no — the corpus drops the subtitle from the name; this is its habit, 72 rows
the name spells a formula in letters ($C^*$ → c-star, $SO(4)$ → so4) no

The last two are the only tolerated departures. They are not disagreements of meaning, only of spelling. Everything else is a defect and gets a rename.

The procedure

Five steps, in this order. The order matters: references are rewritten before the move, so a half-finished run leaves no broken link.

  1. Rename the source. mv articles_maths/<old>.md articles_maths/<new>.md. Never rename the built .html; the build owns it. Do not commit: the author keeps the version control.
  2. Rewrite every reference, before or after the move, over articles_*/*.md plus maths.md and physics.md. The menu holds hrefs, and the internal articles hold cross-links.
  3. Keep the old address alive. Append one pair to LEGACY_COPIES in build.py, live page first, retired address second:

python ("articles_maths/<new>.html", # the page the build produces "articles_maths/<old>.html"), # the address that must keep working

The build reads the first, copies its bytes to the second, and skips the pair silently when the first does not exist. The order is the trap. Written the other way round, the build finds no live page at the retired address and skips the pair without a word: the rename looks done, the old links break, and nothing in the log says so. Verify after the build with grep "^Alias written" build.log, one line per new pair. 4. Rebuild. .venv/bin/python build.py. It prints Removed orphan for a page whose source is gone, which is the signal that a deletion took effect. 5. Verify, and compare with the count before the run. Three checks, and the third is the one that catches a mistake: - the new page exists in the deploy tree; - the old address exists and returns the same bytes as the new page; - the count of broken internal links did not rise.

The third check is what proves the rename was complete. A rename without the reference sweep leaves exactly as many new broken links as there were references to the old name.

Option E — write the tolerated departures into the rule. Open. This is the open half of the work, and the one that decides what the next audit reports as a defect. One paragraph in Index and Notation Conventions, after the sentence on the slug, stating three things: (i) a formula in a title is written in the slug as its letters, without the commands — $M_4(\mathbb{C})$ gives m4c; (ii) a subtitle after a colon is not carried into the slug; (iii) the level of generality is carried by the file name, in the forms over-r, at-n-4, in-m2c, and the title states it in words. Written this way, all 168 rows still reported become compliant by rule, and nothing remains a defect until a title changes again. This is the cheapest option and the one that changes nothing a reader sees. Its risk is that it legalises a title which does not describe its article, which the writing rules forbid; the paragraph must therefore not license (iii) without a title that states the level of generality in words, which Option B has now given each of the 26 collisions.

Option F — normalise the corpus completely. Refused. Rename the 168 rows to the strict slug of their title, or retitle them to the strict reading of their slug. Either way 168 URL changes, 168 new LEGACY_COPIES, a rebuild of every sitemap and of the 3 318 menu lines, and a permanent redirect list of 217 entries. It is a week of mechanical work, and it would contradict §What the Corpus Tolerates: the subtitle and the formula in letters are the corpus's idiom, and 95 of the 168 rows drop a subtitle while 63 spell a formula in letters. A rename owed on a defect is a repair; a rename owed on a habit is a rewrite of the corpus.

What remains. Option E, one paragraph in Index and Notation Conventions recording the tolerated departures, and Option G, so that the next edit of a title is followed by its rename without a new audit.
