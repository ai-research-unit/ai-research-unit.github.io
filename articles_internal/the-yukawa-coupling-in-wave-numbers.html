The Yukawa Coupling in Wave Numbers: One Quotient of Two Wave Numbers, One Mass Ratio, and the Square Root That Is Not a Mass

Status of this note. Every claim carries a tag. [MEASURED] is a number from experiment. [ALGEBRA] is exact arithmetic on stated inputs. [PUBLISHED] is a claim of a cited paper. [ANSATZ] is an assumption made here. [OPEN] is a question this note does not settle.

Ledger on values. Every Yukawa value below is the literature's, read from the table of The Yukawa Sector and from the Particle Data Group. The corpus makes one scale-bearing coupling statement, and it is in The One Coupling That Returns the Standard Model. Nothing here is a second one. This note changes the variable in which the couplings are written; it asserts no strength of its own.

Scope. An internal working note. It is not a menu entry, it states no result of the model, and it changes no published article. It is the working record behind the search for a dimensionless form of Koide's relation, kept beside Flavour Research.

Introduction

The claim. The Yukawa coupling is the one number of the flavour sector that carries no dimension, and there is exactly one way of writing it in which its dimensionlessness is visible on the page: as the quotient of two wave numbers. This note fixes that writing, names the three dimensionless objects it produces, and says where the square root that Koide's relation requires actually comes from. It is a square root of a ratio and of nothing else.

What is being repaired. Koide's relation is printed as a ratio between a sum of masses and the square of a sum of square roots of masses. The numerator carries a unit of mass; the denominator carries the square of the square root of a unit of mass; and the ratio of the two is a pure number only because the unit cancels. That cancellation is correct but invisible, and a reader who asks what one square root of a mass is gets no answer from the printed formula. The answer is that a square root of a mass is nothing on its own. It becomes something when it is divided by another square root of a mass, and that quotient is what the formula is really built from.

What this note does, in four steps. It writes the mass as a wave number and the field as a mode sum, once, and then uses nothing else: the waveform's coefficient becomes a fixed normalisation on the page and not a free symbol. It shows that the Yukawa coupling is a quotient of two such wave numbers, hence dimensionless and scale-bearing at the same time. It separates the three square roots in play and shows that the ladder's root is a root of a mass ratio, while the mode coefficient of the quantised field is a normalisation and not a root of a ratio at all. And it writes Koide's relation, the ladder and the phase in that variable, so that no square root of a dimensioned quantity is taken anywhere.

What this note does not do. It derives nothing. Every number below is measured, and the three questions the writing exposes — why the spread of the roots is one, what the phase is, and whether the relation survives being read at one wave number — are left open, in §What This Note Leaves Open.

1. The Conversion and the Waveform, Made Once and Used Thereafter

The definition. The mass of a particle is read in this note as a wave number,

$$ \nu_{m} := \frac{m\,c}{h}, \qquad [\nu_{m}]=\mathsf{L}^{-1}, $$

the wave number of the corpus, the count of waves per unit length, of unit $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$, that is, the inverse Compton wavelength. The subscript names the input, which is one species; the species is written inside the subscript, $\nu_{m_e}$, $\nu_{m_\mu}$, $\nu_{m_\tau}$, and a wave number is never labelled by the name of a particle alone. The glyph and its readings are those of The Three Wavenumbers of a Particle, and the letter is not an index: $\nu$ carries wave numbers only.

Two quantities, two definitions, and the label of each. The wavelength is the inversion of the wave number, and the inversion carries no $2\pi$:

$$ \lambda_{m}:=\frac{1}{\nu_{m}}=\frac{h}{m\,c}, $$

so $\lambda_m$ is the wavelength in metres and $\nu_m$ is the wave number in $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$, the quantity a spectrometer quotes. The label tells the reader which is meant: $\nu_m$ is a count of waves, $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$; $\lambda_m$ is a length, $\mathrm{m}$.

The numbers, obtained once. With the measured masses $0.51099895$, $105.6583755$ and $1776.86\ \mathrm{MeV}$ and the conversion $h\,c=1239.84198\ \mathrm{MeV}\cdot\mathrm{fm}$,

$e$ $4.1215\times10^{11}$ $2.4263\ \mathrm{pm}$
$\mu$ $8.5219\times10^{13}$ $11.734\ \mathrm{fm}$
$\tau$ $1.4331\times10^{15}$ $0.69777\ \mathrm{fm}$

The angular form is this number times $2\pi$. The older corpus writing multiplied $\nu_m$ by $2\pi$ and called the result the angular wave number, the number of radians of phase per unit length, of unit $\mathrm{rad\,m^{-1}}$. It is a rescaling of $\nu_m$ and carries no extra information: every ratio is the same in either, and every quantity linear in one is $2\pi$ times the other. The other length is not a wavelength and needs no symbol of its own: it is one radian of the phase, $\frac{1}{2\pi\nu_m}=\frac{h}{2\pi mc}$, which is $386.159\ \mathrm{fm}$ for the electron against $2.426\ \mathrm{pm}$ for $\lambda_m$ itself. The corpus writes it as the quotient it is. No shorter length is given a letter, no barred letter is used, and the $2\pi$ stands where the radian stands; §The Letter and the Subscript of the physics article keeps the two apart, and this note writes $\frac{\lambda_m}{2\pi}$ for it throughout.

What the choice of variable costs. [ALGEBRA] Koide and the ladder are ratios of wave numbers, so they are unchanged by the choice, and $Q$ computed from $\nu_m$ is $0.666660511$. Nothing below depends on which of the two scalings is used, and only $\nu_m$ is used.

The electroweak scale, by the same rule. The vacuum expectation value is a mass, so it becomes a wave number by the same quotient, with $v=246.22\ \mathrm{GeV}$:

$$ \nu_{v} := \frac{v\,c}{h}=1.9859\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}} . $$

This one number is the whole of the electroweak scale in what follows. Every Yukawa statement below is a ratio to it, and it never appears in any other form.

Why a wave number and not a mass. Three reasons, and the third is the one that matters here. A wave number is the coefficient the equations already carry: the gradient multiplies by $2\pi i\tilde\Upsilon_{\Phi}$, the mass term of the operator is $2\pi\nu_m$, and a derivative returns an inverse length. A wave number is what the algebra's norm gives: the biquaternion norm of the momentum is $-\langle\tilde\Upsilon_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural}=\nu_{m}^{2}$, so $\nu_m$ is already the square root of an invariant of the algebra. And a ratio of two wave numbers is a pure number with no unit at all, which is the only kind of number a theory can predict.

The field the coefficient belongs to. The coefficient of a waveform is fixed by the field's quantisation, so the waveform is written once here. The corpus's scalar field is central-valued, $\tilde\Xi=\xi\,e_t$, and it obeys the Klein–Gordon equation in wave numbers,

$$ \left(\Box_\Phi-4\pi^{2}\nu_{m}^{2}\right)\tilde\Xi=0 . $$

A plane wave $\tilde\Xi=\xi_0\exp(-i\theta_{\mathrm{pwp}})\,e_t$ solves it when its phase and its wave numbers satisfy

$$ \theta_{\mathrm{pwp}}=2\pi\left(\nu_t^{\prime}r_t^{\prime}-\vec\nu_\Phi\cdot\vec r\right), \qquad \nu_t^{\prime\,2}=\nu_\Phi^{2}+\nu_m^{2}, $$

which is the triangle of The Three Wavenumbers of a Particle. The mass wave number is the rest-frame value of the energy wave number and nothing else.

The mode coefficient. The quantised field is the mode sum

$$ \xi_{\mathrm{fop}}(x)=\int\!\frac{d^{3}p}{h^{3}}\,\sqrt{\frac{h\,c}{4\pi E_{\vec p}}} \left(a_{\vec p}\exp(-i\theta_{\mathrm{pwp}})+b_{\vec p}^{\dagger}\exp(+i\theta_{\mathrm{pwp}})\right), $$

and the square root is the mode coefficient. It is not a free choice. The measure $d^{3}p/h^{3}$ carries $\mathsf{L}^{-3}$ and the commutator $[a_{\vec p},a_{\vec p^{\prime}}^{\dagger}]=h^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\vec p-\vec p^{\,\prime})$ makes the ladder operator carry $\mathsf{L}^{3/2}$, so the field carries $\mathsf{L}^{-1}$ only if the coefficient supplies the remaining $\mathsf{L}^{1/2}$; and it does, since $[h\,c/E]=\mathsf{L}$ and $-\tfrac32+\tfrac12=-1$. One quantum of one wave number is the cosine

$$ A_i\cos\left(2\pi\nu_{m_i}r_t^{\prime}\right), \qquad A_i=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\nu_{m_i}}}, $$

so at rest the coefficient is the exact inverse square root of the mass wave number, with no constant left over. Off rest the same rule gives $A=(4\pi\nu_t^{\prime})^{-1/2}$: the coefficient is fixed by the energy wave number, and $\nu_{m_i}$ replaces it exactly in the rest frame. $[A]=\mathsf{L}^{1/2}$, and only a quotient of two such coefficients is a pure number. This is the last place in the note where the two constants appear; the coefficient is then used in this form only, in §Three Roots and No Amplitude.

The declaration. From this point to the end of the note, the symbols for the mass, for the energy, for the action and for the speed of light do not appear. Every dimensioned quantity is a wave number of dimension $\mathsf{L}^{-1}$ or a root of one, and every dimensionless quantity is a quotient of two of them. The only letters that survive are the symbols of the units in which the wave numbers are quoted, the metre and the second; a unit symbol is not a quantity and carries no physics. The one warning. $\nu_{m}$ is a wave number, of dimension $\mathsf{L}^{-1}$; it is not dimensionless. Writing it in $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ and calling it a pure number would be the error this note exists to avoid. The dimensionless objects are ratios of it, and they are the subject of §The Three Dimensionless Objects.

2. The Coupling Is a Quotient

The matrix in wave numbers. The Yukawa sector writes a mass matrix as a dimensionless matrix times the electroweak scale. Read in wave numbers the same line is exact and carries a dimension on each side:

$$ K_{f}=\frac{Y_{f}\,\nu_{v}}{\sqrt2}, \qquad [Y_{f}]=1, \qquad [K_{f}]=\mathsf{L}^{-1}, $$

with $Y_f$ the complex $3\times3$ Yukawa matrix of the type $f$. The matrix of masses is a matrix of wave numbers, and the only thing the coupling supplies is a pure number per entry.

The diagonal reading. The measured spectrum diagonalises $K_f$, so each rung gives one dimensionless entry:

$$ y_{i}=\frac{\sqrt2\,\nu_{m_i}}{\nu_{v}}, \qquad [y_{i}]=1, \qquad K_{f}^{\mathrm{diag}}=\frac{\nu_{v}}{\sqrt2}\,\mathrm{diag}\left(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}\right), $$

This is the object of the note. $y_i$ is a quotient of two wave numbers, $\nu_{m_i}$ and $\nu_v$, both of dimension $\mathsf{L}^{-1}$; the quotient has no dimension, and it is the number the Lagrangian carries. Nothing is approximated and nothing is set to one.

The entry and the vertex are not the same number. The coupling the matrix carries is $y_i$; the vertex at which the scalar fluctuation $\xi$ attaches to the fermion bilinear is $y_i/\sqrt2$, and it is a quotient of two wave numbers on its own:

$$ \frac{\nu_{m_i}}{\nu_v}=\frac{y_i}{\sqrt2} . $$

The matrix is $K_f=Y_f\nu_v/\sqrt2$, so the factor $\sqrt2$ is the doublet normalisation and not a second coupling. Four numbers are therefore one number: the mass matrix entry, the doublet coupling $y_i$, the physical vertex, and the quotient of two wave numbers. The note writes $y_i$ because it is the entry the matrix carries before the breaking.

Why the quotient is the natural object and the mass is not. A mass needs a unit, and a unit is a choice. The quotient of two wave numbers needs no unit at all. Whatever a flavour theory predicts, it predicts quotients: a mass alone is not a prediction, because the unit it is quoted in is unobservable. That is the count of The Parameter Count as the Measure of the Framework's Boundary: one unit is arbitrary and the physical content is eighteen dimensionless numbers, of which nine are the Yukawa entries.

3. Three Roots and No Amplitude

There is no wave amplitude in this note. Nothing here vibrates with an amplitude, and no medium is given one. What the ladder uses is a mass ratio and the square root of that ratio. Three different square roots appear in the arithmetic, and a note that writes one of them must say which. None of the three is the amplitude of a wave.

The first: the root of the mass ratio, and it is the ladder's variable.

$$ \rho_{i}:=\frac{\nu_{m_i}}{\nu_{m_1}}, \qquad a_{i}:=\sqrt{\rho_{i}}, \qquad [\rho_{i}]=[a_{i}]=1 . $$

[MEASURED] For the charged leptons, with $\nu_{m_1}=\nu_{m_e}$, the ratios are $\rho=(1,\,206.7683,\,3477.2283)$ and their roots are $a=(1,\,14.37944,\,58.96803)$. Dividing by $\nu_{m_1}$ rather than by $\nu_v$ keeps the numbers near one; dividing by nothing at all is what makes the printed form of Koide's relation carry a unit. The normalisation is free, because $Q$ is invariant under $a_i\to\lambda a_i$, and it is made once here.

The second: the mode coefficient, which is a normalisation and not a ratio.

$$ A_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\nu_{m_i}}}, \qquad [A_{i}]=\mathsf{L}^{1/2}, \qquad \frac{A_{i}}{A_{j}}=\sqrt{\frac{\nu_{m_j}}{\nu_{m_i}}}=\frac{a_{j}}{a_{i}} . $$

[ALGEBRA] The half dimension is fixed by the quantisation and not chosen: the measure carries $\mathsf{L}^{-3}$, the ladder operator $\mathsf{L}^{3/2}$, the coefficient $\mathsf{L}^{1/2}$, and the three sum to the field's $\mathsf{L}^{-1}$. The coefficient is the factor that makes the field carry its dimension; it is not a displacement of anything and it carries no physics beyond that. Its ratio is a pure number, and its square is a ratio of two number densities. [MEASURED] The measured ratios are $A_\mu/A_e=1/14.3794=0.069544$ and $A_\tau/A_e=1/58.9680=0.016958$, which are $a_e/a_\mu$ and $a_e/a_\tau$.

The third: the printed root, which is the one the title names.

The object a textbook writes as the square root of a mass is $\sqrt{m_i}$, of dimension $(\text{mass})^{1/2}$; read in this note's variable it is $\sqrt{\nu_{m_i}}$, of dimension $\mathsf{L}^{-1/2}$. It is neither of the two above. Alone it has no value that survives a change of unit; divided by another one of its kind it produces $a_i$ and nothing else. That is the square root that is not a mass: it is a root of a ratio, and the ratio is the object.

The root of the coupling is the same object as $a_i$. The doublet coupling is $y_i=\sqrt2\,\nu_{m_i}/\nu_v$, so its root is

$$ s_{i}:=\sqrt{y_{i}}=\left(\frac{\sqrt2\,\nu_{m_i}}{\nu_v}\right)^{1/2}, \qquad [s_{i}]=1, \qquad \frac{s_{i}}{s_{j}}=\sqrt{\frac{\nu_{m_i}}{\nu_{m_j}}}=\frac{a_{i}}{a_{j}} . $$

So $s_i$ and $a_i$ differ by one rung-independent factor, $s_i=(\sqrt2\,\nu_{m_1}/\nu_v)^{1/2}a_i$, and every ratio is identical. The vertex of §The Coupling Is a Quotient has its own root, $\sqrt{y_i/\sqrt2}=s_i/2^{1/4}$, which differs from $s_i$ by the fixed factor $2^{1/4}$ and by nothing else.

The coefficient and the root are reciprocal, and the product is fixed. [ALGEBRA] From the two definitions, $A_i^{2}=1/(4\pi\nu_{m_i})$ and $a_i^{2}=\nu_{m_i}/\nu_{m_1}$, hence

$$ A_{i}\,a_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\nu_{m_1}}}, $$

which is the same for every rung and carries $\mathsf{L}^{1/2}$. The product is constant, so a statement in the coefficient is a statement in the root with every ratio inverted. Only the root is dimensionless, so only statements in the root are scale-free, and the ladder of The Excitation Ladder Theory of Flavour uses the root for that reason.

What this settles about the square root. Koide's relation is a statement about the $a_i$, that is, about the roots of the mass ratios, and about nothing else. No amplitude is involved at any step. §Koide in Wave Numbers writes it that way and never takes a root of a dimensioned quantity.

4. Koide in Wave Numbers

The relation, in the variable that makes it dimensionless. With the three wave numbers of one tower,

$$ Q=\frac{\nu_{m_e}+\nu_{m_\mu}+\nu_{m_\tau}}{\left(\sqrt{\nu_{m_e}}+\sqrt{\nu_{m_\mu}}+\sqrt{\nu_{m_\tau}}\right)^{2}} . $$

[ALGEBRA] The numerator carries $\mathsf{L}^{-1}$ and the denominator carries the square of $\mathsf{L}^{-1/2}$, which is $\mathsf{L}^{-1}$; the ratio is dimensionless term by term, and no unit cancels in a way the reader has to take on trust. [ALGEBRA] The value is

$$ Q=\frac{1.5188\times10^{15}}{2.2782\times10^{15}}=0.66666 , $$

where the numerator is the sum of the three wave numbers and the denominator is the square of the sum of their square roots, $\left(6.4199\times10^{5}+9.2314\times10^{6}+3.7857\times10^{7}\right)^{2}$. [ALGEBRA] Carried to full precision the value is $0.666660511$, which is $-6.16\times10^{-6}$ below $2/3$ in absolute value, that is $-9.2$ parts per million of $2/3$. The same computation in the root of the coupling, $s_i=\sqrt{y_i}$, gives the same number, because $\nu_{v}$ cancels between numerator and denominator: the relation is invariant under a change of the wave number that sets the unit, and that invariance is the reason it can be written in any of the three variables.

The identity behind it. [ALGEBRA] For any three roots $s_i$ with mean $\bar s$ and standard deviation $\sigma_s$,

$$ Q=\frac{\sum_i s_i^{2}}{\left(\sum_i s_i\right)^{2}}=\frac{1}{3}\left(1+\left(\frac{\sigma_s}{\bar s}\right)^{2}\right), $$

which is the parallel-axis identity and not a fit. So $Q=2/3$ is exactly $\sigma_s=\bar s$: the three coupling roots are as spread as their own mean. [MEASURED] The measured spread is $0.9999908$, and the same number read as an angle is $44.9997^{\circ}$.

The count is not an accident of the numeral three. [ALGEBRA] The same identity gives $Q_N=2/N$ at $\sigma=\bar s$ for any count $N$, because the $1/N$ of the normalisation is the only place the count enters. The value $2/3$ is the case $N=3$ of a dimension-free condition, and the condition is the one to explain.

The triple is the shape statement, and Koide is the one number about it.

7. The Phase, Which Koide Does Not Fix

The shape has two coordinates and Koide fixes one. [ALGEBRA] The normalised triple is a cosine of fixed modulation on a circle of three sites:

$$ \hat a_i=1+\sqrt2\,\cos\!\left(\delta+\frac{2\pi i}{3}\right), \qquad i=1,2,3, \qquad \sum_i\hat a_i=3, \qquad \sum_i\left(\hat a_i-1\right)^{2}=2.9999446 . $$

The coefficient of the cosine is $\sqrt2$ and it is Koide: with a coefficient $R$ in place of $\sqrt2$ the sum of squares is $\tfrac32R^{2}$, so the measured $2.9999446$ gives $R=\sqrt{2\times2.9999446/3}=1.414200$ against $\sqrt2=1.414214$, two parts in $10^{5}$.

The convention, stated once. $i=1$ is the electron, so the electron carries the argument $\delta+120^{\circ}$. [MEASURED] The phase is

$$ \delta=12.7328^{\circ}, \qquad \delta+120^{\circ}=132.7328^{\circ}, $$

and it is not fitted. Three numbers determine it in closed form,

$$ \tan\delta=\frac{\sqrt3\left(\hat a_\mu-\hat a_e\right)}{2\hat a_\tau-\hat a_\mu-\hat a_e} =\frac{0.9351}{4.1383}=0.22596 , $$

which is the corpus's formula read in the normalised roots; relabelling the rungs shifts $\delta$ by $120^{\circ}$, so the number means nothing without the convention. [ALGEBRA] The coordinates $\left(1-0.9596489,\ 1-0.4197742,\ 1+1.3794231\right)$ are the three values of one cosine, so the triple is a point on a circle and not a curve.

What the reconstruction costs. [ALGEBRA] The cosine returns the three normalised roots to $1.3\times10^{-5}$ in $\hat a_i$, which is the same nine parts per million by which Koide misses $2/3$; the residual is the Koide violation and not a separate error. The corpus's Flavour Research, §14.11, quotes the same pair, $\delta=12.73282^{\circ}$ and the first-rung argument $132.7328^{\circ}$, and the two agree.

What this makes the open question. Koide is one condition on a two-parameter shape, so it leaves exactly one number free, and that number is the phase. A mechanism that produces $Q=2/3$ produces the modulation and says nothing about $\delta$; a mechanism that produces $\delta$ produces the whole triple. The corpus records the same conclusion for $N=3$ in Flavour Research, §16 and §17, where the coefficient $k$ and the phase $\delta$ are the two shape coordinates and $k=1$ is Koide.

8. The Coupling Is Scale-Bearing, and the Scale Is a Wave Number

The test, in the new variable. A coupling statement cannot drop its scale. In wave numbers the scale is itself a wave number, $\nu_{\mathrm{rg}}$, at which the coupling is read, and the test is one question: can the sentence drop $\nu_{\mathrm{rg}}$? [MEASURED] The bottom Yukawa shows why the answer is no:

$\nu_{\mathrm{rg}}$ in $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ $y_b$
$3.3714\times10^{15}$ (at the bottom's own wave number) $2.40\times10^{-2}$
$1.0089\times10^{17}$ (at the scalar's wave number) $1.18\times10^{-2}$

The ratio is $2.03$. It is a running and not an arithmetic error, and the two entries are the same coupling read at two wave numbers. The corpus's audit of scheme dependence in The Yukawa Sector, §What the Higgs Coupling Makes Testable, is the published record of the same fact.

The same test applied to this note. Every number in §Koide in Wave Numbers is a ratio read at the leptons' own wave numbers, and the three are not equal: $\nu_{m_e}\ll \nu_{m_\mu}\ll \nu_{m_\tau}$. Strictly, then, the relation as printed mixes three scales, and its $9\times10^{-6}$ precision is a statement about the mixture. [OPEN] Two checks are owed and neither is done here: recompute $Q$ with the three couplings each run to one common $\nu_{\mathrm{rg}}$, and say by how much it moves. The external folder articles_external/Koide/ holds a paper on exactly this scale dependence, and the check is the first thing to run before the precision is quoted anywhere.

Why the note keeps the coupling and the scale apart. A quotient of two wave numbers is dimensionless and it is still scale-bearing, because the denominator $\nu_{v}$ is fixed once and the reading is taken at a stated $\nu_{\mathrm{rg}}$. Dimensionless and scale-free are different properties, and $y_i$ has the first and not the second. Confusing the two is the error that produces a "prediction" of a mass ratio at no scale at all.

9. What the Framework Does Not Supply

The matrices live where the algebra does not act. [ALGEBRA] The fermion space of the corpus is $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{rep}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{N_g}$ with the algebra acting trivially on the second factor, so the Yukawa matrices are precisely the commutant of the algebra's action. An algebra cannot constrain an operator in its commutant: the parameter space is $6N_g^{2}=54$ real numbers at three generations, reduced to thirteen by the flavour symmetry, and every one of the thirteen is a dimensionless ratio of wave numbers.

Why this is the right thing to be stuck on. The coupling is the one object of the sector the algebra is silent about by construction, and it is dimensionless. A framework that fixes norms, ratios of wave numbers included, fixes everything except this; so a flavour programme that works in wave numbers has isolated exactly the object it must attack, and has isolated it without a unit in the way. That is the whole gain of the writing.

The framework's status, stated plainly. [PUBLISHED] The mass selection rule requires a non-central scalar, and the corpus's scalar is central; the Yukawa coupling of a non-central scalar is absent. This note does not supply it and does not claim it.

10. What This Note Leaves Open

Four questions, in decreasing order of sharpness.

  1. Why is the spread equal to the mean? [OPEN] Koide is $\sigma_s/\bar s=1$, one number, and nothing in the corpus produces it. In the new variable the question is sharp: why is the normalised root on the sphere's diagonal at $45^{\circ}$?
  2. What is the phase? [OPEN] $\delta=12.7328^{\circ}$ is obtained in closed form and unexplained, and it is the only free coordinate left once Koide is imposed.
  3. Does the relation survive one common $\nu_{\mathrm{rg}}$? [OPEN] The three leptons are read at three wave numbers; the mixture is untested here.
  4. Does the condition hold for quarks? [PUBLISHED] The literature's coefficient is $k\approx1.08$ for the down type and $k\approx1.25$ for the up type, at $\nu_{\mathrm{rg}}=1.6130\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, so the leptons are the only tower with $k=1$. Whatever fixes $k=1$ is either lepton-specific or a property of the pole wave numbers, and this note cannot tell which.

$\nu_{m_i}$ The same for one species, $i=e,\mu,\tau$
$\nu_{v}$ The electroweak scale read as a wave number, $1.9859\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$; the only scale of the note; defined in §1

$y_{i}/\sqrt2=\nu_{m_i}/\nu_{v}$ The physical vertex, the quotient the scalar fluctuation multiplies; the doublet coupling $y_i$ divided by $\sqrt2$
