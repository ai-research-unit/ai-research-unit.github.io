The Yukawa Coupling in Wave Numbers: One Quotient of Two Wave Numbers, Three Amplitudes, and the Square Root That Is Not a Mass

Status of this note. Every claim carries a tag. [MEASURED] is a number from experiment. [ALGEBRA] is exact arithmetic on stated inputs. [PUBLISHED] is a claim of a cited paper. [ANSATZ] is an assumption made here. [OPEN] is a question this note does not settle.

Ledger on values. Every Yukawa value below is the literature's, read from the table of The Yukawa Sector and from the Particle Data Group. The corpus makes one scale-bearing coupling statement, and it is in The One Coupling That Returns the Standard Model. Nothing here is a second one. This note changes the variable in which the couplings are written; it asserts no strength of its own.

Scope. An internal working note. It is not a menu entry, it states no result of the model, and it changes no published article. It is the working record behind the search for a dimensionless form of Koide's relation, kept beside Flavour Research.

Introduction

The claim. The Yukawa coupling is the one number of the flavour sector that carries no dimension, and there is exactly one way of writing it in which its dimensionlessness is visible on the page: as the quotient of two wave numbers. This note fixes that writing, names the three dimensionless objects it produces, and says where the square root that Koide's relation requires actually comes from.

What is being repaired. Koide's relation is printed as a ratio between a sum of masses and the square of a sum of square roots of masses. The numerator carries a unit of mass; the denominator carries the square of the square root of a unit of mass; and the ratio of the two is a pure number only because the unit cancels. That cancellation is correct but invisible, and a reader who asks what one square root of a mass is gets no answer from the printed formula. The answer is that a square root of a mass is nothing on its own. It becomes something when it is divided by another square root of a mass, and that quotient is what the formula is really built from.

What this note does, in four steps. It writes the mass as a wave number, once, and then uses nothing else. It shows that the Yukawa coupling is a quotient of two such wave numbers, hence dimensionless and scale-bearing at the same time. It separates the two objects that both deserve the name amplitude and shows that they are reciprocal, which is why the ladder's square root and the quantised field's square root are not the same object. And it writes Koide's relation, the ladder and the phase in that variable, so that no square root of a dimensioned quantity is taken anywhere.

What this note does not do. It derives nothing. Every number below is measured, and the three questions the writing exposes — why the amplitude spread is one, what the phase is, and whether the relation survives being read at one wave number — are left open, in §What This Note Leaves Open.

1. The Conversion, Made Once and Used Thereafter

The definition. The mass of a particle is read in this note as a wave number,

$$ k_{m} := \frac{2\pi m\,c}{h}, \qquad [k_{m}]=\mathsf{L}^{-1}, $$

the angular wave number of the corpus, the number of radians of phase per unit length, of unit $\mathrm{rad\,m^{-1}}$. The subscript names the input, which is one species; the species is written inside the subscript, $k_{m_e}$, $k_{m_\mu}$, $k_{m_\tau}$, and a wave number is never labelled by the name of a particle alone. The glyph and its readings are those of The Three Wavenumbers of a Particle, and the letter is not an index: $k$ carries wave numbers only.

Three quantities, three definitions, and the label of each. The wave number $k_m$ is angular, so the length it is inverted to is a wavelength and the inversion carries a $2\pi$. The three are fixed once, here, and every later display uses one of them without a conversion:

$$ \lambda_{m}:=\frac{2\pi}{k_{m}}=\frac{h}{m\,c}, \qquad \tilde\nu_{m}:=\frac{1}{\lambda_{m}}=\frac{k_{m}}{2\pi}=\frac{m\,c}{h}, $$

so $\lambda_m$ is the wavelength in metres and $\tilde\nu_m$ is the spectroscopic wave number in $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$, the number of waves per metre, which is the quantity a spectrometer quotes. The label tells the reader which is meant: $k_m$ is angular, $\mathrm{rad\,m^{-1}}$; $\tilde\nu_m$ is a count of waves, $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$; $\lambda_m$ is a length, $\mathrm{m}$.

The numbers, obtained once. With the measured masses $0.51099895$, $105.6583755$ and $1776.86\ \mathrm{MeV}$, the conversion $\frac{h}{2\pi}\,c=197.32698\ \mathrm{MeV}\cdot\mathrm{fm}$ and $h\,c=1239.84198\ \mathrm{MeV}\cdot\mathrm{fm}$,

| species | $k_{m}$ in $\mathrm{rad\,m^{-1}}$ | $\tilde\nu_{m}$ in $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ | $\lambda_{m}=1/\tilde\nu_{m}$ | |---|---|---| | $e$ | $2.5896\times10^{12}$ | $4.1215\times10^{11}$ | $2.4263\ \mathrm{pm}$ | | $\mu$ | $5.3545\times10^{14}$ | $8.5219\times10^{13}$ | $11.734\ \mathrm{fm}$ | | $\tau$ | $9.0046\times10^{15}$ | $1.4331\times10^{15}$ | $0.69777\ \mathrm{fm}$ |

The corpus writes both, and the two are named apart. The conventions define $\lambda_m=2\pi/k_m=h/(mc)$, the wavelength, and the separate symbol $\bar\lambda_m=1/k_m=\frac{h}{2\pi mc}$, the length per radian: $386.159\ \mathrm{fm}$ for the electron, against $2.426\ \mathrm{pm}$ for its wavelength. The length per radian is the one the operators use, since a derivative returns an inverse length and the exponent carries radians. It is not a wavelength and it is not $\tilde\nu_m^{-1}$, and §The Defect in the Corpus's Lambda records the repair.

What a uniform rescaling costs. [ALGEBRA] $k_m$ and $\tilde\nu_m$ differ by the constant $2\pi$, so every quantity that is a ratio of wave numbers is the same in either, and every quantity that is linear in one is $2\pi$ times the other. Koide and the ladder are ratios, so they are unchanged: $Q$ computed from $k_m$ is $0.666660511$ and from $\tilde\nu_m$ is $0.666660511$.

The electroweak scale, by the same rule. The vacuum expectation value is a mass, so it becomes a wave number by the same quotient, with $v=246.22\ \mathrm{GeV}$:

$$ k_{v} := \frac{2\pi v\,c}{h}=1.2478\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}} . $$

This one number is the whole of the electroweak scale in what follows. Every Yukawa statement below is a ratio to it, and it never appears in any other form.

Why a wave number and not a mass. Three reasons, and the third is the one that matters here. A wave number is the coefficient the equations already carry: the gradient multiplies by $i\tilde K_{\Phi}$, the mass term of the operator is $k_m$, and a derivative returns an inverse length. A wave number is what the algebra's norm gives: the biquaternion norm of the momentum is $-\langle\tilde K_{\Phi}\rangle_{\natural}=k_{m}^{2}$, so $k_m$ is already the square root of an invariant of the algebra. And a ratio of two wave numbers is a pure number with no unit at all, which is the only kind of number a theory can predict.

The amplitude, by the same rule. The canonical mode amplitude of one quantum is fixed by the wave number alone, and the corpus's form is $\sqrt{\frac{h}{2\pi}\,c/(2E)}$; on the mass shell

$$ A=\sqrt{\frac{h\,c}{4\pi E}}\ \propto\ k_{m}^{-1/2}, \qquad [A]=\mathsf{L}^{1/2}, $$

so the absolute amplitude carries a half dimension and only the quotient of two amplitudes is a pure number. This is the second and last place where the two constants of the note appear; the amplitude is then used in this form only, in §Two Amplitudes, Mutually Reciprocal.

The declaration. From this point to the end of the note, the symbols for the mass, for the action and for the speed of light do not appear. Every dimensioned quantity is a wave number of dimension $\mathsf{L}^{-1}$, and every dimensionless quantity is a quotient of two of them. The only letters that survive are the symbols of the units in which the wave numbers are quoted, the metre and the second; a unit symbol is not a quantity and carries no physics. The one warning. $k_{m}$ is a wave number, of dimension $\mathsf{L}^{-1}$; it is not dimensionless. Writing it in $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ and calling it a pure number would be the error this note exists to avoid. The dimensionless objects are ratios of it, and they are the subject of §The Three Dimensionless Objects.

2. The Coupling Is a Quotient

The matrix in wave numbers. The Yukawa sector writes a mass matrix as a dimensionless matrix times the electroweak scale. Read in wave numbers the same line is exact and carries a dimension on each side:

$$ K_{f}=\frac{Y_{f}\,k_{v}}{\sqrt2}, \qquad [Y_{f}]=1, \qquad [K_{f}]=\mathsf{L}^{-1}, $$

with $Y_f$ the complex $3\times3$ Yukawa matrix of the type $f$. The matrix of masses is a matrix of wave numbers, and the only thing the coupling supplies is a pure number per entry.

The diagonal reading. The measured spectrum diagonalises $K_f$, so each rung gives one dimensionless entry:

$$ y_{i}=\frac{\sqrt2\,k_{m_i}}{k_{v}}, \qquad [y_{i}]=1, \qquad K_{f}^{\mathrm{diag}}=\frac{k_{v}}{\sqrt2}\,\mathrm{diag}\left(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}\right), $$

This is the object of the note. $y_i$ is a quotient of two wave numbers, $k_{m_i}$ and $k_v$, both of dimension $\mathsf{L}^{-1}$; the quotient has no dimension, and it is the number the Lagrangian carries. Nothing is approximated and nothing is set to one.

[ALGEBRA] The top sits at $y_t=0.9919$, which is one to within one per cent; the corpus's Yukawa table states this and attributes it to an infrared fixed point of the renormalisation-group equation, and that attribution is the literature's, not this note's. Why the quotient is the natural object and the mass is not. A mass needs a unit, and a unit is a choice. The quotient of two wave numbers needs no unit at all. Whatever a flavour theory predicts, it predicts quotients: a mass alone is not a prediction, because the unit it is quoted in is unobservable. That is the count of The Parameter Count as the Measure of the Framework's Boundary: one unit is arbitrary and the physical content is eighteen dimensionless numbers, of which nine are the Yukawa entries.

3. Two Amplitudes, Mutually Reciprocal

The word amplitude names two different objects here, and they are reciprocal. A note that writes a square root of a mass must say which one it means, and the corpus's ladder and the corpus's quantisation are on opposite sides of the division.

The first: the field's mode amplitude. By §1 the canonical mode amplitude is fixed by the wave number alone, and it goes as the inverse square root of it:

$$ A_{i}\propto k_{m_i}^{-1/2}, \qquad [A_{i}]=\mathsf{L}^{1/2}, \qquad \frac{A_{i}}{A_{j}}=\sqrt{\frac{k_{m_j}}{k_{m_i}}} . $$

[ALGEBRA] The absolute amplitude carries a half dimension, $\mathsf{L}^{1/2}$, and that half dimension is legitimate: the field it multiplies carries $\mathsf{L}^{-3/2}$ and the pair carries an inverse length. The ratio of two amplitudes is a pure number, and it is the observable. [ALGEBRA] The measured ratios are $A_\mu/A_e=1/14.3794=0.069544$ and $A_\tau/A_e=1/58.9680=0.016958$.

The second: the Yukawa amplitude. The coupling's own square root is the amplitude for the rung to emit or absorb the scalar, and it goes the other way:

$$ s_{i}:=\sqrt{y_{i}}=\left(\frac{\sqrt2\,k_{m_i}}{k_{v}}\right)^{1/2}, \qquad [s_{i}]=1, \qquad \frac{s_{i}}{s_{j}}=\sqrt{\frac{k_{m_i}}{k_{m_j}}}=\frac{A_{j}}{A_{i}} . $$

rung $s_i=\sqrt{y_i}$ $A_i$ in units of $A_e$
$e$ $1.7132\times10^{-3}$ $1$
$\mu$ $2.4635\times10^{-2}$ $0.069544$
$\tau$ $1.0102\times10^{-1}$ $0.016958$

The two are reciprocal, and the product is fixed. $A_i\,s_i\propto k_{m_i}^{-1/2}k_{m_i}^{1/2}=1$, so a statement in one amplitude is a statement in the other with every ratio inverted. The ladder of The Excitation Ladder Theory of Flavour uses the second, because its rule is that energies go as the square of an amplitude and its variable is therefore the coupling's root; the quantisation uses the first, because the measure fixes the mode amplitude and its squared modulus is the number density.

What this settles about the square root. The object that has a meaning is $s_i$, the square root of a dimensionless coupling; its absolute value is a pure number once $k_v$ is fixed. The object that has no meaning on its own is $\sqrt{k_{m_i}}$, of dimension $\mathsf{L}^{-1/2}$, and the object printed in textbooks, the square root of a mass in the square root of a unit of mass, is neither of these until it is divided by another one. Koide's relation is a statement about the $s_i$ and about nothing else, and §Koide in Wave Numbers writes it that way.

4. Koide in Wave Numbers

The relation, in the variable that makes it dimensionless. With the three wave numbers of one tower,

$$ Q=\frac{k_{m_e}+k_{m_\mu}+k_{m_\tau}}{\left(\sqrt{k_{m_e}}+\sqrt{k_{m_\mu}}+\sqrt{k_{m_\tau}}\right)^{2}} . $$

[ALGEBRA] The numerator carries $\mathsf{L}^{-1}$ and the denominator carries the square of $\mathsf{L}^{-1/2}$, which is $\mathsf{L}^{-1}$; the ratio is dimensionless term by term, and no unit cancels in a way the reader has to take on trust. [ALGEBRA] The value is

$$ Q=\frac{9.5427\times10^{15}}{1.4314\times10^{16}}=0.66666 , $$

where the numerator is the sum of the three wave numbers and the denominator is the square of the sum of their square roots, $\left(1.6092\times10^{6}+2.3140\times10^{7}+9.4893\times10^{7}\right)^{2}$. [ALGEBRA] Carried to full precision the value is $0.666660511$, which is $-6.16\times10^{-6}$ below $2/3$ in absolute value, that is $-9.2$ parts per million of $2/3$. The same computation in the Yukawa amplitude $\sqrt{y_i}$ gives the same number, because $k_v$ cancels between numerator and denominator: the relation is invariant under a change of the wave number that sets the unit, and that invariance is the reason it can be written in any of the three variables.

The identity behind it. [ALGEBRA] For any three amplitudes $s_i$ with mean $\bar s$ and standard deviation $\sigma_s$,

$$ Q=\frac{\sum_i s_i^{2}}{\left(\sum_i s_i\right)^{2}}=\frac{1}{3}\left(1+\left(\frac{\sigma_s}{\bar s}\right)^{2}\right), $$

which is the parallel-axis identity and not a fit. So $Q=2/3$ is exactly $\sigma_s=\bar s$: the three Yukawa amplitudes are as spread as their own mean. [MEASURED] The measured spread is $0.9999908$, and the same number read as an angle is $44.9997^{\circ}$.

The count is not an accident of the numeral three. [ALGEBRA] The same identity gives $Q_N=2/N$ at $\sigma=\bar s$ for any count $N$, because the $1/N$ of the normalisation is the only place the count enters. The value $2/3$ is the case $N=3$ of a dimension-free condition, and the condition is the one to explain.

7. The Phase, Which Koide Does Not Fix

The shape has two coordinates and Koide fixes one. [ALGEBRA] The normalised triple is a cosine of fixed modulation on a circle of three sites:

$$ \omega_i=1+\sqrt2\,\cos\!\left(\delta+\frac{2\pi i}{3}\right), \qquad i=0,1,2, \qquad \sum_i\omega_i=3, \qquad \sum_i\left(\omega_i-1\right)^{2}=2.9999446 . $$

The modulation amplitude is $\sqrt2$ and it is Koide: any other amplitude would give another $Q$, and $\sum_i\left(\omega_i-1\right)^{2}=2R^{2}$ with $R$ that amplitude, so the measured $2.9999446$ is $3$ to five parts in $10^{5}$. [MEASURED] The phase is

$$ \delta=132.73^{\circ}, $$

obtained by fitting the three normalised amplitudes, with a residual of $2\times10^{-9}$; the corpus's own reconstruction of the same triple quotes $132.732^{\circ}$, and the two agree. [ALGEBRA] The coordinates $\left(1-0.9596489,\ 1-0.4197742,\ 1+1.3794231\right)$ are the three values of one cosine, so the triple is a point on a circle and not a curve.

What this makes the open question. Koide is one condition on a two-parameter shape, so it leaves exactly one number free, and that number is the phase. A mechanism that produces $Q=2/3$ produces the modulation and says nothing about $\delta$; a mechanism that produces $\delta$ produces the whole triple. The corpus records the same conclusion for $N=3$ in Flavour Research, §16 and §17, where the coefficient $k$ and the phase $\delta$ are the two shape coordinates and $k=1$ is Koide.

8. The Coupling Is Scale-Bearing, and the Scale Is a Wave Number

The test, in the new variable. A coupling statement cannot drop its scale. In wave numbers the scale is itself a wave number, $k_{\mathrm{rg}}$, at which the coupling is read, and the test is one question: can the sentence drop $k_{\mathrm{rg}}$? [MEASURED] The bottom Yukawa shows why the answer is no:

$k_{\mathrm{rg}}$ in $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ $y_b$
$2.1183\times10^{16}$ (at the bottom's own wave number) $2.40\times10^{-2}$
$6.3392\times10^{17}$ (at the scalar's wave number) $1.18\times10^{-2}$

The ratio is $2.03$. It is a running and not an arithmetic error, and the two entries are the same coupling read at two wave numbers. The corpus's audit of scheme dependence in The Yukawa Sector, §What the Higgs Coupling Makes Testable, is the published record of the same fact.

The same test applied to this note. Every number in §Koide in Wave Numbers is a ratio read at the leptons' own wave numbers, and the three are not equal: $k_{m_e}\ll k_{m_\mu}\ll k_{m_\tau}$. Strictly, then, the relation as printed mixes three scales, and its $9\times10^{-6}$ precision is a statement about the mixture. [OPEN] Two checks are owed and neither is done here: recompute $Q$ with the three couplings each run to one common $k_{\mathrm{rg}}$, and say by how much it moves. The external folder articles_external/Koide/ holds a paper on exactly this scale dependence, and the check is the first thing to run before the precision is quoted anywhere.

Why the note keeps the coupling and the scale apart. A quotient of two wave numbers is dimensionless and it is still scale-bearing, because the denominator $k_v$ is fixed once and the reading is taken at a stated $k_{\mathrm{rg}}$. Dimensionless and scale-free are different properties, and $y_i$ has the first and not the second. Confusing the two is the error that produces a "prediction" of a mass ratio at no scale at all.

9. What the Framework Does Not Supply

The matrices live where the algebra does not act. [ALGEBRA] The fermion space of the corpus is $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{rep}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{N_g}$ with the algebra acting trivially on the second factor, so the Yukawa matrices are precisely the commutant of the algebra's action. An algebra cannot constrain an operator in its commutant: the parameter space is $6N_g^{2}=54$ real numbers at three generations, reduced to thirteen by the flavour symmetry, and every one of the thirteen is a dimensionless ratio of wave numbers.

Why this is the right thing to be stuck on. The coupling is the one object of the sector the algebra is silent about by construction, and it is dimensionless. A framework that fixes norms, ratios of wave numbers included, fixes everything except this; so a flavour programme that works in wave numbers has isolated exactly the object it must attack, and has isolated it without a unit in the way. That is the whole gain of the writing.

The framework's status, stated plainly. [PUBLISHED] The mass selection rule requires a non-central scalar, and the corpus's scalar is central; the Yukawa coupling of a non-central scalar is absent. This note does not supply it and does not claim it.

10. What This Note Leaves Open

Four questions, in decreasing order of sharpness.

  1. Why is the spread equal to the mean? [OPEN] Koide is $\sigma_s/\bar s=1$, one number, and nothing in the corpus produces it. In the new variable the question is sharp: why is the normalised Yukawa amplitude on the sphere's diagonal at $45^{\circ}$?
  2. What is the phase? [OPEN] $\delta=132.73^{\circ}$ is fitted and unexplained, and it is the only free coordinate left once Koide is imposed.
  3. Does the relation survive one common $k_{\mathrm{rg}}$? [OPEN] The three leptons are read at three wave numbers; the mixture is untested here.
  4. Does the condition hold for quarks? [PUBLISHED] The literature's coefficient is $k\approx1.08$ for the down type and $k\approx1.25$ for the up type, at $k_{\mathrm{rg}}=1.0135\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, so the leptons are the only tower with $k=1$. Whatever fixes $k=1$ is either lepton-specific or a property of the pole wave numbers, and this note cannot tell which.

$\sigma_s/\bar s$ The amplitude spread in units of the mean; $1$ at Koide; measured $0.9999908$
$\delta$ The phase of the three-site cosine; measured $132.73^{\circ}$; the one coordinate Koide leaves free
$k_{\mathrm{rg}}$ The wave number at which a coupling is read; the scale a coupling statement may not drop
$\mathsf{L}$ The dimension of length
