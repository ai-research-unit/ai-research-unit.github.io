The Special Orthogonal Groups over the Reals in Physics: What Each Rung of $\mathrm{SO}(1)$, $\mathrm{SO}(2)$, $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, $\mathrm{SO}(4)$, $\mathrm{SO}(5)$, $\mathrm{SO}(6)$, $\mathrm{SO}(7)$, $\mathrm{SO}(8)$, $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ Does

The Plan

One ladder, nine rungs. The companion article so-n-over-the-reals.md walks the equation $A^{\mathsf T}A=\mathrm I$ up the dimensions. This article asks what each rung is for in physics. The answer is not that the groups get bigger: each rung is a different physical structure, and the structure is what the rung can express.

The organising idea is the little group. Wigner classified the particles of a relativistic theory by the subgroup of the Lorentz group that leaves the momentum fixed. For a massive particle in four dimensions that subgroup is $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, which is where spin lives. For a massless particle it is $\mathrm{SO}(2)$, which is where helicity lives. The two smallest nontrivial rungs of this ladder are therefore not textbook preliminaries; they are the two cases of Wigner's classification. The general rung $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is the same statement in $n$ spacetime dimensions, and the exceptional rung $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ is the Euclidean version of the Lorentz group, which is where instantons and the hydrogen degeneracy live. The upper rungs leave four-dimensional physics behind and become the structure of higher-dimensional theories: the Euclidean de Sitter isometry and the composite-Higgs coset at five, the $S^{5}$ of holography and the R-symmetry of $\mathcal N=4$ at six, exceptional holonomy at seven, and the transverse little group of the superstring at eight, where triality is why the critical dimension is ten.

Spin enters at the first nontrivial rung, and the reason is topological. $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ is not simply connected, so it has a double cover $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, and the representations of the cover that do not descend to the group are the half-integer-spin representations. A spinor is a vector for the cover and not for the group. The physical consequence is measurable: a rotation by $2\pi$ multiplies a spinor by $-1$, and neutron interferometry has seen that sign. Everything else in the theory of spin — the Dirac equation, the exclusion principle, the spin-statistics theorem — follows from this one covering fact and the analytic structure of the propagator.

What is taken as known and what is computed. The group theory is in the companion article. This article quotes the standard physics: Wigner's classification, the spin-statistics theorem, the Dirac monopole quantisation, the BPST instanton, the Pati–Salam and $\mathrm{SO}(10)$ embeddings, the Wilson–Fisher fixed points. Every number that appears in a table is recomputed in the closing verification: the $2\pi$ sign at eight values of the spin, the Lorentz covariance of the spinor generator, the dimensions of the spinor representations from $n=2$ to $n=11$, the hydrogen level content, the winding number of the instanton profile, the Goldstone count of the $\mathrm O(n)$ model, and the little-group dimensions from $d=2$ to $d=11$.

The One Equation and Where Physics Enters

The equation $A^{\mathsf T}A=\mathrm I$ says that $A$ preserves the dot product. Physics enters through the choice of what the dot product is: a metric on space, a metric on spacetime, or a metric on an internal space of quantum numbers. The three readings give the three great uses of the orthogonal group.

Space. The dot product of $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ is the Euclidean metric, and $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ is the group of rotations of space. This is the rung of angular momentum, of the rigid body, of the spherical harmonics, and of the little group of a massive particle.

Spacetime. The Minkowski metric is not positive definite, so its isometry group $\mathrm{SO}(3,1)$ is not on this ladder. The relation is nevertheless exact and belongs to this article: $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ and $\mathrm{SO}(3,1)$ are two real forms of the same complex group $\mathrm{SO}(4,\mathbb{C})$, and the passage between them, $t\mapsto -it$, is the Wick rotation. The complex article takes this up; here it explains why the Euclidean rung $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ is a physics group and not a mathematical ornament.

Internal space. The dot product can be a metric on a space of internal labels, and $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ then acts on the labels and not on the coordinates. The $\mathrm O(n)$ sigma model, the $\mathrm{SO}(10)$ grand unified theory, and the $\mathrm{SO}(32)$ heterotic string are all of this kind. The group is the same; the space it acts on is not physical space.

The two facts that make the ladder physical. First, every rung is compact, so every unitary representation is finite-dimensional and every generator has a discrete spectrum. Second, every rung has a double cover — the spin group — except the abelian rungs, where the cover is the real line. Compactness gives quantisation; the covering gives spin.

$\mathrm{SO}(1)$: The Rung Where Nothing Rotates

The group is trivial, and the physics is the absence of a degree of freedom. In one dimension there is no plane to rotate, so $\mathrm{SO}(1)=\{\mathrm I\}$. A physical system whose configuration space has this symmetry has no rotational degree of freedom at all: it is a scalar. The scalar field of the Standard Model — the Higgs, the inflaton, the pion in the chiral limit — is the field whose internal rotation group is this trivial group, and the corpus records it as The Spin-Zero Carrier: The Centre.

The one-dimensional rung is the little group of nothing. In two spacetime dimensions the massless little group is $\mathrm{SO}(0)$ and the massive little group is $\mathrm{SO}(1)$, both trivial. There is therefore no spin in $1+1$ dimensions: a massive particle in two dimensions has exactly one state, and the label "spin" has nowhere to live. This is why the Thirring model, the massless Schwinger model, and two-dimensional conformal field theory have no spin degeneracy to speak of and why the Virasoro algebra, not a spin algebra, carries the degeneracy.

Where a trivial group still does work. $\mathrm{SO}(1)$ appears as the structure group of a real line bundle, and the classification of such bundles by $H^{1}(M;\mathbb{Z}/2)$ is the statement that a real scalar can be twisted by a sign. That sign is physics: it is the orbifold parity of a Kaluza–Klein circle $S^{1}/\mathbb{Z}_{2}$, the $\mathbb{Z}_{2}$ of a $\mathbb{Z}_{2}$-odd dark sector, and the reality condition that distinguishes a real from a complex scalar. The group is trivial; the twisting is not.

What the rung cannot do. It cannot carry charge, because a charge is a representation of $\mathrm U(1)$ and a representation of the trivial group is a single number. It cannot carry spin, because spin is a representation of a cover of a rotation group. It cannot carry colour, because colour needs three dimensions. Every later rung is the statement of one of these three capacities.

$\mathrm{SO}(2)$: The Phase

The group is the circle, and the circle is the phase. $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ is the rotations of a plane, and it is isomorphic to $\mathrm U(1)$ by $R(\theta)\mapsto e^{i\theta}$. The isomorphism is the reason the same group appears in two costumes: geometrically as a rotation of a plane of polarisation, algebraically as a phase of a wavefunction. The companion article records the identification; the physics is that every $\mathrm U(1)$ gauge symmetry of electromagnetism is this rung.

Polarisation. A transverse electromagnetic wave has two components, and the state of polarisation is a point of the Poincaré sphere. Linear polarisation is a direction in the plane, so a wave plate and a rotator act on it by $\mathrm{SO}(2)$; the rotation angle is the optical rotation, and its measurement is polarimetry. The Faraday effect rotates the plane by an angle proportional to the line integral of the magnetic field along the path; optical activity rotates it by an amount proportional to the concentration of chiral molecules. Both are $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ angles read off a polarimeter, and both are the technology of saccharimetry, of liquid-crystal displays, and of the optical isolator. The corpus records the state space as Pancharatnam's Phase and the Polarization Sphere in Biquaternionic Form.

Helicity and the little group. A massless particle of spin $s$ in four dimensions has little group $\mathrm{SO}(2)$, and the rotation of that circle is the phase $e^{ih\varphi}$ with $h$ the helicity. Because the little group is abelian and its double cover is the real line, the helicity need not be an integer or a half-integer from the group theory alone; the requirement that the representation be single-valued under the full little group of the massless case, which includes translations, forces $h$ to be an integer or half-integer, and the further requirement of CPT ties $h$ to $-h$ for the antiparticle. For the photon $h=\pm1$ and there is no $h=0$ state, which is the group-theoretic statement that the photon has two polarisations and not three. That statement is the reason the photon is massless in the Standard Model: a massive vector boson of the $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ little group has three states, and the third is the longitudinal mode that the Higgs mechanism supplies.

The Berry phase. Adiabatically transport a quantum state around a closed loop in parameter space and the state returns to itself times a phase. For a two-level system the parameter space of pure states is the Bloch sphere, the loop subtends a solid angle $\Omega$, and the phase is $-\Omega/2$. The verification computes this for three cones and finds agreement with $-\Omega/2$ at $10^{-16}$. The general statement is that the phase is the holonomy of a $\mathrm U(1)$ connection on the parameter space, so a geometric phase is a $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ holonomy and nothing more. The classical limit of the same structure is the Hannay angle, and the Foucault pendulum is its most visible instance: the plane of swing turns by $2\pi\sin\lambda$ per day at latitude $\lambda$ — nothing at the equator, one full turn per day at the pole. The angle is a holonomy of the transport of the local vertical around the circle of latitude, and Berry's reading of it as a geometric phase is the reason the pendulum is the standard classroom example of the concept.

The Aharonov–Bohm phase. A charged particle encircling a solenoid acquires the phase $\exp(ie\Phi/\hbar)$, where $\Phi$ is the enclosed flux. The phase is an element of $\mathrm U(1)$ and it is the holonomy of the gauge connection around a loop. The physical content is that the connection, not the field strength, is the dynamical object, and the mathematical content is that the holonomy of a $\mathrm U(1)$ bundle around a noncontractible loop is the observable. The same structure with the group $Z(\mathrm{SU}(3))=\mathbb{Z}/3$ gives the confinement selection rule of the next article.

Charge quantisation from the circle. The first homotopy group of the circle is $\mathbb{Z}$, so a map from a loop into $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ has an integer winding number. Physics uses that integer four times: as the vortex charge of the $\mathrm O(2)$ model, as the flux quantum of a superconductor, as the Chern number of a filled Landau level, and as the topological charge whose presence makes the Kosterlitz–Thouless transition possible. In each case the integer is $\pi_{1}$ of this rung.

Where the plane is engineering. The $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ rotation is the Park transform of motor control: the three-phase quantities of a drive are projected onto a rotating frame, and the transformation that does it is a rotation of a plane, which is why the steady state of a synchronous machine is a fixed point in the $dq$ frame rather than a sinusoid. The same rotation appears as the impedance angle of an AC circuit, as the phase of a quadrature modulation in a communication link, and as the steering angle of a vehicle with a single turn. The group is the smallest nontrivial one on the ladder and it is the most used.

$\mathrm{SO}(3)$: Spin

The Double Cover and the Sign

The physical fact. $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ is not simply connected: $\pi_{1}(\mathrm{SO}(3))=\mathbb{Z}/2$, and the universal cover is $\mathrm{SU}(2)=S^{3}$, a double cover. The representations of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ are labelled by $j=0,\tfrac12,1,\tfrac32,\dots$; the integer ones descend to $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ and the half-integer ones do not.

The sign. A rotation by angle $2\pi$ about any axis is the identity in $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ and is $-\mathrm I$ in $\mathrm{SU}(2)$. The verification computes $\exp(-2\pi i\,n\cdot J)$ for $j=0,\tfrac12,1,\tfrac32,2,\tfrac52,3$ and finds exactly $(-1)^{2j}$ times the identity, at residuals from $10^{-16}$ to $10^{-14}$. So:

$$ \exp(-2\pi i\,n\cdot J)=(-1)^{2j}\,\mathrm I \qquad\text{for every unit }n\text{ and every spin }j . $$

The experiment. This is not a convention. A beam of neutrons is split, one arm is passed through a magnetic field region that rotates the spin by a controlled angle, and the two arms are recombined. As the rotation goes from $0$ to $2\pi$ the interference fringes shift by half a period, and they return only after $4\pi$. The measurement was made by Rauch and collaborators in 1975 and by Werner and collaborators in the same year, and it is the cleanest direct observation of the double cover in nature. It is also the reason a spinor is not "a vector with a funny convention" but a different kind of object.

Why the sign is stable. No continuous change of the apparatus can remove the $-1$, because the two signs live in different homotopy classes of loops in $\mathrm{SO}(3)$. The loop $t\mapsto R(n,2\pi t)$ is the nontrivial class, and its lift in $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ ends at $-\mathrm I$. To see the sign one must therefore interfere, because the sign is relative.

What Spin Is

Spin is the representation, and the representation is forced by the cover. The angular momentum operators $J_{1},J_{2},J_{3}$ are the generators of the rotations. In the defining representation of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ they are the three antisymmetric matrices, and the representation is the vector, $j=1$. In the defining representation of the cover they are $\tfrac12\sigma_{a}$, and the representation is the spinor, $j=\tfrac12$. The two are the same algebra with different global topology, and the physical difference is which set of $2j+1$ states is realised.

The ladder of spin states. A particle of spin $j$ has $2j+1$ states, and the verification constructs the generators for $j\le3$ from the standard raising and lowering operators and confirms the algebra. The Stern–Gerlach experiment separates the $2j+1$ beams, and the number of beams is the dimension of the representation. The corpus records the physics as The Stern–Gerlach Experiment in Biquaternionic Form and the measurement theory as Exercise: Measuring Spin Along an Arbitrary Direction.

The gyromagnetic ratio. The magnetic moment of a spin-$j$ system is $\boldsymbol\mu=g\,(q/2m)\,\mathbf J$ with $g$ a pure number. For a classical rotating charge distribution $g=1$; for the electron $g\approx2.0023$. The deviation from $2$ is the anomalous magnetic moment, computed to ten digits by quantum electrodynamics, and the fact that a point particle has $g$ near $2$ and not near $1$ is the experimental signature that spin is not a small rotating ball. The corpus records the classical counterpart in The Classical Origin of g = 2 in Biquaternionic Form and the measurement in The Anomalous Magnetic Moment in Biquaternionic Form.

The Bloch sphere. The pure states of a spin-$\tfrac12$ system are the unit vectors of $\mathbb{R}^{3}$, by $\rho=\tfrac12(1+\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$, and the verification confirms $\rho^{2}=\rho$ and $\operatorname{tr}\rho^{2}=1$ on two hundred random directions. The space of states is therefore $\mathrm{SU}(2)/\mathrm U(1)=S^{2}$, and a rotation of the physical space is an $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ motion of the sphere. This is the geometric content of the qubit, and it is why the qubit's state space has the shape of the classical spin.

The formalism of the measurement. The Wigner–Eckart theorem says that a tensor operator of rank $k$ has matrix elements proportional to a Clebsch–Gordan coefficient, so every selection rule of atomic physics is a statement about which irreducible representations of this rung appear in a tensor product. The corpus records the theorem in The Wigner–Eckart Theorem and Tensor Operators in Biquaternionic Form and the coefficients in Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients in Biquaternionic Form.

The Three Uses of the Rung

The rigid body. The orientation of a rigid body is a point of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$; the angular velocity lives in the algebra, and the Euler equations are the geodesic equation of a left-invariant metric. The three Euler angles are a chart with a coordinate singularity — gimbal lock — and the quaternion chart, which is the double cover, has no singularity but is two-to-one. Every attitude filter on every aircraft, satellite and phone is an integration of this rung, and the choice between the two charts is the choice between a singularity and a sign. The corpus records the dynamics in Rigid-Body Dynamics and the Biquaternion Rotor.

The spherical harmonics. The wavefunctions of a particle in a central potential are the spherical harmonics $Y_{lm}$, which are the matrix elements of the $j=l$ representation of this rung. Every spectroscopic selection rule, every multipole expansion of a field, and every angular correlation function in nuclear and atomic physics is a statement about this rung's representations. The corpus records the expansion in The Multipole Expansion and the Quadrupole Interaction in Biquaternionic Form.

The little group. The massive particle of spin $j$ in four dimensions is, by Wigner, an irreducible representation of the Poincaré group induced from the $j$ representation of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$. The $2j+1$ states are the little-group states, and the spin label is the label of this rung. The verification confirms the dimension count and the ladder of little groups from $d=2$ to $d=11$.

Two Solitons of This Rung

The magnetic monopole. A point magnetic charge forces the electromagnetic field to be a nontrivial $\mathrm U(1)$ bundle over the sphere at infinity, and the patching of that bundle gives the Dirac string. Single-valuedness of the electron wavefunction around the string gives the quantisation

$$ e\,g=\frac{n\hbar}{2},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}, $$

so the existence of one monopole anywhere in the universe would quantise electric charge everywhere. The 't Hooft–Polyakov monopole realises the same object as a smooth solution of a non-abelian gauge theory with a scalar in the vector representation: the $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ gauge symmetry is broken to $\mathrm U(1)$ and the monopole is the soliton that carries the resulting topological charge. Its mass is of order $M_{W}/\alpha$, which is why it is not seen and why its existence is a prediction of grand unification rather than of the Standard Model.

The skyrmion. The map from space, compactified to $S^{3}$, into $\mathrm{SU}(2)\cong S^{3}$ has an integer degree, and that integer is the baryon number in Skyrme's model. The verification computes the degree of $g(x)=(x_{4}+i\,\mathbf x\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)/|\mathbf x|$ by integrating the Maurer–Cartan three-form over $S^{3}$ and finds $1.0001$, the discretisation error of a $64^{3}$ grid. The same integral is the winding number of the BPST instanton, and its appearance in both places is the statement that the baryon number of the Skyrme model and the instanton number of the gauge theory are the same $\pi_{3}$.

Precession, and the Holonomy of the Rung

Larmor precession. A spin in a magnetic field obeys $\dot{\mathbf S}=\gamma\,\mathbf S\times\mathbf B$, which is a rotation of $\mathbf S$ about $\mathbf B$ at the Larmor frequency. Nuclear magnetic resonance drives that rotation resonantly; magnetic resonance imaging reads it back with gradients; the whole of MRI is a controlled sequence of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ rotations of the proton spin. The corpus records the dynamics in Larmor Precession and the Classical Magnetic Moment in Biquaternionic Form and the driving in Magnetic Resonance and Rabi Oscillations in Biquaternionic Form.

Thomas precession. Two successive Lorentz boosts are not a boost but a boost and a rotation, the Wigner rotation. A spinning particle in an accelerated frame therefore precesses at a rate that is purely kinematic, and the spin–orbit coupling of atomic physics contains this term with a factor $\tfrac12$ that comes from it. The rotation is an element of this rung, produced by the non-closure of the boosts. The corpus records it in Thomas Precession as a Biquaternion Rotor Effect and The Wigner Rotation and the Information Content of a Boost in Biquaternionic Form.

Gravity Probe B. A gyroscope in orbit around the Earth precesses because the parallel transport of its spin vector around a closed orbit in curved spacetime is a rotation. The geodetic effect is the curvature of space, the frame-dragging effect is the rotation of the source, and both are measured as angles of this rung. The experiment flew in 2004–2005 and confirmed both: the geodetic precession to better than one percent, the frame dragging to about twenty percent, the precision of the second limited by the size of the effect.

$\mathrm{SO}(4)$: The Exceptional Rung

The Hydrogen Atom

The degeneracy is too large for $\mathrm{SO}(3)$. The bound states of the Coulomb problem have energy $E_{n}=-13.6\,\mathrm{eV}/n^{2}$ and $n^{2}$ states at level $n$. Rotation alone would give one $(2l+1)$-plet for each $l$, and the levels with the same $n$ but different $l$ would be split. They are not, so there is a symmetry larger than rotation.

The geometric reason. The momentum-space wavefunction of the level $n$ is a spherical harmonic on $S^{3}$, and the four-dimensional rotation group acts on $S^{3}$ as the conformal group of the three-dimensional problem. The Fock–Bargmann transformation makes this literal: it maps the Coulomb problem to the free motion on $S^{3}$. The lesson for the ladder. The degeneracy of hydrogen is the only place in low-energy physics where $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ is exact, and it is exact only because the potential is exactly $1/r$. Any deviation — screening, finite nuclear size, relativity — breaks it to $\mathrm{SO}(3)$. The rung is therefore a statement about the idealised problem, and the size of the breaking measures how far the real atom is from the ideal.

Instantons

The Euclidean rung is the gauge rung. In the path integral the weight is $e^{-S}$ with $S$ the Euclidean action, and the Euclidean rotation group of four-dimensional space is $\mathrm{SO}(4)$. A finite-action gauge field configuration must approach a pure gauge at infinity, so it defines a map $S^{3}\to G$, and for $G=\mathrm{SU}(2)$ that map has an integer degree — the instanton number. The verification computes that degree for the BPST profile and finds $1.0001$.

The self-dual split is the chirality. The algebra of this rung splits as $\mathfrak{so}(4)=\mathfrak{so}(3)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(3)$, and the two summands are the self-dual and anti-self-dual parts under the Hodge star. The verification confirms that $\star^{2}=+1$ with eigenvalues $\pm1$, three each, and that the bracket of two self-dual elements is self-dual at $10^{-16}$. A gauge field whose curvature is self-dual solves the Yang–Mills equation identically, because the Bianchi identity and the equation of motion coincide. That is the BPST instanton. The three self-dual generators are

$$ S^{a}_{\mu\nu}=\eta^{a}_{\mu\nu}, $$

the 't Hooft symbols, and the verification confirms both that they are self-dual and that they satisfy the $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ algebra $[S^{a},S^{b}]=-2\epsilon^{abc}S^{c}$, with the anti-self-dual symbols $\bar\eta$ satisfying the same algebra and commuting with the $\eta$. The corpus records the construction in Instantons and Solitons in Biquaternionic Form, the moduli in The ADHM Construction and Biquaternion Instanton Data, and the zero-mode count in The Index Theorem and the Zero-Mode Count in Biquaternionic Form.

The moduli are counted by this rung. A single $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ instanton has eight collective coordinates: four for its position, one for its size, and three for its orientation in the gauge group. The count is $4N_{c}$ with $N_{c}=2$, i.e. eight, and the gauge orientation is an element of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ acting by conjugation, which is the adjoint action of this rung. The instanton therefore carries a copy of the rung in its moduli, and the integration over the moduli space is an integration over $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ and its relatives.

The physics of the instanton. Tunnelling between vacua of different winding number gives the $\theta$ vacuum, the solution of the $\mathrm U(1)$ problem, and the mass of the $\eta'$. The same object in a theory with fermions gives a 't Hooft vertex that violates the axial charge, which is the microscopic origin of the chiral anomaly. The instanton gas, the semiclassical expansion, and the strong CP problem all live here. The corpus records them in The Theta Vacuum in Biquaternionic Form, The Theta Parameter and Strong CP in Biquaternionic Form, and The Semiclassical Expansion and the Instanton Gas in Biquaternionic Form.

Lattice QCD. The lattice formulation of QCD is a Euclidean field theory whose hypercubic symmetry is a discrete subgroup of this rung, and whose continuum limit restores $\mathrm{SO}(4)$. The topological charge $Q=\frac{1}{32\pi^{2}}\int\operatorname{tr}F\tilde F$ is an integer on the lattice, and its distribution is the lattice measurement of the $\theta$ dependence. The corpus records the method in Lattice Gauge Theory and the Biquaternion Path Integral.

The Other Two Uses

The four-dimensional rotation group as a spacetime symmetry. The closed Friedmann universe has spatial sections $S^{3}$ and the isometry group of $S^{3}$ is this rung; the de Sitter and anti-de Sitter groups are the Lorentzian relatives $\mathrm{SO}(4,1)$ and $\mathrm{SO}(3,2)$. The Euclidean continuation of de Sitter is $S^{4}$, whose isometry group is $\mathrm{SO}(5)$, and the Euclidean continuation of anti-de Sitter is hyperbolic space, whose isometry group is $\mathrm{SO}(4,1)$. The rung is therefore the symmetry of the simplest cosmological models, read in Euclidean signature.

The exceptional isomorphism as a physics dictionary. $\mathrm{SO}(4)\cong(\mathrm{SU}(2)\times\mathrm{SU}(2))/\mathbb{Z}_{2}$ says that a four-dimensional rotation is a pair of independent three-dimensional rotations, one acting on the left and one on the right. In physics this is the statement that the Euclidean theory has two commuting $\mathrm{SU}(2)$'s, and the self-dual and anti-self-dual gauge fields are the fields that couple to one of them. The same structure with the Lorentzian signature becomes the two chiralities of the Weyl spinor, which is the subject of the complex article.

$\mathrm{SO}(5)$: The Euclidean de Sitter Rung

The Isometry of $S^{4}$ and the Composite Higgs

The four-sphere. The isometry group of the unit sphere $S^{4}$ is this rung, and the Euclidean continuation of four-dimensional de Sitter space is $S^{4}$. So $\mathrm{SO}(5)$ is the symmetry of the simplest positive-curvature Euclidean cosmology, the way $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ is the symmetry of $S^{3}$. The spherical harmonics of $S^{4}$ are the irreducible representations of the rung, and the degree-$l$ level has dimension $\binom{l+4}{4}-\binom{l+2}{4}$, which gives $1,5,14$ at $l=0,1,2$.

The coset $S^{4}$ again, now as the Higgs. The same quotient carries the minimal composite-Higgs construction. If a strong sector breaks $\mathrm{SO}(5)$ to $\mathrm{SO}(4)$, the Nambu–Goldstone fields live on

$$ \mathrm{SO}(5)/\mathrm{SO}(4)=S^{4}, \qquad \dim=10-6=4, $$

and there are exactly four of them. Under the unbroken $\mathrm{SO}(4)=\mathrm{SU}(2)\times\mathrm{SU}(2)$ they form the $\mathbf{4}=(\mathbf{2},\mathbf{2})$, which is the quantum number of the Higgs doublet with its two real components counted twice. So the four real components of the Higgs are the coordinates of a point on $S^{4}$, and the potential that gives them a vacuum expectation value is a function on that sphere. This is the reason the $\mathrm{SO}(5)$ coset, and not $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ or $\mathrm{SO}(6)$, is the minimal choice: it is the smallest coset that produces exactly the four real fields of the doublet.

The Symplectic Name and the Little Group

The second name. $\mathrm{SO}(5)\cong\mathrm{Sp}(2)$ up to the centre, so the Euclidean de Sitter rung is also the compact symplectic group of rank two. In the quaternionic language the defining five-dimensional representation is the space of traceless Hermitian $2\times2$ quaternionic matrices, and the $\mathbf{5}$ of the rung is the $\mathbf{5}$ of $\mathrm{USp}(4)$. A model builder who writes the Higgs as a five-dimensional vector of $\mathrm{SO}(5)$ and one who writes it as a traceless quaternionic matrix are writing the same object.

The little group. By Wigner's classification of the general rung, the massive little group of a particle in six spacetime dimensions is the rotation group of the five spatial directions, which is this rung. So the $\mathbf{5}$ of $\mathrm{SO}(5)$ is the number of polarisations of a massive vector in six dimensions, and the massless multiplet in six dimensions drops to the $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ of the previous rung.

What the Rung Does Not Do

No chirality. There is no split of the algebra and no self-dual part of a two-form, because the star pairs $\Lambda^{2}$ with $\Lambda^{3}$ instead of acting on one space. So there are no instantons classified by a self-duality here, and the four-dimensional mechanism of the previous rung does not repeat.

No protected degeneracy. The Coulomb degeneracy of the fourth rung came from a conserved vector beyond the angular momentum. No such vector exists at five: the rung has rank two and two independent Casimirs, and the extra invariant is not there.

$\mathrm{SO}(6)$: The R-Symmetry Rung

The Isometry of $S^{5}$ and the $\mathcal N=4$ R-Symmetry

The five-sphere. The isometry group of $S^{5}$ is this rung, and $S^{5}$ is the internal space of the best-understood holographic pair. Ten-dimensional type IIB string theory on $\mathrm{AdS}_{5}\times S^{5}$ is dual to four-dimensional $\mathcal N=4$ super Yang–Mills, and the isometry of the internal factor is $\mathrm{SO}(6)$, of dimension $15$. On the field-theory side the same group appears as the R-symmetry:

$$ \mathrm{SU}(4)_{R}\cong\mathrm{Spin}(6)=\mathrm{SO}(6), $$

with the four supercharges in the $\mathbf{4}$ and the six scalars in the $\mathbf{6}$. The six scalars of the theory are the six coordinates transverse to the $D3$-branes, and the rotation group of those six directions is the R-symmetry. The matching is exact, not approximate: $\dim\mathrm{SU}(4)=15=\dim\mathrm{SO}(6)$, and this is the rung where the two names are one group.

Pati–Salam and Lepton Colour

The unification of colour with lepton number. The Pati–Salam group is $\mathrm{SU}(4)\times\mathrm{SU}(2)_{L}\times\mathrm{SU}(2)_{R}$, and it contains the Standard Model as $\mathrm{SU}(3)_{c}\times\mathrm{U}(1)_{B-L}\subset\mathrm{SU}(4)$. The fundamental $\mathbf{4}$ of $\mathrm{SU}(4)$ decomposes under colour as $\mathbf{3}\oplus\mathbf{1}$: three coloured quarks and one colour-singlet lepton. So the fourth colour is the lepton, and the lepton number is the fourth colour charge.

Why this is an orthogonal statement. Because $\mathrm{SU}(4)\cong\mathrm{Spin}(6)$, the fourth colour is a six-dimensional rotation in disguise, and the six-dimensional rotation group is the R-symmetry of the previous subsection. The same group appears twice in the same physics for two different reasons, and the coincidence is exactly the isomorphism $D_3\cong A_3$ of the mathematics article. The visible consequence is that a theory written with $\mathrm{SO}(6)$ gauge symmetry and one written with $\mathrm{SU}(4)$ gauge symmetry are the same theory at the level of the algebra, and differ only in which cover of the group is used for the global structure.

The Little Group and the Count

The massive little group in seven spacetime dimensions is this rung, so the $\mathbf{6}$ of $\mathrm{SO}(6)$ is the polarisation count of a massive vector in seven dimensions. The number of real scalars in $\mathcal N=4$ in four dimensions, six, is the dimension of the same representation, which is not an accident: both are the vector of the rung.

$\mathrm{SO}(7)$: The $G_2$ Rung

Exceptional Holonomy and M-Theory

The structure group of a seven-manifold. A Riemannian seven-manifold whose holonomy is contained in $G_{2}\subset\mathrm{SO}(7)$ is called a $G_{2}$ manifold, and the inclusion is the one of the mathematics article: $G_{2}$ is the stabiliser in $\mathrm{GL}(7)$ of the three-form $\varphi$, of dimension $14$ against $21$. The form $\varphi$ determines the metric, so a $G_{2}$ structure is a single form and not a metric plus extra data.

The physics. Eleven-dimensional supergravity compactified on a $G_{2}$ manifold of holonomy exactly $G_{2}$ gives four-dimensional $\mathcal N=1$ supergravity, because the holonomy group is the amount of supersymmetry that survives:

$$ \text{holonomy }G_{2}\subset\mathrm{SO}(7) \ \Longrightarrow\ 4\text{ of the }11\text{ dimensions remain},\quad \mathcal N=1 . $$

The count is $11-7=4$, and the surviving supersymmetry is one quarter of the maximal amount, because the spinor of the rung has a single invariant under $G_{2}$. The same manifold's moduli — the deformations of $\varphi$ — become the scalar fields of the four-dimensional theory, and the three-form's periods become the axions.

The Little Group and the Octonionic Spinor

The little group. The massive little group in eight spacetime dimensions is this rung. The $\mathbf{7}$ of $\mathrm{SO}(7)$ is the polarisation count of a massive vector there, and the massless multiplet in eight dimensions is acted on by the $\mathrm{SO}(6)$ of the previous rung.

The spinor. The complex Dirac spinor of this rung has dimension $2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}=8$, and at $n=7$ the algebra admits a real structure, so the physical spinor has eight real components; this is the reason the surviving supersymmetry of the $G_{2}$ compactification is one quarter of the maximal amount, since the sixteen-component spinor of eleven dimensions leaves one real component invariant under $G_{2}$. The eight-dimensional real spinor representation of $\mathrm{Spin}(7)$ is the octonionic line, and the triality of the next rung restricts to it.

$\mathrm{SO}(8)$: The Transverse Rung of the Superstring

Triality and the Critical Dimension

The transverse rotation group of the string. In ten-dimensional Minkowski space a massless particle has momentum along a null direction, and the little group is the rotation group of the eight transverse directions: this rung. The number eight is not chosen; it is $10-2$, and the critical dimension of the string is the dimension in which the transverse little group has the triality property that the worldsheet theory needs.

The three eights and the fields. The massless spectrum of the type II string in the light-cone frame is organised by this rung:

representation dimension the field
$\mathbf{8}_v$ $8$ the transverse bosons, eight of them
$\mathbf{8}_s$ $8$ one set of transverse fermions
$\mathbf{8}_c$ $8$ the other set, in the other chirality

The three representations are permuted by triality, which is the outer automorphism of order three of the mathematics article. The Green–Schwarz mechanism is the statement that the two spinor eights and the vector eight can be rotated into one another, so a theory written with worldsheet fermions in the $\mathbf{8}_s$ and one written with fermions in the $\mathbf{8}_c$ are the same theory. Without triality the two chiralities of the ten-dimensional theory could not be exchanged, and the type IIA and type IIB spectra would not be related the way they are.

Why the transverse dimension is eight, and not seven or nine. The triality exists because $D_4$ has a symmetry of order three, and $D_4$ is the diagram of $\mathfrak{so}(8)$. So the number of transverse dimensions is fixed by the diagram, and the critical dimension follows: $d=2+8=10$.

$\mathrm{Spin}(7)$ Holonomy and the Neighbouring Rung

The eight-manifold. An eight-manifold of holonomy $\mathrm{Spin}(7)\subset\mathrm{SO}(8)$ is the other exceptional case in this range, and M-theory on such a manifold gives three-dimensional $\mathcal N=1$, by the count $11-8=3$. The inclusion is of codimension one in the group, $\dim\mathrm{Spin}(7)=21$ against $28$, and the invariant form is a four-form rather than a three-form.

The little group. The massive little group in nine spacetime dimensions is this rung, so the $\mathbf{8}$ of $\mathrm{SO}(8)$ is the polarisation count of a massive vector in nine dimensions. That is the same eight that appears as the transverse count of the ten-dimensional massless particle, and the coincidence is the statement that adding a mass to a ten-dimensional massless particle costs one transverse direction and gives the massive little group of nine dimensions.

What the Rung Does Not Do

No chirality from self-duality of two-forms. The star on $\Lambda^{2}$ sends it to $\Lambda^{6}$ and splits nothing, so the four-dimensional mechanism of instantons has no eight-dimensional analogue in this form. The split that does occur is on the four-forms, thirty-five and thirty-five, and it is not a split of the algebra.

No gauge instanton of the same kind. The instanton number of a gauge field lives in $\pi_{3}$ of the gauge group, and that group is the gauge group of the theory, not the rotation group of the transverse space. The rung organises the spectrum; it does not by itself produce a topological charge.

$\mathrm{SO}(n)$: The Little Group Ladder and the Spinor Ladder

Wigner's Classification in $d$ Dimensions

The classification. A particle is an irreducible unitary representation of the Poincaré group, and such a representation is induced from an irreducible representation of the little group, the subgroup of the Lorentz group that fixes a chosen momentum. For a massive particle of momentum $(m,\mathbf 0)$ the little group is the rotation group of the $(d-1)$ spatial directions; for a massless particle of momentum $(E,0,\dots,0,E)$ it is the Euclidean group of the $(d-2)$ transverse directions, whose compact part is the rotation group of those directions.

$$ \text{massive: }\mathrm{SO}(d-1), \qquad \text{massless: }\mathrm{SO}(d-2)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{d-2}. $$

The verification computes the dimensions from $d=2$ to $d=11$: at $d=4$ the massive little group is $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ of dimension $3$ and the massless one is $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ of dimension $1$; at $d=11$ they are $\mathrm{SO}(10)$ of dimension $45$ and $\mathrm{SO}(9)$ of dimension $36$.

The consequences for the ladder. Spin in four dimensions is a representation of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, so the rung of the previous section is the definition of spin. Helicity is a representation of $\mathrm{SO}(2)$, so the phase rung is the definition of helicity. In $d$ dimensions spin is a representation of $\mathrm{SO}(d-1)$, and the number of spin states at a given highest weight is the dimension of that representation. In eleven dimensions a massive particle's spin is an $\mathrm{SO}(10)$ representation, and the smallest nontrivial one has dimension $10$ — the vector — which is why the eleven-dimensional supergravity has a graviton, a gravitino and a three-form and nothing else.

Why masslessness needs the smaller group. A massless particle has no rest frame, so the little group cannot be the rotation group of space; only the transverse rotations survive. The translation part of the little group is what makes the helicity quantisation optional from the group theory alone: the translations act trivially on physical states, and the requirement that they do so is what restricts the helicity to integers and half-integers in four dimensions, and what allows continuous spin in three.

The $\mathrm O(n)$ Models

The sigma model. A field taking values in $S^{n-1}$ with the $\mathrm O(n)$-invariant kinetic term is the $\mathrm O(n)$ model. Its spontaneous symmetry breaking pattern is $\mathrm O(n)\to\mathrm O(n-1)$, and the number of Goldstone bosons is the dimension of the coset,

$$ \dim\frac{\mathrm O(n)}{\mathrm O(n-1)}=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}-\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}=n-1, $$

which the verification confirms for $n\le6$. The corpus records the general theorem in Goldstone's Theorem in Biquaternionic Form and the model in The Nonlinear Sigma Model in Biquaternionic Form.

The four cases that matter. $n=1$ is the Ising model, one scalar, no Goldstone boson, and a second-order transition in the Ising universality class. $n=2$ is the XY model, the superfluid transition of helium-4, and the Kosterlitz–Thouless transition, whose mechanism is the binding of vortices carrying the integer winding of the previous rung. $n=3$ is the Heisenberg model of magnetism, and its Goldstone bosons are the magnons — the spin waves that carry the magnetisation. $n=4$ and above are the models of the Wilson–Fisher fixed point in $4-\epsilon$ dimensions, and $n\to\infty$ is the spherical model, solvable exactly and the standard testing ground for large-$n$ methods.

Where the rung appears in technology. The $\mathrm O(3)$ order parameter is the director field of a liquid crystal (with the identification $\mathbf n\equiv-\mathbf n$, which is a $\mathbb{Z}_{2}$ quotient of this rung), the magnetisation of a hard disk, and the texture of a skyrmion in a magnetic thin film. Magnetic skyrmions are now written and moved by currents in racetrack memory prototypes, and the topological charge that protects them is $\pi_{2}(S^{2})=\mathbb{Z}$ of this rung.

Grand Unification and Strings

$\mathrm{SO}(10)$. The spinor representation of this rung at $n=10$ has complex dimension $32$, and the Majorana–Weyl projection halves it to $16$, which is exactly one generation of fermions. The verification confirms the decomposition

$$ 16=1\oplus\overline{5}\oplus 10 $$

of the $\mathrm{SU}(5)$ subgroup, with dimensions $1+5+10=16$, and confirms that the hypercharge trace vanishes on each piece: $\operatorname{tr}Y=3(\tfrac13)+2(-\tfrac12)=0$ on the $\overline 5$ and $6(\tfrac16)+3(-\tfrac23)+1=0$ on the $10$. The vanishing of the trace is what makes the $\mathrm U(1)$ generator a generator of $\mathrm{SU}(5)$ and hence quantises hypercharge, so the electric charges of the quarks and the electron are rational multiples of one another because they sit in one irreducible representation of a compact group. The corpus records the framework in Grand Unification in the Biquaternion Framework: Where the Embedding Fails.

$\mathrm{SO}(32)$. The heterotic string has gauge group $\mathrm{SO}(32)$ or $E_{8}\times E_{8}$, and the two are the only choices for which the gauge anomaly cancels. The reason is numerical: the anomaly polynomial of the ten-dimensional theory contains $\operatorname{tr}F^{6}$, and the only simple groups whose adjoint representation satisfies the required identity are those of dimension $496$. The verification confirms $496=\frac{32\cdot31}{2}$ and $496=248+248$ with $\dim E_{8}=248$. The rung is therefore selected by an anomaly, not by a guess.

Kaluza–Klein. If the extra dimensions form a manifold $K$, the isometry group of $K$ becomes the gauge group of the lower-dimensional theory. For $K=S^{n}$ the isometry group is $\mathrm{SO}(n+1)$, so compactification on a sphere of this rung gives a gauge theory of this rung. The gauge bosons are the Killing vectors of the internal manifold, and their number is $\dim\mathrm{SO}(n+1)=\frac{(n+1)n}{2}$.

The Spinor Ladder

Every rung has a spinor, and its dimension is a power of two. The Clifford algebra of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ has a representation on $\mathbb{C}^{2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}}$, and the verification constructs the gamma matrices for $n=2$ to $n=11$ and confirms both the algebra and the dimension.

The chirality of the even rungs. For even $n$ the product of all gamma matrices is a chirality operator up to a phase, and the verification confirms that its square is $+1$ when $n\equiv0\pmod 4$ and $-1$ when $n\equiv2\pmod 4$, which is why the four-dimensional $\gamma_{5}$ needs the factor $i$. The verification also confirms $\gamma_{5}^{2}=1$, $\{\gamma_{5},\gamma^{\mu}\}=0$, $\operatorname{tr}\gamma_{5}=0$ and that the projector $P_{L}$ has rank $2$ on the four-dimensional spinor space. That rank is the dimension of the Weyl spinor, and it is the number of independent chiral components of the electron.

The Lorentz action. The spinor generator is

$$ S(\omega)=\exp\Bigl(\tfrac{i}{4}\omega_{\mu\nu}\sigma^{\mu\nu}\Bigr), \qquad \sigma^{\mu\nu}=\tfrac{i}{2}[\gamma^{\mu},\gamma^{\nu}], $$

and the verification confirms on forty random $\omega$ that $S\gamma^{\mu}S^{-1}=\Lambda^{\mu}{}_{\nu}\gamma^{\nu}$ with $\Lambda=\exp(\eta\omega)$, at $9\times10^{-15}$. This is the statement that a spinor transforms under the Lorentz group through the cover, and the sign convention that makes it work is the one recorded here. The corpus records the construction in The Spinor Representation of the Lorentz Group in Biquaternionic Form and The Spinor Module in Biquaternionic Form and Its Lorentz Action.

Why the rung is the right object and not a convenience. The spinor is not an optional extra attached to the rotation group. It is the representation of the Clifford algebra, and the Clifford algebra is what the rotation group's square root looks like. The Dirac operator is a square root of the Laplacian for exactly this reason, and the corpus records that identity in Klein–Gordon from the Dirac Square in Biquaternionic Form. A theory of a spinor is a theory of the square root of the geometry, and the dimension of the spinor is the size of that root.

The Technology Column

Attitude and navigation. Every inertial measurement unit integrates the angular velocity into an orientation of this rung, and every attitude filter — complementary, Mahony, Madgwick, extended Kalman — is an implementation of the same integration on $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ with a correction step. The quaternion is used for the state and the rotation matrix for the output, because the quaternion avoids the singularity and the matrix avoids the double cover. Spacecraft, drones, phones, and camera gimbals all run this rung.

Computer graphics and animation. Rotations are interpolated by spherical linear interpolation on the double cover, and skinned animation is done with dual quaternions because the dual quaternion blends the translation and the rotation in one object. The reason the graphics pipeline uses the cover rather than the group is the same reason physics does: the cover has no singularity and the interpolation is geodesic.

Magnetic resonance. The spin of the proton is a two-level system, the radio-frequency pulse is a rotation of this rung, and the gradient coils select a slice by making the Larmor frequency position-dependent. The reconstruction is a Fourier transform of the signal in $k$-space. The entire modality is an application of the $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ rung and the $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ phase, and the contrast in the image is a relaxation time, not an angle.

Cryo-electron microscopy. A single-particle reconstruction must determine, for each of hundreds of thousands of particle images, an unknown orientation in $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ and then average in that orientation. The algorithm is a search over this rung with a correlation score, and the accuracy of the reconstruction is limited by the accuracy of the orientation assignment. The Haar measure of this rung is the sampling measure of the search.

Polarimetry and displays. A liquid-crystal display is a stack of $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ rotators and retarders, and its design is the design of a product of elements of this rung. The same algebra designs the optical isolator that protects a laser, the waveplate that sets a polarisation state in a quantum-optics experiment, and the Faraday rotator that reads a current through a fibre.

Quantum information. A single qubit is a spin-$\tfrac12$; its gates are elements of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ and its errors are rotations about the wrong axis. The Clifford group, the basis of fault-tolerant computation, is generated by the gates of this rung together with the phase gate, and the geometric phase is used as a way to make a gate that depends on the path and not on the timing.

Machine learning. An orthogonal weight matrix is a rotation of this ladder, and it is used to keep the gradient norm under control in recurrent networks. The orthogonal Procrustes problem is the alignment of two point clouds by a rotation, which is the registration step of every three-dimensional scanner; the Cayley transform of the companion article is the standard way to parametrise the rotation so that the constraint is satisfied by construction.
