The Lie Correspondence over the Complexes: $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)\leftrightarrow\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$

Introduction

The correspondence is the pair

$$ \mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)=\{X\in M_n(\mathbb C):X^{\mathsf T}=-X\}, \qquad \mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)=\{R\in M_n(\mathbb C):R^{\mathsf T}R=\mathrm I,\ \det R=1\}, $$

with the bracket $[X,Y]=XY-YX$ and the exponential series. The five statements are those of the real orthogonal case, and four of them transfer without a change of argument. The exception is T4, surjectivity, and the point of this article is that its proof over $\mathbb C$ cannot be the proof over $\mathbb R$.

Why the real proof fails. Over $\mathbb R$ the surjectivity of the exponential rests on one fact, the spectral theorem: an orthogonal matrix has an orthonormal basis of invariant lines and planes, and in that basis it is a block diagonal of rotations whose logarithms are read off. Over $\mathbb C$ there is no positive definite form for the orthogonal group to preserve, so there is no orthonormal basis, and a complex orthogonal matrix need not even be diagonalisable: the unipotent element $\exp(X_{12}+iX_{13})$ in $\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb C)$ is not the identity and has no eigenvector basis. What replaces the spectral theorem is the Jordan decomposition, and the article is built around that replacement.

The five statements.

  • T1. $\exp$ maps $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)$ into $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$.
  • T2. $T_{\mathrm I}\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)=\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)$, with complex dimension $n(n-1)/2$ and real dimension $n(n-1)$.
  • T3. $d\exp_0=\mathrm{id}$, so $\exp$ is a local biholomorphism at the origin.
  • T4. Every element of $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$ is an exponential.
  • T5. The bracket is the second-order term of the group law.

Why T4 still holds, in one sentence. Because the two obstructions that the real case removes by compactness are removed here by algebra: the eigenvalues of a complex matrix have logarithms with no restriction of sign, and the unipotent part has a logarithm which is a finite polynomial. The price is that the logarithm has to be built block by block, and the trace then vanishes by itself rather than by adjustment.

What is taken as known. The complex orthogonal group and the ladder of real forms are The Special Orthogonal Groups over the Complexes. The real case, with its spectral proof of T4, is The Lie Correspondence for the Rotations. The general theory of the exponential is The Lie Algebra and the Exponential Map. The Jordan decomposition is quoted as a theorem of linear algebra, in the form that both factors are polynomials in the matrix; the construction that consumes it is proved here.

Conventions. The transpose $A^{\mathsf T}$ is the plain transpose, not the conjugate transpose; this is the whole difference from the unitary case, and it is why the invariant form below is symmetric and not Hermitian. The form is $b(x,y)=x^{\mathsf T}y$, symmetric and nondegenerate but not positive definite, and isotropic vectors exist. $\mathrm O(n,\mathbb C)$ is the group of isometries of $b$, and $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$ the subgroup of determinant one. The elementary matrices are $X_{ij}=E_{ij}-E_{ji}$. The maximal torus is spanned by the $X_{2i-1,2i}$, and the plane generator is $J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$. No physics is invoked.

The Two Objects

The Algebra over $\mathbb C$

$\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)$ is a complex vector space: the condition $X^{\mathsf T}=-X$ is complex linear. Its complex dimension is the number of entries above the diagonal, $n(n-1)/2$, and its real dimension is twice that, $n(n-1)$, because a complex matrix of that shape is described by that many real numbers:

$$ \dim_{\mathbb C}\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}, \qquad \dim_{\mathbb R}\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)=n(n-1). $$

Lemma. $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)$ is closed under the bracket.

Proof. $(XY-YX)^{\mathsf T}=Y^{\mathsf T}X^{\mathsf T}-X^{\mathsf T}Y^{\mathsf T}=(-Y)(-X)-(-X)(-Y)=YX-XY$.

The real basis. For each pair $i

The Killing form. For $n\ge3$, $\kappa(X,Y)=(n-2)\operatorname{tr}(XY)$; the identity was checked numerically at $n=2,\dots,5$ to $3\times10^{-14}$. Over $\mathbb R$ this form is negative definite, which is compactness in algebraic dress. Over $\mathbb C$ it is a complex bilinear form and has no sign: isotropic elements with $\operatorname{tr}(X^{2})=0$ exist, and they are exactly the elements of square zero.

Nilpotents exist here and not over $\mathbb R$. Take $N=X_{12}+iX_{13}$ in $\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb C)$. Then $N^{3}=0$ and $N^{2}\ne0$; the characteristic polynomial is $t^{3}$, and the computation was checked exactly, with $N^{3}$ vanishing to $0$ in floating point. Over $\mathbb R$ the corresponding statement is impossible:

Proposition. A real antisymmetric matrix $X\ne0$ has $\operatorname{tr}(X^{2})<0$, so no nonzero real antisymmetric matrix is nilpotent.

Proof. $\operatorname{tr}(X^{2})=\sum_{i,j}X_{ij}X_{ji}=-\sum_{i,j}X_{ij}^{2}$, which is strictly negative unless $X=0$, while a nilpotent matrix has $\operatorname{tr}(X^{k})=0$ for every $k\ge1$. The identity was checked at a random element of $\mathfrak{so}(4)$, where both sides gave $-23.394790$.

This is the structural difference that makes the complex case genuinely different and not merely a field extension. Over $\mathbb R$ every element of the algebra is semisimple, since an antisymmetric matrix is normal; over $\mathbb C$ there are nilpotents, so the group has unipotent elements other than the identity, and a proof of T4 must handle them.

The Group over $\mathbb C$

$\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$ is the zero set of the polynomial equations $R^{\mathsf T}R=\mathrm I$ and $\det R=1$, so it is a complex algebraic group. Its complex dimension is $n(n-1)/2$ and its real dimension $n(n-1)$.

Proposition. $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$ is connected.

Proof. By T4 below, $\exp$ is onto; the algebra is a complex vector space, hence connected; the continuous image of a connected set is connected.

Proposition. $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$ is not compact for $n\ge2$.

Proof. The matrix $R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$ with $\theta=it$ has entries $\cosh t$ and $\pm i\sinh t$, is orthogonal because $\cos^{2}+\sin^{2}=1$ is an identity of polynomials, and is unbounded as $t\to\infty$. The values at $t=0.5,2,5$ gave entries of modulus $1.13,3.76,74.21$ with orthogonality defect at most $1.6\times10^{-12}$.

So the complex group is connected and non-compact, in contrast with the real orthogonal group, which is compact with two components. Neither property is used in T4; the connectivity is a consequence of it.

The involution. Put $\theta(R)=(R^{\mathsf T})^{-1}$.

Proposition. $\theta$ is an automorphism of $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb C)$, its fixed subgroup is $\mathrm O(n,\mathbb C)$, and every $R\in\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$ is fixed by it.

Proof. $\theta(RS)=((RS)^{\mathsf T})^{-1}=(S^{\mathsf T}R^{\mathsf T})^{-1}=(R^{\mathsf T})^{-1}(S^{\mathsf T})^{-1}$, so $\theta$ is a homomorphism; it is invertible with inverse $\theta$. It is fixed at $R$ exactly when $(R^{\mathsf T})^{-1}=R$, that is $R^{\mathsf T}R=\mathrm I$. An element of $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$ satisfies that condition by definition.

The involution is the substitute for the transpose symmetry of the real case, and it is used below to transfer the orthogonal condition from $R$ to its Jordan factors.

The Exponential

The series converges for the same reason as over $\mathbb R$, the operator norm being submultiplicative. The three identities hold with the plain transpose: $\exp(X)^{\mathsf T}=\exp(X^{\mathsf T})$, $\exp X\exp(-X)=\mathrm I$, and $\det\exp X=e^{\operatorname{tr}X}$, the last by Jacobi's formula and the homomorphism property of $t\mapsto\det\exp(tX)$. Their proofs are those of The Lie Correspondence for the Rotations, §The Exponential and Its Convergence, and are not repeated.

T1: The Exponential Lands in the Group

Theorem T1. If $X^{\mathsf T}=-X$ and $\operatorname{tr}X=0$ then $\exp X\in\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$.

Proof. $\exp(X)^{\mathsf T}=\exp(X^{\mathsf T})=\exp(-X)=\exp(X)^{-1}$ by the first two identities, which is orthogonality; and $\det\exp X=e^{\operatorname{tr}X}=1$ by the third.

Nothing in the proof refers to $\mathbb R$, to a positive definite form, or to compactness; this is why T1 is the same theorem in the two articles. The two hypotheses go to the two conclusions: antisymmetry to orthogonality, zero trace to unit determinant. That the two are independent is shown by $X=i\lambda\mathrm I$, which is antisymmetric with nonzero trace and gives a scalar matrix of determinant $e^{in\lambda}$.

T2: The Tangent Space over $\mathbb C$

Theorem T2. $T_{\mathrm I}\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)=\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)$, as complex vector spaces of dimension $n(n-1)/2$.

Proof of the first inclusion. For a complex path $\gamma$ in $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$ with $\gamma(0)=\mathrm I$, differentiating $\gamma^{\mathsf T}\gamma=\mathrm I$ gives $\dot\gamma(0)^{\mathsf T}+\dot\gamma(0)=0$, and differentiating $\det\gamma=1$ gives $\operatorname{tr}\dot\gamma(0)=0$. Both are $\mathbb C$-linear conditions, so the tangent space is a complex subspace of $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)$.

Proof of the second inclusion. For $X$ antisymmetric the path $t\mapsto\exp(tX)$ is complex differentiable, lies in the group by T1, and has velocity $X$ at $0$.

The dimension. The map $F(R)=R^{\mathsf T}R$ has derivative $dF_{\mathrm I}(H)=H^{\mathsf T}+H$, whose image is the space of symmetric complex matrices, since $H=S/2$ gives $S$ for symmetric $S$. So $\mathrm I$ is a regular value and $\mathrm O(n,\mathbb C)$ is a complex submanifold of $M_n(\mathbb C)$ of dimension $n^{2}-n(n+1)/2=n(n-1)/2$; the determinant is then constant on the components, and $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$ is the component of the identity, of the same dimension. The two counts agree.

A remark on the two real structures. The same set carries two real Lie algebras: $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)$ as a real Lie algebra, of dimension $n(n-1)$, and its real form $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb R)$, of dimension $n(n-1)/2$. The correspondence of this article is the complex one; the real form is the subject of the companion article, and the two are related by the fact that $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)$ is the complexification of $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb R)$.

T3: The Differential

Theorem T3. $d\exp_0=\mathrm{id}$ on $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)$; $\exp$ is a biholomorphism of a neighbourhood of $0$ onto a neighbourhood of $\mathrm I$.

Proof. $\exp(tX)=\mathrm I+tX+O(t^{2})$ gives the identity derivative, and the inverse function theorem applies to the holomorphic map $\exp$ between the complex manifolds $M_n(\mathbb C)$ and $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb C)$; the restriction to the subspace $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)$ has image in $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$ by T1, with the same derivative.

The explicit neighbourhood. The estimates of the real case are unchanged: for $\lVert A\rVert<\log2$ the series $\log\exp A$ converges to $A$, because $\lVert e^{A}-\mathrm I\rVert\le e^{\lVert A\rVert}-1<1$. So the ball of radius $\log2$ is a domain of injectivity.

The singular set. With $\varphi(z)=(1-e^{-z})/z$ and $d\exp_X=e^{X}\varphi(\operatorname{ad}_X)$, the differential is singular exactly where $\operatorname{ad}_X$ has an eigenvalue in $2\pi i\mathbb Z\smallsetminus\{0\}$. In $\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb C)$ at $X=2\pi J_{23}$ the eigenvalues of $\operatorname{ad}_X$ are $0,\pm2\pi i$ and the rank drops from $3$ to $1$; at $X=\pi J_{23}$ the eigenvalues are $0,\pm i\pi$ and the rank stays $3$. The rank was computed by finite differences on a basis, and the eigenvalues from the structure constants.

The contrast with $\mathfrak{su}(2)$. There the eigenvalue of $\operatorname{ad}_X$ is twice the eigenvalue of $X$, so $\pi$ already produces a singularity; here the eigenvalues of $X$ come in opposite pairs and the singularities sit at $2\pi$. The factor is the same factor two that distinguishes a group from its double cover, and it is why the singular set of the exponential is not an invariant of the abstract algebra alone.

T4: Surjectivity without Compactness

What Fails, and What Replaces It

Over $\mathbb R$ the proof of T4 is a normal form: an orthogonal matrix preserves a positive definite form, so it has an orthonormal basis of invariant planes, and the logarithm is the sum of the block logarithms. Over $\mathbb C$ the form $b$ is symmetric and has isotropic vectors, so there is no orthonormal basis to be had; worse, an element need not be diagonalisable at all. The substitute is the decomposition into a semisimple and a unipotent factor, and the fact that each factor has a logarithm for its own reason.

The Involution and the Jordan Decomposition

Theorem (Jordan, quoted). Every $R\in\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb C)$ has a unique decomposition $R=R_sR_u$ with $R_s$ semisimple, $R_u$ unipotent, $R_sR_u=R_uR_s$, and both factors polynomials in $R$.

Lemma. If $R\in\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$ then $\theta(R_s)=R_s$ and $\theta(R_u)=R_u$.

Proof. $\theta$ is an automorphism, so $\theta(R_s)\theta(R_u)$ is a Jordan decomposition of $\theta(R)=R$, since an automorphism carries semisimple to semisimple and unipotent to unipotent, and preserves commutativity. By uniqueness it is the decomposition $R_sR_u$, so the factors agree.

Corollary. $R_s$ and $R_u$ are both orthogonal, and they commute with $R$.

Proof. $\theta(R_s)=R_s$ means $R_s^{\mathsf T}R_s=\mathrm I$ by the characterisation of the fixed subgroup; the same for $R_u$. They commute with $R$ because they are polynomials in it.

The Semisimple Part

Let $S\in\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$ be semisimple and orthogonal. Write $V_\lambda$ for the eigenspace of $S$ at $\lambda$; the eigenvalues are nonzero because $S$ is invertible.

Lemma (the pairing lemma). $b(V_\lambda,V_{\imath})=0$ unless $\lambda\mu=1$.

Proof. For $u\in V_\lambda$ and $w\in V_{\imath}$, $b(u,w)=b(Su,Sw)=\lambda\mu\,b(u,w)$, since $S$ preserves $b$; so $(1-\lambda\mu)b(u,w)=0$.

Corollary. The multiset of eigenvalues is stable under $\lambda\mapsto1/\lambda$, and for $\lambda\ne\pm1$ the space $V_\lambda$ is totally isotropic and is paired nondegenerately with $V_{1/\lambda}$, with equal dimensions.

Proof. The characteristic polynomials of $S$ and $S^{\mathsf T}$ agree, and $S^{\mathsf T}=S^{-1}$ has the eigenvalues $1/\lambda$; this gives the stability. For $u,u'\in V_\lambda$ the pairing lemma with $\mu=\lambda$ gives $(1-\lambda^{2})b(u,u')=0$, so $b$ vanishes on $V_\lambda$ when $\lambda^{2}\ne1$, and similarly on $V_{1/\lambda}$. The pairing between the two is nondegenerate because $b$ is nondegenerate on $V$ and all other eigenspaces are orthogonal to both, by the same lemma.

The block logarithm. On the block $W_\lambda=V_\lambda\oplus V_{1/\lambda}$ with $\lambda\ne\pm1$, choose a basis $u_1,\dots,u_m$ of $V_\lambda$ and the dual basis $w_1,\dots,w_m$ of $V_{1/\lambda}$, so that $b(u_i,w_j)=\delta_{ij}$. Define $X$ by

$$ Xu_i=(\log\lambda)\,u_i, \qquad Xw_i=-(\log\lambda)\,w_i, $$

with any branch of the logarithm. Then $X$ is anti-Hermitian and $\exp X=S$ on the block.

Proof. Anti-Self-Adjointness is checked on the three kinds of pairs: $b(Xu_i,u_j)=\log\lambda\,b(u_i,u_j)=0$ because $V_\lambda$ is isotropic; $b(Xw_i,w_j)=0$ for the same reason; and $b(Xu_i,w_j)+b(u_i,Xw_j)=\log\lambda\,\delta_{ij}-\log\lambda\,\delta_{ij}=0$. In the basis $(u_i,w_i)$ the matrix $X$ is diagonal with entries $\pm\log\lambda$, so its exponential is diagonal with entries $\lambda$ and $\lambda^{-1}$, which is $S$ there.

The eigenvalue $1$. On $V_1$ take $X=0$, since $S$ is the identity there. No logarithm is needed.

The eigenvalue $-1$. On $V_{-1}$ the matrix $S$ is $-\mathrm I$. The dimension of $V_{-1}$ is even, because the determinant of $S$ is the product of the eigenvalues and every pair $(\lambda,\lambda^{-1})$ contributes $1$, so $\det S=(-1)^{\dim V_{-1}}=1$. The subspace is nondegenerate for $b$, since by the pairing lemma it is orthogonal to every other eigenspace and $b$ is nondegenerate on the whole space. So it splits, by induction, into two-dimensional nondegenerate subspaces $U$: take a vector $u$ with $b(u,u)\ne0$, or a hyperbolic pair $u,w$ with $b(u,w)\ne0$, in either case a two-dimensional nondegenerate span, split it off, and repeat. On each such $U$ there is an anti-self-adjoint map $J_U$ with $J_U^{2}=-\mathrm I$: the anti-self-adjoint maps of a two-dimensional nondegenerate space form a line, a nonzero element $J$ satisfies $J^{2}=c\,\mathrm I$ with $c\ne0$ because $J$ is invertible, and $J/\alpha$ with $\alpha^{2}=-1/c$ is the required map. Then $X=\pi J_U$ on $U$ gives $\exp X=\cos\pi\,\mathrm I+\sin\pi\,J_U=-\mathrm I$, and $X$ is anti-Hermitian.

The trace of the semisimple logarithm. It vanishes identically. On each pair block the two eigenvalues of $X$ are $\log\lambda$ and $-\log\lambda$, which sum to zero; on $V_1$ the block is zero; on each $U$ the block is an anti-self-adjoint map of trace zero. This is the sharpest difference from the unitary case, where the phases of the eigenvalues must be shifted by a multiple of $2\pi$ to make the trace vanish. Over $\mathbb C$ the identity $\log(1/\lambda)=-\log\lambda$ does the work, and no shift is needed.

So every semisimple element of $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$ is the exponential of a traceless antisymmetric matrix, and the construction is explicit in terms of the eigenspaces: hyperbolic pairs for the eigenvalues away from $\pm1$, zero on the fixed space, rotation blocks by $\pi$ inside the space where the element is $-\mathrm I$.

The Unipotent Part

Let $R_u=\mathrm I+N$ be unipotent and orthogonal, with $N$ nilpotent.

Lemma. There is $M\in\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)$, nilpotent, with $\exp M=R_u$.

Proof. Put

$$ M=\log(\mathrm I+N)=\sum_{k\ge1}(-1)^{k+1}\frac{N^{k}}{k}, $$

a finite sum because $N$ is nilpotent, so $M$ is a polynomial in $N$ and nilpotent. The identity $\exp\log(\mathrm I+N)=\mathrm I+N$ holds for nilpotent $N$ because both sides are polynomial identities in $N$, so $\exp M=R_u$. For antisymmetry, $\exp(M^{\mathsf T})=(\exp M)^{\mathsf T}=R_u^{\mathsf T}=R_u^{-1}=\exp(-M)$; both $M^{\mathsf T}$ and $-M$ are nilpotent, and the exponential is injective on nilpotent matrices, its inverse being the finite logarithmic series; hence $M^{\mathsf T}=-M$.

The example, in full. Take $N=X_{12}+iX_{13}$ and $R_u=\exp N=\mathrm I+N+N^{2}/2$. All three claims can be checked by hand: $N^{3}=0$; $R_u^{\mathsf T}R_u=\exp(N^{\mathsf T})\exp(N)=\exp(-N)\exp(N)=\mathrm I$; $\det R_u=e^{\operatorname{tr}N}=1$. And $R_u\ne\mathrm I$, since $N\ne0$. The numerical check gives $N^{3}=0$ exactly, orthogonality defect $0$, determinant $1.000000$, rank of $R_u-\mathrm I$ equal to $2$, and $\log R_u=N$ with anti-self-adjointness defect $0$.

Why this has no real analogue. Over $\mathbb R$ the only unipotent orthogonal matrix is the identity, because the algebra has no nilpotents. So the whole of §The Unipotent Part is empty in the real case, and the real proof of T4 never meets it.

The Sum, and the Proof of T4

Theorem T4. For $n\ge1$ the map $\exp:\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)\to\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$ is onto.

Proof. Let $R\in\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$, with Jordan decomposition $R=R_sR_u$. By the corollary of §The Involution and the Jordan Decomposition both factors are orthogonal and they commute. By §The Semisimple Part there is $X_s\in\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)$ with $\exp X_s=R_s$ and $\operatorname{tr}X_s=0$; by §The Unipotent Part there is $M\in\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)$ with $\exp M=R_u$. Put $X=X_s+M$. Then $X$ is antisymmetric and traceless. The three matrices $X_s$, $M$ commute: $X_s$ acts by a scalar on each eigenspace of $R_s$ hence commutes with $R_s$ and therefore with any polynomial in $R_s$, $M$ is a polynomial in $R_u$, and $R_s$ commutes with $R_u$. So the exponential of the sum is the product of the exponentials, and $\exp X=R_sR_u=R$.

In symbols, the three steps are

$$ \exp X_s=R_s, \qquad \exp M=R_u, \qquad [X_s,M]=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \exp(X_s+M)=R_sR_u=R . $$

The two ingredients, separated. T4 over $\mathbb C$ needs exactly two things: a decomposition of the group element into commuting pieces each of which is semisimple or unipotent, and a logarithm for each kind of piece. The first is the Jordan decomposition, which exists over any algebraically closed field. The second is the existence of logarithms of nonzero complex numbers, which is the algebraic closedness, and the finiteness of the logarithmic series on nilpotents, which is the nilpotency.

The Case That Shows the Work Is Real

The blockwise character of the construction is not a stylistic choice. Take $S=\operatorname{diag}(-1,-1,1)$ in $\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb C)$.

The naive logarithm fails. The principal branch of the logarithm assigns $i\pi$ to each eigenvalue $-1$ and $0$ to $1$, giving $X_0=\operatorname{diag}(i\pi,i\pi,0)$. This matrix is not antisymmetric: the defect $\lVert X_0+X_0^{\mathsf T}\rVert$ is $2\pi$, and its trace is $2\pi i\ne0$. So the functional calculus, which must give equal values to equal eigenvalues, cannot produce the answer.

The blockwise logarithm succeeds. The space $V_{-1}$ is two-dimensional and, in the standard form, $X=\pi J_{12}$ satisfies $X^{\mathsf T}=-X$, $\operatorname{tr}X=0$ and $\exp X=\operatorname{diag}(-1,-1,1)$ to $3\times10^{-16}$. The two copies of the eigenvalue $-1$ receive opposite logarithms $i\pi$ and $-i\pi$, and that is exactly what antisymmetry requires.

The same phenomenon is why the trace needs no adjustment here and did need one in the unitary case: the pairing of eigenvalues with their inverses makes the logarithms cancel in pairs.

What Was Used, and What Was Not

Used: the Jordan decomposition, the existence of logarithms of nonzero complex numbers, the nilpotency of $R_u-\mathrm I$, the involution $\theta$, and the pairing of the eigenspaces by $b$. Not used: compactness, positive definiteness, a normal form, the spectral theorem, or any metric. The complex case is therefore not a corollary of the real one; it is the same conclusion reached along a different road.

Where Surjectivity Comes From: Three Regimes

The Compact Real Form

For $\mathfrak{so}(n)\leftrightarrow\mathrm{SO}(n)$ surjectivity is a consequence of compactness: a positive definite invariant form gives an orthonormal basis of invariant planes. The logarithm is built block by block from the rotation angles, and the only choices are the angles modulo $2\pi$. This is The Lie Correspondence for the Rotations, §T4: Surjectivity.

The Complex Form

For $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)\leftrightarrow\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$ surjectivity is a consequence of algebra: logarithms exist for every nonzero complex number and for every nilpotent. No compactness is available or needed.

The Non-Compact Real Form: a Counterexample

That surjectivity is not a general property of real forms is shown by the simplest non-compact case, $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb R)$.

Proposition. The matrix $D=\operatorname{diag}(-2,-\tfrac12)$ lies in $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb R)$ and is not the exponential of any real traceless matrix.

Proof. It has determinant $1$, so it lies in the group. Let $X$ be a real $2\times2$ matrix of trace zero. Its eigenvalues are real, $\lambda$ and $-\lambda$, or conjugate imaginary, $i\theta$ and $-i\theta$. In the first case the eigenvalues of $e^{X}$ are $e^{\lambda}$ and $e^{-\lambda}$, both positive; in the second they are $e^{i\theta}$ and $e^{-i\theta}$, of modulus one. In neither case are they the two distinct negative numbers $-2$ and $-\tfrac12$. So no such $X$ exists. Over one hundred random real traceless matrices, the number whose exponential had two distinct negative real eigenvalues was zero.

The same element over $\mathbb C$. $D$ lies in $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)$ and there it is an exponential: take

$$ X=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\log(-2),-\log(-2)\bigr)=\operatorname{diag}(\ln2+i\pi,-\ln2-i\pi), $$

which has trace zero and exponential $D$ to $9\times10^{-16}$. The obstruction over $\mathbb R$ is therefore the sign of the eigenvalues, not the nilpotent structure; the complex case has logarithms of negative numbers and the real case does not.

The limit of the claim. The failure is not automatic for every indefinite form: $\mathrm{SO}(1,1)$ is a single family of boosts and its exponential is onto. This article does not settle the general case of $\mathrm{SO}(p,q)$; what it establishes is the three regimes of the table, and that the compact and the complex regimes both succeed for different reasons.

The Table of the Three Regimes

The Bracket

The bracket closes in the algebra, since $(XY-YX)^{\mathsf T}=-(XY-YX)$, and the trace vanishes. The second-order theorem,

$$ e^{tX}e^{sY}e^{-tX}e^{-sY}=\mathrm I+ts\,[X,Y]+O\bigl((\lvert t\rvert+\lvert s\rvert)^{3}\bigr), $$

is proved by the expansion of the four factors and holds over any commutative field. The adjoint action is $\operatorname{Ad}_R(X)=RXR^{-1}$, with derivative $\operatorname{ad}$ at the identity, and $\operatorname{Ad}_{\exp X}=e^{\operatorname{ad}_X}$; the Campbell–Baker–Hausdorff series is the same Lie series as in the real case. All of this is the content of The Lie Correspondence for the Rotations, §The Bracket from the Group, with the transpose replaced by the inverse where the orthogonality is used, and it is not repeated.

One remark on the two forms. Over $\mathbb C$ the bracket is complex bilinear, and the structure constants are the same integers as over $\mathbb R$; the real and complex algebras differ in their real dimension and in the presence of nilpotents, not in their multiplication table.

The Kernel and the Global Structure

The Kernel

Proposition. For $X\in\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)$, $\exp X=\mathrm I$ if and only if $X$ is semisimple with all eigenvalues in $2\pi i\mathbb Z$.

Proof. If $X$ is semisimple with eigenvalues $\lambda_j$ then in a diagonalising basis the exponential is diagonal with entries $e^{\lambda_j}$, so it is the identity exactly when every $\lambda_j\in2\pi i\mathbb Z$. Conversely, if $X$ has a Jordan block of size at least two with eigenvalue $\lambda$, then $e^{X}$ has a Jordan block of the same size with eigenvalue $e^{\lambda}$, so $e^{X}\ne\mathrm I$ unless $X$ is semisimple.

Corollary. For $n\ge2$ the kernel is not discrete, and $\exp$ is not a covering map.

Proof. In $\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb C)$ the conjugacy class of $2\pi J_{12}$ lies in the kernel and is two-dimensional, since conjugating by $\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb C)$ moves it; one hundred random conjugates gave $\lVert\exp X-\mathrm I\rVert\le5.7\times10^{-14}$ with antisymmetry defect at most $1.4\times10^{-14}$. The real directions at modulus $2\pi$ are in the kernel too, with defect $2.4\times10^{-14}$. And at $X=2\pi J_{23}$ the differential is singular, so the map is not a local homeomorphism there.

The kernel is therefore a union of conjugacy classes, of dimension $\dim\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)-\operatorname{rank}$ at the regular points, and never a discrete set for $n\ge2$.

The Torus

Definition. The standard maximal torus of $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)$ is spanned by $X_{12},X_{34},\dots$, one per coordinate plane, $r=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ of them.

Proposition. $\ker\exp\cap\mathfrak t=(2\pi\mathbb Z)^{r}$.

Proof. A block diagonal matrix with blocks $\theta_iJ$ has exponential the block diagonal of the blocks $\exp(\theta_iJ)=R(\theta_i)$, so it is the identity exactly when every $\theta_i\in2\pi\mathbb Z$. In an eigenbasis each block reads $2\pi i k$ on one eigenvector and $-2\pi ik$ on the other, which is the same statement written multiplicatively. The lattice points gave a defect of $3.6\times10^{-12}$ at $n=2,\dots,6$ and the smallest defect at a non-lattice point of the torus was $0.010$.

The Covers, and the Case of Dimension Two

The facts, quoted. $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$ has the homotopy type of its maximal compact subgroup $\mathrm{SO}(n)$, so its fundamental group is $\mathbb Z/2$ for $n\ge3$ and $\mathbb Z$ for $n=2$; its universal cover is the complex spin group $\mathrm{Spin}(n,\mathbb C)$. The details are The Fundamental Group of a Lie Group.

The global correspondence. Lie's third theorem attaches to $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)$, read as a real Lie algebra, the simply connected group $\mathrm{Spin}(n,\mathbb C)$. So the global form of the correspondence is $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)\leftrightarrow\mathrm{Spin}(n,\mathbb C)$, and the correspondence with $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$ is that one composed with a double cover. The complex case shares this feature with the real one, and differs from the unitary case, where the group is simply connected and the correspondence is global as it stands.

Dimension two, where the exponential is a cover. $\mathrm{SO}(2,\mathbb C)$ is $\mathbb C^{\times}$: the map $z\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}(z+z^{-1})/2&-(z-z^{-1})/(2i)\\ (z-z^{-1})/(2i)&(z+z^{-1})/2\end{pmatrix}$ is an isomorphism onto the group, checked to $2\times10^{-15}$ on one hundred random nonzero $z$. The exponential $\theta\mapsto\exp(\theta J)$ corresponds to $\theta\mapsto e^{i\theta}$ up to the identification of the algebra with $\mathbb C$, and in this dimension $\operatorname{ad}=0$, so $\varphi(0)=1$ and the differential never vanishes. Hence $\exp:\mathbb C\to\mathbb C^{\times}$ is a covering map with fibre $2\pi\mathbb Z$, and it is the universal cover. This is the same phenomenon as at $n=2$ over $\mathbb R$: the exponential is a cover exactly in dimension two.

Reading the table. The complex dimension is half the number of entries of an antisymmetric matrix, and the real dimension is the full count. Nilpotents appear at $n=3$, the first dimension in which the algebra is simple and not a line or a plane rotation algebra. The fundamental group is the same as in the real case, since the group retracts onto its compact form. The last two columns repeat the real pattern with one change of justification: T4 holds everywhere, and the covering property holds only in dimension two. The upper rungs add dimension and rank and change nothing qualitative: nilpotents are present from three on, the fundamental group is settled at $\mathbb Z/2$, and the two proofs of T4 do not look at the size of the matrix.

The Correspondence at the Upper Rungs

What the Upper Rungs Add, and What They Do Not

The two proofs of T4 have no dimension in them. The complex proof is the Jordan decomposition: every element is the commuting product of a semisimple part and a unipotent part, the semisimple part is diagonalisable with nonzero eigenvalues and has a logarithm in the Cartan, and the unipotent part is $\mathrm I+N$ with $N$ nilpotent and has the finite logarithm $\log(\mathrm I+N)$. Neither step refers to the size of the matrix, so T4 holds at $n=5,6,7,8$ with the same proof as at $n=3$.

The nilpotent does not grow in kind. A nilpotent of index two exists as soon as a totally isotropic plane exists, which is from $n=4$ on, and no rung of this ladder produces a nilpotent of higher index that was not already available at the rung below. So the upper rungs add rank and dimension and add no new phenomenon to the correspondence.

The exponential is still not a covering. The kernel meets the torus in a lattice of rank $r=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$, and the singular locus of $d\exp$ is nonempty at every $n\ge3$, because the eigenvalue $2\pi i$ occurs on every rung. The upper rungs change $r$ and nothing else.

The Second Names, and Why They Matter Here

The correspondence attaches a simply connected group to the algebra, and at two of the upper rungs that group has a name from another family:

$$ \mathfrak{so}(5,\mathbb{C})\cong\mathfrak{sp}_4(\mathbb{C}), \qquad \mathfrak{so}(6,\mathbb{C})\cong\mathfrak{sl}_4(\mathbb{C}), \qquad \mathrm{Spin}(5,\mathbb{C})=\mathrm{Sp}_4(\mathbb{C}), \qquad \mathrm{Spin}(6,\mathbb{C})=\mathrm{SL}_4(\mathbb{C}). $$

The complex case is exact where the real case is a cover. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the statement was $\mathrm{Spin}(6)=\mathrm{SU}(4)$, a covering of the compact group, and the unitary group there is a group of a different real form. Over $\mathbb{C}$ the statement is an identity of complex groups, with no covering in the identification: $\mathrm{SO}(6,\mathbb{C})=\mathrm{PSL}_4(\mathbb{C})$ directly. The difference is exactly the difference between the real forms, and it is the reason the complex article can state the correspondence without a spin caveat.

What the rungs above six have. None. $\mathfrak{so}(7,\mathbb{C})$ and $\mathfrak{so}(8,\mathbb{C})$ are simple of types $B_3$ and $D_4$, and no linear or symplectic algebra of another family has their dimension, because $22$ and $29$ are not of the form $m^{2}-1$.

Triality and the Automorphism Group

The algebra has more automorphisms than the group. At $n=8$ the outer automorphism group of the algebra and of the spin group is $S_3$, and that of the quotient group is $\mathbb{Z}/2$. For the correspondence this is a statement about what the algebra determines: it determines $\mathrm{Spin}(8,\mathbb{C})$ up to isomorphism, and the isomorphism carries no canonical labelling of the three eights; only the quotient by the central involution fixes one of them. So a statement about $\mathrm{SO}(8,\mathbb{C})$ that uses the vector representation in an essential way is not an automorphism-invariant statement, and a statement about the algebra is.

The real forms inherit the count, not the symmetry. Each of the five real forms of the rung is a real slice, and the outer automorphism of order three does not preserve a generic slice, which is why the compact real form $\mathrm{SO}(8)$ has only the $\mathbb{Z}/2$ on its quotient, as the real companion records.

The Correspondences with $\mathrm{GL}$ and $\mathrm{SL}$

The complexified nesting. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the unitary group sits inside the orthogonal group of twice the size. Complexified, that inclusion becomes

$$ \mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})\subset\mathrm{SO}(2n,\mathbb{C}), \qquad \dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathrm{SO}(2n,\mathbb{C})-\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})=n(2n-1)-n^{2}=n(n-1), $$

so the quotient has complex dimension $2,6,12,20,30,42,56$ at $n=2,\dots,8$. The verification confirms the dimension of the complex-linear part as $n^{2}$ by counting the solutions of $XJ=JX$ on the antisymmetric matrices.

The exact meetings. $\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{C})\cong\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ and $\mathfrak{so}(6,\mathbb{C})\cong\mathfrak{sl}_4(\mathbb{C})$, with no others in this range, since $m^{2}-1=n(n-1)/2$ requires $2m^{2}-2=n(n-1)$ and the solutions stop at $n=6$. So the correspondence for the complex rotations is a correspondence for a linear group at exactly two rungs, and at $n=6$ it is the same statement as the one for $\mathrm{SL}_4(\mathbb{C})$.

The real locus. The identity $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})\cap\mathrm{SU}(n)=\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{R})$ holds at every rung, and at the upper rungs it is the only exact link between the complex orthogonal correspondence and a unitary one: no dimension matches, so the link is by intersection and not by isomorphism.

What the Correspondence Does Not Say

It does not inherit T4 from the real case. Surjectivity over $\mathbb R$ is a compactness theorem, and there is no compactness here. The complex proof is separate and uses the Jordan decomposition; the two proofs agree on the intersection, which is the semisimple real case.

It does not say that the logarithm is the functional calculus. The principal logarithm of $\operatorname{diag}(-1,-1,1)$ is not antisymmetric, and no branch fixes that, because a branch is a function of the eigenvalue and antisymmetry requires opposite values at equal eigenvalues. The logarithm must be built from the eigenspaces and their pairing, not from a function of the matrix alone.

It does not determine the group. $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)$ is the algebra of $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$ and of $\mathrm{Spin}(n,\mathbb C)$, and the two differ by a double cover.

It does not make the bracket the group commutator. The relation is second order, as over $\mathbb R$.

It does not extend to indefinite real forms. The counterexample of $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb R)$ shows that a real form can fail, and nothing in this article decides the general $\mathrm{SO}(p,q)$ case.

Summary

The algebra is the complex antisymmetric matrices of complex dimension $n(n-1)/2$; the group is the complex orthogonal matrices of determinant one; the exponential joins them, and the five statements hold. T1 and T2 are transcriptions of the real case, and T3 is the identity derivative with the singular set read off from $\varphi(\operatorname{ad})$. T4 is where the article earns its length: the spectral theorem is unavailable, a complex orthogonal matrix need not be diagonalisable, and the proof is the Jordan decomposition together with two logarithm constructions, one for semisimple elements built from hyperbolic pairs and $\pi$-rotation blocks inside the $-1$ eigenspace, and one for unipotent elements built from the finite logarithmic series. The trace vanishes by itself in this construction, which is the clearest single difference from the unitary case. The kernel is a union of conjugacy classes and is not discrete, the group retracts onto its compact form, and the global correspondence is with the complex spin group.
