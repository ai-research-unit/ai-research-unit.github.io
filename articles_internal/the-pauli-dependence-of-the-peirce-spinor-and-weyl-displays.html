The Pauli Dependence of the Peirce, Spinor and Weyl Displays

Introduction

The physics corpus now writes its regular matrices in the axis-free display, $\mathrm{axf}$, and keeps the Peirce display, $\mathrm{pau}$, in the gamma sections and in the second display of Matrix Representations Conventions. That settles the label of the $4\times4$ arrays. It does not settle three constructions that start from the Pauli combination itself, and this note inventories them. Nothing here is a defect of the corpus; it is the list of what a later pass would have to touch.

The three constructions are the Peirce decomposition, the spinor module and the Weyl blocks. Each depends on the chosen root $\vec\Xi=i\vec e_z$, and a display that selects no direction, like the axis-free one, does not remove the dependence: it moves it from the array to the object.

The Three Dependencies

The Peirce decomposition. The idempotents are $\tilde\Pi_\pm=\tfrac12(e_t\pm i\vec e_z)$, and every Peirce component, every Peirce basis and every Peirce-frame array is built on them. The root is a chosen spatial direction, so the decomposition singles out the $z$ axis. The corpus says so where it matters — Matrix Representations Conventions, §The Peirce Frame, the Second Display — and the axis-free display of the same regular matrices carries the idempotents as the first and the fourth columns of $C_{\Pi}$, which are not the coordinate vectors of units. A reader who wants the idempotents still has to choose the direction; what he no longer has to do is accept it in the arrays of the sixteen cells.

The spinor module. The $2\times2$ image $\mathsf M_2$ is the Pauli map, $\mathsf M_2(\vec e_k)=-i\sigma_k$, and every statement about the spinor module $S\cong\mathbb C^2$, about a two-component spinor, about $M_2(\mathbb C)$ acting on columns, is a statement in that display. The corpus's own answer is structural and is stated in Matrix Representations Conventions, §The Clifford Structure and the Frame: the state is an element of $\mathbb B$, the left regular module is the Dirac spinor space, and no column and no minimal left ideal has to be chosen. The answer covers the equations; it does not cover the articles that print $M_2$ matrices, and those are the ones counted in the table below.

The Weyl blocks. The chiral projectors and the chiral blocks are the Peirce projectors and the Peirce blocks: $\gamma_5=\operatorname{diag}(-I_2,I_2)$ is a Peirce array, and the block decomposition $\Delta=S\oplus\bar S$ is the Peirce decomposition of the module. In the axis-free display the same chirality cell is a signed permutation times $i$ and the blocks are not square blocks of the array. A block statement is therefore always a Peirce statement, even when it is written in cells.

What a Change Would Touch

Three passes, in this order.

The Weyl blocks come first, because they are the cheapest: the operator whose Peirce array is $\operatorname{diag}(-I_2,I_2)$ has the axis-free array $C_{\Pi}\operatorname{diag}(-I_2,I_2)C_{\Pi}^{-1}$, and a block statement can be replaced by the corresponding statement about the two central projectors, which is display-free. Nothing in the physics changes when this is done; the arrays change.

The spinor module comes second, and it is the sharp one: the $2\times2$ image is the Pauli map by construction, so writing a spinor in the axis-free display means writing it as an element of $\mathbb B$ or as a column in the coefficient basis, and the two are different objects. The corpus already prefers the first, which is why the gamma sections can be the only place where the second appears.

The Peirce decomposition comes last, and it may not need a pass at all. The decomposition is a decomposition of the algebra by an idempotent, and an idempotent needs a direction. What can be removed is not the choice but its invisibility: every article that uses the decomposition should name the root, as Matrix Representations Conventions does in §The Peirce Frame, the Second Display.
