Possible General Definitions of the Spin of an Algebra

Introduction

The test of usefulness. The biquaternion sector has three observed spin values: $0$ for the scalar, $\tfrac12$ for the fermion, and $1$ for the vector. Since $\mathbb{B}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$, a definition is pertinent for the sector only if, applied to $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ alone, it produces exactly $$ \{0,\ \tfrac12,\ 1\}. $$ A definition that misses a value, adds a value, or gives a different value is non-pertinent for the sector, whatever its interest elsewhere. This article presents the four definitions that pass. The companion article, The Incomplete Definitions of the Spin of an Algebra, presents the methods that fail, with the reason for each, and one method that passes the test but is rejected as dubious, the Casimir list.

An algebra alone has a finite list of possible spins. The list is finite because every definition that uses only the algebra is bounded by its dimension. The list is not canonical: different definitions select different subsets of the same range. The four definitions below are different computations; they agree at $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ and diverge outside it.

What the list is not. It is not a single number and not a single formula. The top weight of a chosen carrier, the dimension of a carrier, the orbit area, and the top of a chain give different numbers for the same algebra. The article keeps them apart, states what data each one needs, and records where each one generalizes.

The corpus's approaches. The polar-operator articles read the coefficient of the half-angle in the exponential. The integer-spin article reads the top weight of the adjoint action on the material sector. The principal-pair articles read the top weight of the triple. The articles on the individual algebras read the presence or absence of a ladder or a triple. These are different readings, and they do not agree on every algebra. The four definitions below are the ones that agree on the biquaternion.

The cases. The real line $\mathbb{R}$; the complex numbers over $\mathbb{R}$ and over $\mathbb{C}$; the split-complex numbers $\mathbb{D}$; the dual numbers $\mathbb{D}'$; the reduced biquaternions $\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm r}\cong\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}$; the quaternions over $\mathbb{R}$ and over $\mathbb{C}$; the split quaternions $\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm s}\cong M_2(\mathbb{R})$; the biquaternions $\mathbb{B}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$; the split biquaternions $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb D}\cong\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H}$; and the matrix algebras $M_n(\mathbb{C})$ for $n=2,\ldots,6$. The field is stated in every table, because the dimension of $\mathbb{C}$ is $2$ over $\mathbb{R}$ and $1$ over $\mathbb{C}$, and the dimension of $\mathbb{H}$ is $4$ over $\mathbb{R}$ and $2$ over $\mathbb{C}$.

The finite range. Let $A$ be a finite-dimensional unital $k$-algebra and $d=\dim_k A$. The definitions in §§3–7 read only $A$, its modules of dimension at most $d$, and the complexification when required. There are finitely many isomorphism classes of such modules, and each definition returns a set determined by them. The spin values of the triple, the module list, the highest-weight list, and the orbit list lie in $$ \left\{0,\ \tfrac12,\ 1,\ \tfrac32,\ \ldots,\ d-1\right\}. $$ The bound is $d-1$: a carrier has dimension at most $d$, so a defining spin is at most $(d-1)/2$, and the adjoint top of a triple is at most $d-1$. A method that returns real values that are not half-integers is not a spin list. The Casimir list is such a method; it is not one of the four, and the companion article explains why.

What is excluded. A definition that quantifies over all representations of an external Lie algebra, or that uses an unnormalized continuous scale, can produce infinitely many values. The highest weight over all representations of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ is the standard example: every $s\ge0$ occurs. Such a definition is a definition of a representation, not of the algebra's own list. Those definitions are in the companion article.

The conventions are those of the corpus. The field is $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$ as stated. The indexing is $1$-based. Matrix notation is used only for the matrix algebras. No Clifford algebra is used. The biquaternion is $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{H}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$.

1. The Test and the Cases

The algebras. The complex numbers are $\mathbb{C}=\mathbb{R}[i]/(i^2+1)$. The split-complex numbers are $\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{R}[j]/(j^2-1)\cong\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$. The dual numbers are $\mathbb{D}'=\mathbb{R}[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$, with $\varepsilon$ nilpotent of index $2$. The reduced biquaternions are $\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm r}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}$. The quaternions are $\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{R}\{1,e_x,e_y,e_z\}$ with $e_x^2=e_y^2=e_z^2=-1$ and $e_xe_y=e_z$, cyclically. The split quaternions are $\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm s}$, the real algebra with generators $e_1^2=-1$, $e_2^2=e_3^2=+1$, anticommuting, and $e_1e_2=e_3$. The split biquaternions are $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb D}=\mathbb{D}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}\cong\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H}$. The matrix algebras are $M_n=M_n(\mathbb{C})$.

The commutative algebras. $\mathbb{C}$ over $\mathbb{R}$ and over $\mathbb{C}$ is a field: every nonzero element is invertible, the only idempotents are $0$ and $1$, and every bracket vanishes. $\mathbb{D}$ is not a field: it has the idempotents $e_\pm=\tfrac12(1\pm j)$ and the zero divisors $1\pm j$. The dual numbers $\mathbb{D}'$ have the nilpotent $\varepsilon$ and the maximal ideal $(\varepsilon)$, and only the idempotents $0,1$. The nilpotent does not give a triple: every element of $\mathbb{D}'$ has the single eigenvalue $a$ (its scalar part), so no $S$ has the spectrum $s,s-1,\ldots,-s$ with $s>0$. The reduced biquaternions $\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm r}$ have the two central idempotents $\Pi_\pm=\tfrac12(1\pm j)$ and two non-isomorphic simple modules $\mathbb{C}_\pm$. All of them are commutative, so every bracket vanishes and every element is central; none has a spin triple.

The quaternions. $\mathbb{H}$ over $\mathbb{R}$ is a division algebra. Its only nilpotent is $0$ and its only idempotents are $0$ and $1$. It has no spin triple in itself: for every $S\in\mathbb{H}$ the eigenvalues of $\operatorname{ad}_S$ are $0$ and $\pm2i\lvert s\rvert$, where $s$ is the imaginary part, and the value $1$ never occurs, so $[S,U]=U$ has no nonzero real solution. The triple exists in the complexification $\mathbb{B}$. The split biquaternions $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb D}\cong\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H}$ are a product of two division algebras, so they have no nilpotent and no spin triple either; their complexification is $\mathbb{B}\times\mathbb{B}$.

The matrix algebras. $M_n$ has the principal triple $$ S=\operatorname{diag}\left(\tfrac{n-1}{2},\tfrac{n-3}{2},\ldots,-\tfrac{n-1}{2}\right),\qquad U=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}E_{i,i+1},\qquad V=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}i(n-i)E_{i+1,i}. $$ The defining module $\mathbb{C}^n$ has weights $\tfrac{n-1}{2},\ldots,-\tfrac{n-1}{2}$. The adjoint module has weights the differences of those numbers, from $-(n-1)$ to $n-1$.

The biquaternion carriers. At $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ the three carriers are the centre $\mathbb{C}I$, the defining module $\mathbb{C}^2$, and the traceless part $\mathfrak{sl}_2$. Their dimensions are $1,2,3$. The observed set $0,\tfrac12,1$ is the set of their spins. Every definition below reads this family in a different way.

2. The Natural Carrier Family

The family. Let $A$ be a finite-dimensional unital algebra. The natural family is $$ \mathcal{F}(A)=\{W:\ W\ \text{is an irreducible constituent of }A\text{ as a left }A\text{-module}\}\ \cup\ \{W:\ W\ \text{is an irreducible constituent of }A\text{ under the adjoint action}\}. $$ When $k$ is not algebraically closed the family is taken after complexification.

Why this family. The left regular module and the adjoint module are the two modules that every unital algebra carries without extra structure, so the family is intrinsic. It is not the family of all simple modules: for $M_n$ that is only $\mathbb{C}^n$. It is not the family of all triples either: a triple quantifies over nilpotents and produces a chain that need not be a constituent of $A$ (see §3).

The small size. The family has at most six classes in the cases above. It has three classes for $\mathbb{D}$, $\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm r}$, $\mathbb{H}$ over $\mathbb{R}$ and over $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm s}$, $\mathbb{B}$, and $M_n$: one or two simple modules, and an adjoint that splits into the centre and the traceless part. It has two classes for $\mathbb{C}$ over $\mathbb{R}$. It has one class for $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$ over $\mathbb{C}$, and $\mathbb{D}'$, whose regular module has a unique simple constituent. It has six classes for $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb D}\cong\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H}$, because each factor contributes a simple module, a centre line, and a traceless part. A non-semisimple algebra, or a product with more simple modules, has a larger family; $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb D}$ is the only such case here.

At $M_2(\mathbb{C})$. The family is the centre $\mathbb{C}I$, the defining module $\mathbb{C}^2$, and the traceless part $\mathfrak{sl}_2$. These are exactly the three carriers of the biquaternion.

3. The Triple

Two gradings. The section records two gradings. The idempotent grading $S_{\mathrm{id}}$ gives the flat list $\{0,\tfrac12,1\}$ for every $n\ge2$. The chain grading $S$ of a nilpotent gives the triple list, and it grows with the length of the chain. The two are different elements; they agree on the idempotent's own two-level chain.

The idempotent grading. Let $e$ be an idempotent with $e\ne0$ and $e\ne1_A$, and put $f=1_A-e$. Define $$ S_{\mathrm{id}}=e-\tfrac12 1_A=\tfrac12(e-f). $$ This is the whole definition of the idempotent element: one idempotent and one subtraction. No nilpotent is chosen, and no formula is postulated.

The weights of $S_{\mathrm{id}}$. Since $e^2=e$, $$ S_{\mathrm{id}}^2=e^2-e+\tfrac14 1_A=\tfrac14 1_A, $$ so $S_{\mathrm{id}}$ is semisimple with spectrum contained in $\{\pm\tfrac12\}$; the spectrum is exactly $\{\pm\tfrac12\}$ because $e\ne0,1_A$. On the Peirce corners $A_{st}=e_sAe_t$, $$ \operatorname{ad}_{S_{\mathrm{id}}} x=\tfrac12(s-t)x,\qquad s,t\in\{+1,-1\}. $$ So $L_{S_{\mathrm{id}}}$ has weights $\pm\tfrac12$ on $Ae$, and $\operatorname{ad}_{S_{\mathrm{id}}}$ has weights $0,\pm1$ on the adjoint. The three carrier spins are obtained in one line.

No value comes from nowhere. The value $\tfrac12$ is an eigenvalue of $S_{\mathrm{id}}$; the value $1$ is the difference of two such eigenvalues; the value $0$ is the diagonal.

The idempotent list. It is $\{0,\tfrac12,1\}$ exactly when $A$ has a non-central idempotent, and it is independent of the choice of $e$. It is $\{0,\tfrac12\}$ when every nontrivial idempotent is central, and $\{0\}$ when there is none. The centre contributes $0$ always. The Peirce carrier $Ae$ contributes $\tfrac12$ for every nontrivial $e$; its lower level $-\tfrac12$ is present exactly when $fAe\ne0$. The adjoint contributes $1$ exactly when a mixed corner is nonzero, and its string is full exactly when both mixed corners are nonzero.

In $T_2$ only $A_{+-}$ is nonzero, so the value $1$ is a top without the full string $1,0,-1$; the triple list of $T_2$ is $\{0\}$. The idempotent list records tops; the triple below certifies fullness.

Computed weights for $M_n$. With $e=E_{11}$, $L_{S_{\mathrm{id}}}$ gives $-\tfrac12$ and $+\tfrac12$; $\operatorname{ad}_{S_{\mathrm{id}}}$ gives $-1$, $0$ and $1$, for every $n$. The list does not grow with $n$.

The companion count. Every $\nu\in fAe$ is square-zero and satisfies $[S_{\mathrm{id}},\nu]=-\nu$. The corner $fAe$ is the lowering space. Its companion count is the least number of generators over $D=eAe$, and for $M_N$ with an idempotent of rank $r$ it is $$ s=\left\lceil\frac{N-r}{r}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\frac Nr\right\rceil-1; $$ for a primitive idempotent, $s=N-1$. The corner has $r(N-r)$ parameters, so the count is not the dimension; the multiplicity of the weight $-\tfrac12$ on the carrier is $r(N-r)$. The count measures the elaboration of the nilpotent part; it does not raise the spin.

Two elements named $S$. The idempotent element $S_{\mathrm{id}}$ and the chain grading $S$ of the triple are different in general. They coincide on the idempotent's own two-level chain, where $S=\tfrac12(e-f)$; in general they are different elements. The idempotent list is the list of $S_{\mathrm{id}}$; the triple list is the list of all chain gradings. For $n\ge3$ the principal pair uses a longer chain and a different $S$.

The relations are consequences. The triple is the method whose list grows with $n$ through longer chains, and every value in it is realised by a chain in $A$. Its relations are therefore derived, not postulated. The input is a nilpotent $U$; $S$ is the grading of its chain; $V$ is forced. Nothing else is assumed.

The input. Let $U\in A$ be a nonzero nilpotent and let $x_0,\dots,x_{\ell-1}$ be a Jordan chain written from the top down, $$ Ux_0=0,\qquad Ux_k=x_{k-1}\quad(k=1,\dots,\ell-1), $$ so $x_0$ is the top and $x_{\ell-1}$ is the bottom. A nilpotent is intrinsic data: it exists in $M_n$, in $\mathbb{B}$ and in $\mathbb{D}'$, and it does not exist in the division algebras $\mathbb{H}$ and $\mathbb{C}$. The chain is the sequence of states that $U$ steps through.

The grading $S$. The chain is finite, so it has a top and a bottom. Label the levels as a spin: the top is $s$, the bottom is $-s$, and consecutive levels differ by one. With $\ell$ levels this forces $s=(\ell-1)/2$ and $$ Sx_k=(s-k)x_k. $$ The labels are the only symmetric unit-step labelling of a finite chain. The spectrum $s,s-1,\dots,-s$ is the level number, not an assumption.

The partner $V$. The operator $U$ steps one level up, toward $x_0$. A carrier also needs the step down, so let $$ Vx_k=c_kx_{k+1}\quad(k=0,\dots,\ell-2),\qquad Vx_{\ell-1}=0. $$ Then $$ [U,V]x_k=(c_k-c_{k-1})x_k,\qquad c_{-1}=0. $$ The requirement $[U,V]=2S$ gives $c_k-c_{k-1}=2(s-k)$. With $c_{-1}=0$ the sum is $$ c_k=(k+1)(\ell-1-k). $$ So $V$ is not extra data: it is the unique operator that closes with $S$.

Existence. The formula gives the only possible $V$; it gives an element of $A$ exactly when the shift is realised by multiplication in $A$. In $M_n$ and in $\mathbb{B}$ it is. In a commutative algebra it is not: $[U,V]=0$ for all $U,V$, so $S=0$ and there is no nonzero triple. This is why $\mathbb{D}'$ has a nilpotent and no triple.

The three relations. With these definitions, $$ [S,U]=U,\qquad [S,V]=-V,\qquad [U,V]=2S $$ hold, one line each. The first says $U$ raises the level by one, the second says $V$ lowers it by one, the third is the definition $S=\tfrac12[U,V]$. Equivalently, the input is the pair $(U,V)$ constructed above, with $S=\tfrac12[U,V]$; the chain of $U$ supplies the levels.

Why the factor $2$. The step of $S$ is one unit, so the step of $2S$ is two. The element $H=2S$ is the coroot normalisation of the frame-root articles. The spin is read from $S$, never from $H$.

Closure. The relations are the Chevalley relations of $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ with $H=2S$: $$ [H,U]=2U,\qquad [H,V]=-2V,\qquad [U,V]=H. $$ Jacobi holds on the span, so for a nonzero nilpotent whose shift is an element of $A$, the span is a copy of $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ in $A$. A triple is a copy of $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ with a chosen Cartan element, and the method asks whether the algebra has rank one.

The biquaternion. In $\mathbb{B}$ the two Peirce nilpotents $\vec\Upsilon_-\in\mathbb{B}_{+-}$ and $\vec\Upsilon_+\in\mathbb{B}_{-+}$ satisfy $$ \vec\Upsilon_-\vec\Upsilon_+=\tilde\Pi_+,\qquad \vec\Upsilon_+\vec\Upsilon_-=\tilde\Pi_-, $$ so $$ [\vec\Upsilon_-,\vec\Upsilon_+]=\tilde\Pi_+-\tilde\Pi_-=2S. $$ The triple is the frame. This is why the biquaternion passes without extra structure. In general, if $u\in eAf$ and $v\in fAe$ satisfy $uv=e$ and $vu=f$, the same computation gives $[u,v]=2S$; the biquaternion is the case where the companion corner is one-dimensional.

Why the list grows. In $M_n$ a nilpotent has index at most $n$, so $\ell$ runs from $2$ to $n$ and the spins run over $$ 0,\ \tfrac12,\ \dots,\ \frac{n-1}{2}; $$ the adjoint adds the integers $0,1,\dots,n-1$. The growth is the growth of possible chain lengths.

Definition (spin triple). A spin triple in $A$ is a triple $(S,U,V)$ satisfying the three relations. By the derivation above, such a triple is the same data as a nonzero nilpotent with a Jordan chain for which the shift is an element of $A$; the levels are then forced. The triple list is the set over all triples in $A$, with the spin of a carrier read as the top eigenvalue of $S$ on it.

The flat idempotent list and the growing triple list. The idempotent list is $\{0,\tfrac12,1\}$ for every $M_n$, $n\ge2$, and it should not be read as the triple list. The idempotent's companions are square-zero, so the companion chain on the column has length two and top $\tfrac12$; the adjoint string has length three and top $1$. Neither grows with $n$. The triple list quantifies over all triples, including nilpotents of index $\ell>2$, and it is the list that grows with $n$. The two lists agree only where the longest chain has length two, which is the $M_2$ case. The Peirce chain of The Incomplete Definitions of the Spin of an Algebra is a third object: it reads only the corners $e_iAe_{i+1}$ and gives $\{0,\tfrac12\}$ on $M_2(\mathbb{C})$, missing the adjoint value.

The matrix lists. For $M_n$ with the principal triple, the defining spin is $(n-1)/2$ and the adjoint top is $n-1$; the elementary list is $$ \left\{0,\ \frac{n-1}{2},\ n-1\right\}. $$ Over all triples, a nilpotent of index $\ell\le n$ gives the defining spin $(\ell-1)/2$ and the adjoint top $\ell-1$. The union is $$ \left\{0,\tfrac12,\ldots,\tfrac{n-1}{2}\right\}\cup\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}. $$ For $n=5$ this is $\{0,\tfrac12,1,\tfrac32,2,3,4\}$; the values $\tfrac52$ and $\tfrac72$ are absent because the defining carrier has at most five levels and the adjoint top is an integer.

The quaternion rows. $\mathbb{H}$ over $\mathbb{R}$ has no triple in itself. The triple exists in $\mathbb{B}$, where the carrier $\mathbb{H}$ (as a left module) has spin $\tfrac12$ and the adjoint has content $0,1$. The list $\{0,\tfrac12,1\}$ is a list of $\mathbb{B}$, not of $\mathbb{H}$ alone. The split biquaternions $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb D}\cong\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H}$ also have no triple, for the same reason: no nonzero nilpotent. Their complexification is $\mathbb{B}\times\mathbb{B}$.

What the definition does. The triple gives the closure of the ladder and the growing list. The idempotent element $S_{\mathrm{id}}$ gives the flat list $\{0,\tfrac12,1\}$ without any nilpotent; it is the grading of the Peirce corners, not the grading of a chain. On $M_n$ the triple gives the principal list $\{0,(n-1)/2,n-1\}$ and the adjoint content of the row. It is the definition used in the principal-pair articles and in the biquaternion articles. The triple list is not intrinsic to $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{D}$, $\mathbb{D}'$, $\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm r}$, $\mathbb{H}$, or $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb D}$: those algebras have no nonzero triple, so it collapses to $\{0\}$ (or to the list of the complexification). The idempotent list is finer there: it is $\{0,\tfrac12\}$ on $\mathbb{D}$, $\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm r}$ and $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb D}$, because those algebras have a nontrivial central idempotent but no nonzero mixed corner.

Verdict. The physical definition for $M_n$ and for $\mathbb{B}$. On $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ it gives $\{0,\tfrac12,1\}$, the experimental set. It is one of the four definitions that pass, and it is the one that generalizes to all $M_n$.

The $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ test is necessary for the biquaternion; it is not sufficient for a general definition. The companion article records the methods that fail even the necessary test.

9. Checks

The triple. With $S=\operatorname{diag}(\tfrac12,-\tfrac12)$, $U=E_{12}$, $V=E_{21}$, $$ [S,U]=U,\qquad [S,V]=-V,\qquad [U,V]=2S. $$ The defining module has weights $\pm\tfrac12$; the adjoint has weights $0,\pm1$; the centre has weight $0$.

The left multiplication. $L_S$ on $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ has eigenvalues $\pm\tfrac12$, each of multiplicity $2$. These are the weights of the defining module; its top weight is $\tfrac12$.

The adjoint. $\operatorname{ad}_S$ has eigenvalues $0$ of multiplicity $2$ and $\pm1$. These are the adjoint weights $0,\pm1$; the adjoint spin is $1$.

The orbit cut. The largest constituent has dimension $3$, so $2s+1\le3$ gives $s\le1$.

The quaternion check. $\operatorname{ad}_i$ has eigenvalues $0$ of multiplicity $2$ and $\pm2i$; the normalized generator $J_3=\tfrac12\operatorname{ad}_{ie_3}$ has $0$ of multiplicity $2$ and $\pm1$. This is the spin-$1$ content of the adjoint carrier in $\mathbb{B}$.

The agreements. On $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$ over $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{D}$, $\mathbb{D}'$, and $\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm r}$ all four give $\{0\}$. On $\mathbb{H}$ over $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm s}$, $\mathbb{B}$, and $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ all four give $\{0,\tfrac12,1\}$; this is the biquaternion list. $\mathbb{C}$ over $\mathbb{R}$ is the one commutative case where the module list separates: it gives $\{0,\tfrac12\}$ from the real dimension of $\mathbb{C}$, while the other three give $\{0\}$.

The scope. The rows are the finite-dimensional algebras over $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$ that Part VI treats as categories. The categories Complexoid, Split-Complexoid, Quaternioid, and Biquaternion Algebraic Structures are not separate algebras: they carry extra products, forms, and subspaces on $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{D}$, $\mathbb{H}$, and $\mathbb{B}$, which are already rows. Structural Comparison around Biquaternions compares the rows and adds no algebra. The Booleans, $\mathbb{N}$, and $\mathbb{Z}$ are not finite-dimensional algebras over a field, and $\mathbb{Q}$ is excluded by the convention that the base field is $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$; the four definitions do not apply to them.

Two real forms do not agree. On $\mathbb{H}$ over $\mathbb{R}$ and on $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb D}\cong\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H}$ the triple gives only $\{0\}$, because neither algebra has a nonzero nilpotent. The module list gives $\{0,1,\tfrac32\}$, because the simple module has real dimension $4$. The highest-weight and orbit lists give $\{0,\tfrac12,1\}$, because each semisimple factor is $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ and the carrier is its fundamental representation. A spin-$\tfrac12$ carrier can exist without a triple.

The reduced biquaternion. The row uses the complex base field of the corpus. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the two simple modules have real dimension $2$ and the module list becomes $\{0,\tfrac12\}$; the other three lists remain $\{0\}$, because the algebra is commutative and has no nilpotent. The reduced biquaternion has central idempotents but no nilpotent, so it has no triple and no spin-$\tfrac12$ value in either reading.

Above $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ the four diverge. At $M_3$ the module list reads the adjoint dimension $8$, the highest-weight list returns $1$ and $2$, and the orbit list returns every half-integer up to $\tfrac72$. The divergence grows with $n$: at $M_6$ the module list returns $17$, the highest-weight list returns $\tfrac52$ and $5$, and the orbit list returns every half-integer up to $17$. The triple list is finite in each case, with values up to $n-1$.

Two different obstructions. $\mathbb{D}'$ has a nonzero nilpotent but no semisimple element with distinct eigenvalues; $\mathbb{H}$ over $\mathbb{R}$ has a spin-$\tfrac12$ carrier but no nilpotent. Neither has a triple. A triple needs both a nilpotent and a semisimple grading element, and neither condition alone suffices.

The Casimir is not in the table. It passes the $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ test, but on $M_3(\mathbb{C})$ it returns $0.758$ and $1.303$. These are not half-integers, so they are not spins. The formula $s=(-1+\sqrt{1+4c})/2$ is an $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ formula; it is meaningful only when the semisimple part is $\mathfrak{sl}_2$. The Casimir is therefore a representation label, not a definition of the algebra's spin list. It is in the companion article, under Dubious Methods.

What the table selects. The $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ test is passed by all four because the natural family there is exactly the centre, the defining module, and the traceless part, of dimensions $1,2,3$. The test does not separate them, and it does not reject the Casimir either. The test is necessary, not sufficient. The separation is the behavior on $M_n$ for $n\ge3$ and on the compact real forms.

Summary

Four definitions pass the biquaternion test. They are the triple, the module list, the highest-weight list, and the orbit list with the cut. On $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ all four give $\{0,\tfrac12,1\}$, the experimental set.

They agree on the biquaternion and on its split and complex forms. They give $\{0,\tfrac12,1\}$ on $\mathbb{H}$ over $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm s}$, $\mathbb{B}$, and $M_2(\mathbb{C})$. They give $\{0\}$ on the commutative cases with one-dimensional simples, and $\{0\}$ from all methods except the module list on $\mathbb{C}$ over $\mathbb{R}$. On the compact real forms $\mathbb{H}$ and $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb D}$ the triple gives only $\{0\}$: a spin-$\tfrac12$ carrier exists, but no nilpotent, so no triple.

They diverge above $M_2(\mathbb{C})$. The triple and the highest-weight list generalize to the principal family of $M_n$. The module list gives the adjoint-dimension value. The orbit list is overcomplete. The $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ test selects the pertinent definitions; the behavior on $M_n$ selects the general ones.

The incomplete methods are elsewhere. The methods that fail the $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ test — dimension, ladder, Peirce, quiver, spectral, the adjoint alone, the half-angle, the unrestricted representation labels, the circle weight, and the ribbon twist — are in The Incomplete Definitions of the Spin of an Algebra. Each of them captures a part; none of them is a definition of the list. The Casimir list is the one dubious method: it passes the test and is rejected anyway, because $0.758$ is not a spin. It is in the same article, §13.
