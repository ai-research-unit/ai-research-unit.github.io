Pair Migration: The Two Equations in Place of the Glyph

Introduction

The verdict. Every article that used the pair operator is now written without it. Thirty-one files printed the glyph. Eleven use it as a shorthand for two equations that the definition supplies in one step. Nine use it where a determinant, a propagator or an index is computed. Five use it as an operator on one element. Five cite it and compute nothing. One prints the same letter for a different operator. None of the thirty-one needed it, and none writes it now.

Why the glyph went. The pair operator compresses four statements into one, and the four are the physics. The adjoint is the clearest case. $\mathbb{D}^{\dagger}=-\mathbb{D}$ holds for the massless operator and for no other: with the mass the operator reads $(\mathbb{D}-k_m)^{\dagger}=-\mathbb{D}-k_m$, which is neither Hermitian nor anti-Hermitian, and no constant multiple repairs it. The metric of the pair is never written, although the block adjoint uses one. The signature sits in the signs of the two members' spatial units, and $\mathbb{D}^2=\Box I$ reports one scalar for both. And the glyph names an $8\times8$ array and a $4\times4$ array, so the notation does not say whether an article counts one field or two.

What the two equations restore. The mass as the coupling of two named parts. The operator of each line, written on its line. The metric once, as the algebra's sesquilinear form. The sign of each member's spatial units. And the Hermiticity on the action, where the corpus already states it in the articles that need it.

What the article does not claim. It does not claim that the block array is wrong, and it does not claim that a coupled system can be inverted without a matrix. It claims that the matrix needs no glyph, and that every statement the corpus made with the glyph is available without it. The migration has since been carried out: the thirty-one articles and both conventions pages are rewritten, and this article records the migration, the warrant for each article, and what each file now says. The old text of the definition section is kept in the appendix, as a record and not as a usage.

The organisation. §What the Glyph Says fixes the four statements the glyph carries. §The Adjoint gives the arithmetic that the single-glyph writing cannot state. §The Metric and the Signature shows what the block structure stores. §The Numbers Are One-Slot Numbers reduces the determinant, the index and the propagator. §The Articles lists the thirty-one files with their links and their two-equation form. §What the Migration Costs names the four places that keep a matrix, and the section names that changed. §The State of the Corpus gives the count as it now stands.

What the Glyph Says

The four statements, and the two lines behind each. Every occurrence of the glyph in the corpus is one of four sentences.

  • The definition. $\mathbb{D}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}=(\tilde\nabla\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R},\ \tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L})$ on $\mathbb{B}\oplus\mathbb{B}$, with $\mathbb{D}^2=\Box I$.
  • The massive equation. $(\mathbb{D}-k_m)\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}=0$, with massless limit $\mathbb{D}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}=0$.
  • The symbol. The same map with each gradient replaced by its plane-wave value.
  • The numbers. The determinant, the Green matrix, the index and the trace that the three sentences above produce.

Each of the four is a sentence about two lines. The definition is the two chiral equations with the components gathered into one tuple. The massive equation is the two lines with $k_m$ moved to the left of each. The symbol is the two lines on a plane wave. And every number is computed from the two lines.

The pre-migration text already printed the two lines. Operator and Calculus Conventions stated the definition as a compression: "The two first-order equations of the series, $\tilde\nabla\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}=k_m\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}$ and $\tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}=k_m\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}$, are one operator on $\mathbb{B}\oplus\mathbb{B}$." The equations come first and the glyph is the abbreviation. The Proton in Biquaternionic Form prints both rows in one table, the two-equation row and the glyph row, as two readings of one equation. Supersymmetric Quantum Physics wrote "read as one operator the same content is the pair operator $\mathbb{D}$; read as two equations it is the pair printed above". All of these sentences are gone from the articles; the quotes are kept here and in the appendix as the record of what was replaced. The Chiral Condensate printed the linear chiral pair and named the two writings as two displays of one fact. The Spectral Triple printed the chiral pair as "the defining form" and the glyph as the second form.

So the migration is a deletion, not a rewrite. In the articles that print both, the second row goes and the first stays. In the articles that print only the glyph, the two lines are recovered from the definition in one step.

The Adjoint

The Arithmetic

The adjoint of one gradient. With respect to the sesquilinear form of the algebra the gradient satisfies

$$ \tilde\nabla^{\dagger}=-\tilde\nabla^{\natural} . $$

Supersymmetric Quantum Physics, §One sign, recorded once, derives it: the derivative contributes a minus, the units contribute $e_t^{*}=e_t$ and $\vec e_k^{*}=-\vec e_k$, and the two minus signs cancel on the spatial part.

The adjoint of the pair, block by block. The adjoint of a block array is the array of the adjoints of its blocks, transposed. Each block is a left multiplication, and the adjoint of $L_{\tilde A}$ is $L_{\tilde A^{\dagger}}$. With the two off-diagonal blocks exchanged and one minus on each,

$$ \mathbb{D}^{\dagger}=-\mathbb{D} . $$

The mass is not covered. Add the mass and the sign survives on one term and not the other:

$$ (\mathbb{D}-k_m)^{\dagger}=-\mathbb{D}-k_m . $$

No rescaling repairs it. A multiple $c(\mathbb{D}-k_m)$ is Hermitian when $\bar c(-\mathbb{D}-k_m)=c(\mathbb{D}-k_m)$, which requires $\bar c=-c$ and $\bar c=c$ at once, and $k_m\neq0$ forces $c=0$. The Hermitian operator of the massive theory does exist, and it is $i\mathbb{D}-k_m$: its adjoint is $-i\mathbb{D}^{\dagger}-k_m=i\mathbb{D}-k_m$. It is not a multiple of the corpus's operator: the nearest multiple, $i(\mathbb{D}-k_m)$, differs from it by the shift $(i-1)k_m$. The corpus writes the non-Hermitian one and repairs it by $i$, and the repair covers the massless case only.

The corpus sees the difficulty and works around it. Supersymmetric Quantum Physics states the adjoint of the freely massless operator, opens the paragraph with "The supercharge carries no mass", and re-attaches the mass afterwards as an eigenvalue, $\mathbb{D}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}=k_m\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}$.

What that costs. The physics is that the derivative term is anti-Hermitian and the mass term is Hermitian. The glyph cannot say it, because the two terms are inside one letter and the letter's adjoint is a single sign. The two-equation form says it on the two lines, where each term stands in its own place.

The repair by $i$, and its price. $i\mathbb{D}$ is Hermitian, since $(i\mathbb{D})^{\dagger}=-i\mathbb{D}^{\dagger}=i\mathbb{D}$. Its square is $-\Box$, not $\Box$, so the sign of the second-order operator flips. The corpus records the flip and calls it the price of Hermiticity: "Multiplying an anti-Hermitian operator by $i$ multiplies its square by $-1$, so the anticommutator of the repaired pair is $-\Box$ while the algebra's pairing is $+\Box$." The price exists because two Hermitian objects were bundled into one letter. Written as two lines, no repair is needed and no sign is paid.

The Metric That Is Never Written

The block adjoint chooses a form silently. The adjoint of a pair needs a form on $\mathbb{B}\oplus\mathbb{B}$, and $\mathbb{D}^{\dagger}=-\mathbb{D}$ uses the diagonal one, slot by slot. No article states that choice. The two-equation form needs one form, the algebra's sesquilinear form, applied twice, and it is stated once in Norm and Pairing Conventions.

On one element the form has to be named, and naming it exposes the defect. The Spectral Triple writes, of the single-element operator, that $i\tilde\nabla$ "is not self-adjoint for that form", that the operator is self-adjoint on the time direction and skew on the three spatial ones, and that "the defect is the Minkowski signature itself". That sentence is the physics. It cannot be written about $\mathbb{D}$ with the same force, because $\mathbb{D}$ is diagonal in the metric that matters and off-diagonal in the slots that do not.

The Metric and the Signature

The two members differ by the metric, and that is why there are two. In the coordinates $Q$, the gradient is $\tilde\nabla=\sum_\mu e_\mu\,\partial/\partial Q_\mu$ and its conjugate is the same sum with the three spatial units negated. The difference between the two members is one sign per spatial direction, and that sign vector is the metric of the frame. The pair therefore stores the metric in the relation between its two blocks.

The square erases the storage. $\mathbb{D}^2=\Box I$ reports one scalar for the pair, and the scalar is the same for a Euclidean frame and for a material frame; the two differ in the coordinate and not in the notation. An article that writes only the square cannot be read for its signature. An article that writes the two lines has the two signs on the page.

The sign of the square is indefinite, and the glyph does not carry it. On the material sector $\Box=\Delta-\partial^{2}/\partial {r'_{t}}^{2}$, whose spectrum is indefinite: a plane wave contributes $-\lvert\vec k\rvert^{2}+k_0^{2}$. So no statement of positivity is available for $\mathbb{D}^{2}$, and the repaired operator's square is its negative. The glyph fixes neither sign. The two lines carry the sign of each member in the coefficient of the time derivative, where the $i$ of the material sector is visible.

The Numbers Are One-Slot Numbers

The Determinant

The block identity. For blocks $A$, $B$ of a four-dimensional carrier and a scalar $m$,

$$ \det\begin{pmatrix}-m I&A\\ B&-m I\end{pmatrix}=\det\bigl(BA-m^{2}I\bigr). $$

Checked. On twenty random complex $4\times4$ pairs the two sides agree to a relative $4.5\times10^{-15}$. With $BA=p^{2}I$ the same identity gives the corpus's printed value, $\det(\mathbb{D}-m)=(p^{2}-m^{2})^{4}$, to a relative $1.3\times10^{-15}$.

The exponent is the dimension of one slot. The determinant of the pair is the determinant of the one-slot Klein–Gordon operator, not its square. That is why the exponent is four and not eight. The Fermionic Path Integral already prints the consequence, $\det(\mathbb{D}-k_m)=(p^{2}-m^{2}c^{2})^{4}=\det(\Box-k_m^{2})$, and The Functional Determinant derives the same identity in the scalar case, $k_m^{2}-\mathcal{F}_R\mathcal{F}_L$.

So the determinant needs no glyph. The two-equation statement is: eliminate one member of the pair and the fluctuation operator is $\Box-k_m^{2}$ on the remaining slot; its determinant is the Gaussian result.

The Index

The corpus already counts the two kernels. The Index Theorem computes $\mathrm{ind}\,\tilde D_R=n_+-n_-$ with the one-slot operator, and the supersymmetry article writes $\mathrm{ind}=\dim\ker\tilde\nabla^{\natural}-\dim\ker\tilde\nabla$. The glyph appears in two sentences of the index article and in none of its notation rows. The index of the pair is the difference of the two kernel counts, and that is a statement about the two lines.

The Propagator

The Green matrix is two kernels. The equation $(\mathbb{D}-k_m)\mathcal S=i\delta I$ is the pair of equations

$$ \tilde\nabla S_R-k_mS_L=i\,\delta,\qquad \tilde\nabla^{\natural}S_L-k_mS_R=i\,\delta , $$

one for each slot. The solution printed by the corpus, $\mathcal S=(\mathbb{D}+k_m)G$, is the two kernels $S_L=(\tilde\nabla+k_m)G$ and $S_R=(\tilde\nabla^{\natural}+k_m)G$ with $(\Box-k_m^{2})G=\delta$. Substitution cancels the cross terms and returns $i\delta$ in each slot, since $\tilde\nabla\tilde\nabla^{\natural}=\tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde\nabla=\Box$ and $k_m$ is central.

The Regulator

The square of the regulator is the square of each member. The Fujikawa Method regulates the determinant with the square of the operator and uses $\mathbb{D}^{2}=\Box I$. Each member's composition with its conjugate is $\Box$, so the regulator is $\Box$ without naming a pair. The article's own sentence, "the square of the pair operator is scalar", is the statement that the two members share one square.

What the four inversions need. A matrix. The Gaussian integral over two coupled fields, the inverse of a coupled system, the block determinant and the index of a two-block complex all need the array. None of them needs a letter for it. The array is written once, in place, with its two blocks named, and the reader sees the coupling.

The State of the Corpus

Thirty-one articles printed the glyph; none of the thirty-one prints it now. A scan of articles_physics/ and articles_maths/ for \mathbb{D} returns two files, and both occurrences in each are the same sentence, the prohibition itself: "No letter gathers them into one object on $\mathbb{B}\oplus\mathbb{B}$: no $\mathbb{D}$, no two-by-two array and no subscripted glyph." A mention that forbids a symbol is not a use of it, and the two conventions pages keep that sentence in both menus.

Four rewritings were used, and they are the four cases of the tables above.

  1. Two lines, mass explicit. The commonest. $\tilde\nabla\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}=k_m\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}$ and $\tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}=k_m\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}$, with each line composed with its partner equal to $\Box$.
  2. The array, written in place, with no letter. Where a coupled system is inverted or its determinant taken, the two-by-two block array is printed and its entries named.
  3. $\mathcal{D}$, the two-sided contraction. Where the field is one element of the module and the operator genuinely acts from both sides, the letter is changed and the words change with it: not "pair operator" but "the two-sided contraction" or "the two lines".
  4. The sentence alone. Where the glyph was a citation or a table note, the sentence now names the two equations and the row is gone.

The third case is the one that breaks the letter rather than the object. $\mathbb{D}$ and $\mathcal{D}$ are different glyphs for different carriers: $\mathbb{D}$ is the two-slot map on $\mathbb{B}\oplus\mathbb{B}$, $\mathcal{D}$ is one two-sided operator on the spinor module whose two chiral blocks are the two lines. Articles that keep a two-sided operator therefore keep an operator, but not the pair.

What the Migration Costs

Four matrices stay. The Gaussian integral, the inverse, the block determinant and the index each invert or factor a coupled system, and a coupled system is a matrix. The migration writes the matrix in place, with its two blocks named, and gives it no letter. The cost is three lines of display per article in those places.

The two first-order-root articles keep their argument, with a new warrant. Both argue that no single first-order operator on one field carries a mass, because one gradient on one field is first order and cannot equal a second-order operator. Written without the glyph the argument is the same and needs no new object: one equation on one field is first order, the mass is a second-order term, and two equations carry it. The warrant is the failure of $\tilde\nabla^{2}$ to be scalar, which the articles already prove.

The section heading lost its subject. §The Pair Operator and Its Symbol is now §The Two First-Order Equations and Their Symbol. Twelve files cite the section by name: ten cite the current name and two cite names that no longer existed, §The Pair Operator on the Algebra and §The Pair Operator. The migration repaired the two and renamed the section once.

The notation tables lost one row. The row was $\mathbb{D}$ with its square. In the eleven shorthand files it is redundant with the equation row above it, and in the five one-element files it is wrong about its carrier.

What the corpus gains. The mass is visible as the coupling. The two operators are visible on their two lines. The metric is named once. The signature is on the page. Two of the three collisions go; the third, the words for $\mathrm{Sd}_{x,y}$, carries no glyph and remains. And the notation says how many fields an article counts.

The Order the Work Took

  1. The two conventions pages, definition first, since every citation points at them. Done: the section is now The Two First-Order Equations and Their Symbol, and the old text stands in the appendix below.
  2. The eleven shorthand files, beginning with the six that stated the operator and its square as their own notation. Done.
  3. The nine computations, where the determinant, the Green matrix and the index were reduced one at a time and each reduction checked. Done.
  4. The five one-element articles, where the passage from the pair to one element was already written. Done; these are the articles that now write $\mathcal{D}$.
  5. The five citations, the two mathematics articles, the notation tables, and the section citations. Done.

The order was not the order of difficulty. The two mathematics articles came last because they are the only two whose subject was the array itself, and a rewrite there had to move the argument from a letter to a printed matrix rather than replace one sentence.

Summary

Thirty-one files printed the pair operator. All thirty-one are now written as two chiral equations with no glyph, or, in the five one-element articles, as the two-sided contraction $\mathcal{D}$; no physics or mathematics article writes $\mathbb{D}$. Eleven use the glyph as a shorthand that the definition supplies in one step. Nine use it where a number is computed, and every number is a one-slot number: the determinant of the pair is the determinant of the Klein–Gordon operator on one slot, which is why its exponent is four and not eight; the index is the difference of two kernel counts; the Green matrix is two kernels. Five use the glyph for a one-element operator, which is the two equations read on one element with the two slots identified with the two chirality parts. Five cite it, and one prints the same letter for a different operator.

The glyph is to go because it hides four things. The adjoint is the first: $\mathbb{D}^{\dagger}=-\mathbb{D}$ covers the massless operator only, since $(\mathbb{D}-k_m)^{\dagger}=-\mathbb{D}-k_m$ and no multiple of it is Hermitian, so the operator the corpus writes is not the Hermitian one; that operator, $i\mathbb{D}-k_m$, is not a multiple of it. The metric is the second: the block adjoint uses the diagonal form on $\mathbb{B}\oplus\mathbb{B}$ and never names it. The signature is the third: it sits in the signs of the two members' spatial units, and $\mathbb{D}^{2}=\Box I$ reports one scalar for both. The carrier is the fourth: eight dimensions or four, with no mark to say which, and a determinant exponent that is the same for both counts.

The migration was a deletion. The conventions printed the two equations and then called the glyph their abbreviation; four articles printed both rows of the same statement in one table; the computations already printed their one-slot values. The cost was four unnamed block arrays, kept where a coupled system is inverted, and the section citations, now repaired.

What is left. Two mentions of the glyph, both inside the sentence that forbids it, on the two conventions pages. Every notation table that carried a $\mathbb{D}$ row now carries the two equations there instead.

Appendix: The Retired Section, Verbatim

The section below stood in Operator and Calculus Conventions until the migration. It is kept here as the record of what was removed, in its own words, and it is not the usage of the corpus: the pair operator it defines is not allowed, and every article that used it now writes the two first-order equations.

The Pair Operator and Its Symbol (Legacy Text)

The pair. The two first-order equations of the series, $\tilde\nabla\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}=k_m\,\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}$ and $\tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}=k_m\,\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}$, are one operator on $\mathbb{B}\oplus\mathbb{B}$. With $\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}=(\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L},\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R})$ it is

$$ \mathbb{D}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}=\bigl(\tilde\nabla\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R},\ \tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}\bigr), \qquad \mathbb{D}^2=\Box I , $$

and the massive equation is $(\mathbb{D}-k_m)\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}=0$, with massless limit $\mathbb{D}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}=0$. The operator is the pair written once: it carries no matrix generator, no Clifford index and no chart, and its only content is which gradient acts on which part. The mass is the off-diagonal coupling of the two parts and is the identity on $\mathbb{B}$ in each place it acts. Every component equation of the series is a coordinate form of that pair and not a second dynamics. Here $k_m=mc/\hbar$ is the mass wave number, the inverse of the mass length.

Neither gradient squares to the d'Alembertian, and their composition does. Written as a product of elements, $\tilde\nabla^2$ is not $\Box$: $e_t$ commutes with the three spatial units, so the mixed derivatives survive and the square is not scalar. Only the composition with the conjugate is scalar, $\tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde\nabla=\tilde\nabla\tilde\nabla^{\natural}=\Box$, and this is why the pair is the least object that carries a first-order massive equation: one gradient on one field is first order and cannot equal a second-order operator, so no one-field first-order massive equation exists in this algebra. The free equation stays first order on one field, and the mass is what needs the pair.

Written on one element. Where the field is carried on one element, the same operator is written with its two sides in position,

$$ \mathbb{D}:\ \tilde X\longmapsto-\frac{\partial\tilde X}{\partial r'_t}\,\vec e_x-\sum_k\vec e_k\frac{\partial\tilde X}{\partial R_k}\,\vec e_y , $$

and the sides are the whole of the content. The time term is a right multiplication by $\vec e_x$ alone; each space term carries $\vec e_k$ on the left and $\vec e_y$ on the right, and the two multipliers are fixed basis elements that select the chiral slots and do not depend on the field. Left and right multiplications commute and the pure units anticommute, $\vec e_x\vec e_y=-\vec e_y\vec e_x$, with $\vec e_x^2=\vec e_y^2=-e_t$, so the cross terms cancel and the square is the d'Alembertian of the material sector, $\Delta-\partial^2/\partial {r'_{t}}^2$. The $i$ of the coordinate derivative is absorbed in the multiplier, $\partial/\partial R_t=-i\,\partial/\partial r'_t$, and no factor is left in front of the derivative; this is the pair read on one element and not a second definition.

The symbol. On a plane wave the two gradients act as left multiplication by the momentum element and its conjugate, $i\hbar\tilde K_{\Phi}=i\tilde P_{\Phi}=-(E/c)\,e_t+i\vec p$ with $-\hbar^{2}\tilde K_{\Phi}\tilde K_{\Phi}^{\natural}=E^2/c^2-\lvert\vec p\rvert^2=p^2$, the four-momentum being $p_0=E/c$; the symbol of the pair is the momentum element itself, so that it carries the momentum $p_\mu$ that the gradient's inverse length becomes once multiplied by $\hbar$,

$$ \mathbb{D}_p:\ (w_L,w_R)\longmapsto\bigl(i\hbar\tilde K_{\Phi}\,w_R,\ -i\hbar\tilde K_{\Phi}^{\natural}w_L\bigr), \qquad \mathbb{D}_p^2=p^{2}\,I , $$

and on one element it reads $\mathbb{D}_p:\tilde X\mapsto i\frac{E}{c}\,\tilde X\vec e_x-i\sum_k p_{k}\,\vec e_k\tilde X\vec e_y$. The square of the element is not the scalar: $-\hbar^{2}\tilde K_{\Phi}^{2}$ has scalar part $E^2+\lvert\vec p\rvert^2$ and a vector part that does not cancel, and only the composition with the conjugate returns $p^2$. On the mass shell $p^{2}=m^{2}\,c^{2}$ the symbol of $\mathbb{D}_p-m\,c$ is therefore singular exactly there and nowhere else, and the two chiral slots share the one condition.

The inverse is a Green matrix. With $G$ the Green's function of the d'Alembertian, $(\Box-k_m^{2})G=\delta$, the Green matrix of the pair is $\mathcal{S}=(\mathbb{D}+k_m)G$, and $(\mathbb{D}-k_m)\mathcal{S}=(\mathbb{D}^2-k_m^{2})G=\delta I$. The inverse introduces no new object: the scalar $k_m$ commutes with $\mathbb{D}$, and the factorization is the algebraic identity $(\mathbb{D}-k_m)(\mathbb{D}+k_m)=\mathbb{D}^2-k_m^{2}$.

The glyph and its marks. The glyph is $\mathbb{D}$, and it takes a mark for the factor and never for the chart. A subscript names the factor when that factor is not the material gradient — $\mathbb{D}_p$ for the constant momentum covector, which is the symbol — and an element in parentheses is an argument, $\mathbb{D}(i\hbar\tilde K_{\Phi})$, because a letter on the right without parentheses, $\mathbb{D}\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp}}$, is the field acted on. The gradient follows the same rule: $\tilde\nabla$ bare, $\natural$ or a sector label only where a second gradient is in play. The pair is never written $\mathbb{D}_{\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}}$ or $\mathbb{D}^{\natural}$; the sector enters through the coordinate, and the conjugate of the pair is the pair with its two gradients exchanged.

The pair is sector-free. On $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ and on $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ the operator is the same and only the four derivatives change, $\partial/\partial R_t=-i\,\partial/\partial r'_t$ against $\partial/\partial R_t=\partial/\partial r_t$. Its square follows the d'Alembertian of §The Quaternionic Bilinear Product, Its Form and Its Norm: $\Delta-\partial^2/\partial {r'_{t}}^2$ on the material sector and $\partial^2/\partial r_t^2-\Delta|_{\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}}$ on the informational one, and a reader who moves the pair from one sector to the other moves one overall sign and no operator.
