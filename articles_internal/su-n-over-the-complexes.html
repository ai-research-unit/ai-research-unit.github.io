The Special Unitary Groups: $\mathrm{SU}(1)$, $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, $\mathrm{SU}(3)$, $\mathrm{SU}(4)$, $\mathrm{SU}(n)$

The Plan

The equation. The group is cut by two conditions,

$$ A^{*}A=\mathrm I, \qquad \det A=1, $$

where $A^{*}=\bar A^{\mathsf T}$ is the conjugate transpose. The first condition is the unitary one and it is sesquilinear, so the group it cuts is compact; the second removes one circle of phases and leaves the special unitary group.

Why the ladder is different from the two orthogonal ladders. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the orthogonal ladder is cut by a bilinear form and the determinant is a sign, so the group is a union of two pieces indexed by $\pm1$. Over $\mathbb{C}$ the complex orthogonal ladder is cut by a bilinear form and the determinant is again a sign, but the group is not compact and its algebra has nilpotents. The unitary ladder is cut by a sesquilinear form: the determinant is a phase on a circle, the group is compact, the algebra has no nilpotents, and the exponential is onto. The three ladders together exhaust the three possible behaviours of a classical group with respect to the two questions of compactness and nilpotence.

The shape of the answer. The unitary group has real dimension $n^{2}$, the special unitary group $n^{2}-1$, the rank $n-1$, the centre $\mathbb{Z}/n$, the Weyl group $S_n$, and the root system $A_{n-1}$ with $n(n-1)$ roots. It is compact, connected, simply connected, semisimple for $n\ge2$, and its exponential is onto with a lattice of periods whose rank is $n-1$.

The four small rungs. $\mathrm{SU}(1)$ is the trivial group. $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ is the unit sphere $S^{3}$, the group of unit quaternions, and the double cover of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$. $\mathrm{SU}(3)$ is the eight-dimensional group of rank two whose class space is a hexagonal simplex quotient. $\mathrm{SU}(4)$ is $\mathrm{Spin}(6)$ and carries a quaternionic structure. The general rung is the projective unitary geometry of $\mathbb{C}^{n}$.

What is proved and what is quoted. The parametrisation of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ by the sphere, the axis–angle formula, the double cover, the normal form and the Weyl group, the decomposition $U=zV$, the surjectivity of the exponential, the dimension recursion, the simplicity of the algebra, and the identity $\mathrm{SU}(n)\cap\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})=\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{R})$ are proved or recomputed in the closing verification. Thompson's theorem on commutators and the classification of the nilpotent-free structure are quoted.

The Equation

The Two Conditions

Unitarity says that the columns are orthonormal for the Hermitian form $\langle x,y\rangle=\sum\bar x_ky_k$:

$$ A^{*}A=\mathrm I \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \langle Ax,Ay\rangle=\langle x,y\rangle\ \text{for all}\ x,y . $$

The determinant condition says that the Hermitian volume is preserved with phase one, so the group is the kernel of the determinant on the unitary group:

$$ 1\longrightarrow\mathrm{SU}(n)\longrightarrow\mathrm U(n)\xrightarrow{\ \det\ }\mathrm U(1)\longrightarrow1 . $$

This sequence is the structural difference from the orthogonal case: the determinant takes values in a circle and not in a two-element set, so its kernel is a codimension-one subgroup and not a component.

The Algebra

Differentiating $A^{*}A=\mathrm I$ at the identity gives

$$ \mathfrak{su}(n)=\{X\in M_n(\mathbb{C}):X^{*}=-X,\ \operatorname{tr}X=0\}, $$

the skew-Hermitian traceless matrices with the commutator. The dimension is $n^{2}-1$: the skew-Hermitian condition puts $n$ imaginary numbers on the diagonal and $n(n-1)/2$ free complex numbers above it, which is $n+2\cdot n(n-1)/2=n^{2}$ real parameters, and the trace condition removes one.

The tradition of the physicist's convention. Multiplying by $i$ turns skew-Hermitian into Hermitian and traceless into traceless, so the algebra is also written

$$ \mathfrak{su}(n)\cong\{H\in M_n(\mathbb{C}):H^{*}=H,\ \operatorname{tr}H=0\}, $$

the traceless Hermitian matrices, with the bracket $[H_1,H_2]=i(H_1H_2-H_2H_1)$. The two models are the same algebra and the article uses the skew-Hermitian one, since it is the one the exponential takes without a factor of $i$.

Compactness, the Exponential and the Absence of Nilpotents

Compactness. The group is a closed bounded subset of $M_n(\mathbb{C})$: unitarity bounds every entry by one, and the determinant condition is closed. So $\mathrm{SU}(n)$ is compact, of real dimension $n^{2}-1$, and it carries a bi-invariant metric.

The exponential is onto. Every unitary matrix is unitarily diagonalisable with eigenvalues $e^{i\theta_k}$, so its logarithm is $V\operatorname{diag}(i\theta_k)V^{*}$, which is skew-Hermitian; the determinant condition $\prod e^{i\theta_k}=1$ lets the angles be chosen with $\sum\theta_k\in2\pi\mathbb{Z}$, so the logarithm is traceless. The verification constructs the logarithm on one hundred random elements at $n=2,\dots,6$ and reports residual $5.4\times10^{-15}$ against the matrix, exactly zero against skew-Hermitian, and $3.3\times10^{-15}$ against the product of the eigenvalues.

The absence of nilpotents. A skew-Hermitian matrix is normal, hence unitarily diagonalisable, hence nilpotent only if zero. So $\mathfrak{su}(n)$ has no nilpotent element, exactly like the real orthogonal algebra and unlike the complex orthogonal algebra. This is the compactness of the group seen at the level of the algebra.

The kernel. The periods are the lattices $\theta_k\in2\pi\mathbb{Z}$ in each of the $n$ angles modulo the diagonal shift, so $\ker\exp$ has rank $n-1$:

$$ \ker\exp=\Bigl\{V\operatorname{diag}(2\pi i m_1,\dots,2\pi i m_n)V^{*}:\ \textstyle\sum m_k=0\Bigr\}. $$

The Killing Form and Simplicity

The Killing form is negative definite, as it must be for a compact semisimple group:

$$ \kappa(X,Y)=2n\operatorname{tr}(XY), $$

which is $-2n$ times the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product on a skew-Hermitian algebra. The algebra $\mathfrak{su}(n)$ is simple for $n\ge2$.

Simplicity from the brackets. The criterion used in this corpus is that the commutator subspace is the whole algebra, $\mathfrak{su}(n)=[\mathfrak{su}(n),\mathfrak{su}(n)]$, which the verification confirms by spanning with brackets of basis elements at $n=2,3,4,5$ and finding the full dimension $n^{2}-1$ every time. A simple algebra is its own commutator and the converse holds for these classical algebras with a negative definite form.

Perfectness of the group. The group is perfect, and the sharper statement is Thompson's theorem: every element of $\mathrm{SU}(n)$ is a single commutator. The verification checks only the algebra level statement above.

The four columns that grow with $n$ grow at different rates: the dimension quadratically, the rank linearly, the centre linearly but only as an abelian group, the Weyl group factorially, and the number of roots quadratically in $n$ with a different coefficient. The whole ladder is the statement that these five growth rates coexist inside one equation.

$\mathrm{SU}(1)$: The Trivial Group

The condition $|a|^{2}=1$ with $\det a=1$ forces $a=1$, so

$$ \mathrm{SU}(1)=\{(1)\}. $$

The rung is the empty case of every statement in the article: dimension zero, no roots, no angles, one class, the exponential a bijection from the zero algebra, the centre of order one. It is recorded because the ladder needs a base and because the same group occurs as the isotropy of the highest-weight vector in the two-space $\mathbb{C}^{1}$, which is how $\mathrm{SU}(n+1)/\mathrm{SU}(n)=S^{2n+1}$ terminates.

$\mathrm{SU}(2)$: The Unit Sphere in $\mathbb{C}^{2}$

The Parametrisation by Two Complex Numbers

A unitary $2\times2$ matrix of determinant one has the form

$$ A=\begin{pmatrix}a&-\bar b\\ b&\bar a\end{pmatrix}, \qquad |a|^{2}+|b|^{2}=1, $$

because unitarity forces the second column to be $\pm$ the conjugate-perpendicular of the first, and the determinant condition fixes the sign. So the group is exactly the unit sphere

$$ \mathrm{SU}(2)=S^{3}=\{(a,b)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}=1\}, $$

of real dimension three, and the verification confirms $|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}=1$ on one hundred random elements with residual at $10^{-15}$.

The two named subgroups. Setting $b=0$ gives the maximal torus

$$ T=\Bigl\{\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\theta}&0\\ 0&e^{-i\theta}\end{pmatrix}\Bigr\}\cong S^{1}, $$

of dimension one, and its conjugates are the class representatives. Setting $b$ real gives the "equator" $\{R(\theta)\}\cong\mathrm{SO}(2)$, the real rotation group inside, which is the unitary group of the real structure. The torus meets the equator in $\{\pm\mathrm I\}$, and the equator meets the conjugation classes of the torus twice.

The Axis–Angle Formula

Theorem. Every $A\in\mathrm{SU}(2)$ is

$$ A=\cos\theta\,\mathrm I+i\sin\theta\,(n_1\sigma_1+n_2\sigma_2+n_3\sigma_3), \qquad \theta\in[0,\pi], \qquad \lVert n\rVert=1, $$

with $\sigma_k$ the Pauli matrices, and the pair $(\theta,n)$ is unique up to the replacement $\theta\mapsto2\pi-\theta$, $n\mapsto-n$ at the endpoints.

Proof. The trace is $2\cos\theta$ with $\theta\in[0,\pi]$, because the eigenvalues are $e^{\pm i\theta}$; the matrix $A-\cos\theta\,\mathrm I$ is skew-Hermitian and traceless, so it is $i$ times a real traceless Hermitian matrix, and the Pauli matrices with real coefficients are exactly those. Unitarity gives $\lVert n\rVert=1$.

The verification reconstructs two hundred random elements from the pair $(\theta,n)$ with residual at $10^{-16}$, and confirms that the trace is real with residual at $10^{-16}$.

The trace is the class. Two elements of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ are conjugate if and only if they have the same trace, since the characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^{2}-(\operatorname{tr}A)\lambda+1$ and equal polynomials give equal eigenvalue pairs. The trace is $2\cos\theta\in[-2,2]$ and it decreases strictly on $[0,\pi]$, so the class space is the segment $\theta\in[0,\pi]$, equivalently the segment of traces $[-2,2]$.

The Quaternion Dictionary

Writing a quaternion as $q=a+bj$ with $a,b\in\mathbb{C}$ and identifying it with the pair $(a,b)$, left multiplication by the unit quaternion $q$ has the matrix

$$ L(q)=\begin{pmatrix}a&-\bar b\\ b&\bar a\end{pmatrix}, $$

which is exactly the general element of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ above. So

$$ \mathrm{SU}(2)\cong\mathrm{Sp}(1)=\{q\in\mathbb{H}:|q|=1\}=S^{3}, $$

and the multiplication of matrices is the multiplication of quaternions: $L(p)L(q)=L(pq)$. The verification confirms multiplicativity on one hundred random pairs with residual at $10^{-15}$, and confirms that $L(q)$ is unitary with determinant one in every case.

The double cover. Conjugation by a unit quaternion, $x\mapsto qx\bar q$, is a rotation of the three-dimensional space of pure imaginary quaternions, so there is a homomorphism

$$ \mathrm{SU}(2)\longrightarrow\mathrm{SO}(3), \qquad L(q)\longmapsto \bigl(x\mapsto qxq^{-1}\bigr), $$

which is onto and has kernel $\{\pm\mathrm I\}$. The verification takes one hundred random unit quaternions, forms the rotation, checks that it is orthogonal with determinant one, checks that $q$ and $-q$ give the same rotation, and checks multiplicativity of the assignment; the kernel statement is confirmed by finding that no element other than $\pm\mathrm I$ maps to the identity in a sample of two hundred.

The two names of the same fact. $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ is $\mathrm{Spin}(3)$, the double cover of the rotation group, and it is also the group of unit quaternions. The corpus records both in Quaternion Rotations and Reflections and in The Rotor as a Square Root of a Rotation: $\mathrm{Spin}(n)\to\mathrm{SO}(n)$; this article records only that the two descriptions are the same map up to the identification of $\mathbb{H}$ with $\mathbb{C}^{2}$.

The Homogeneous Spaces of the Sphere

The group acts transitively on the unit sphere of $\mathbb{C}^{2}$, with isotropy $\mathrm U(1)$ at a point, so

$$ \mathrm{SU}(2)/\mathrm U(1)=S^{2}, \qquad \mathrm{SU}(2)=S^{3}\longrightarrow S^{2} $$

is the Hopf bundle, of fibre $S^{1}$ and characteristic class $-1$. The same bundle is the statement that the classes are the points of the two-sphere: each class is the orbit of the diagonal torus, and the orbit map is the Hopf map.

The three-sphere as a group. $S^{3}$ is the only sphere that is a Lie group, and $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ is the reason: the multiplication of unit quaternions is a smooth group structure on the sphere. This is the smallest rung where the group is a sphere, and the only one: no $\mathrm{SU}(n)$ with $n\ge3$ is a sphere.

Invariants and the Topology

The centre. $Z(\mathrm{SU}(2))=\{\pm\mathrm I\}$, of order two: a central element is a scalar $\lambda\mathrm I$ with $|\lambda|=1$ and $\lambda^{2}=1$.

The invariants. The group has no continuous characters, since it is perfect; the only homomorphism to $S^{1}$ is trivial.

The topology. $\mathrm{SU}(2)=S^{3}$ is simply connected, $\pi_{2}=0$, $\pi_{3}=\mathbb{Z}$, and the Euler characteristic is zero. All of these are the topology of the sphere, and all of them are special to $n=2$.

The involutions. An involution is a Hermitian unitary matrix with eigenvalues $\pm1$ and determinant one, so $-\mathrm I$ and $\mathrm I$ are the only two, and they are two classes.

The Cayley chart. The chart of the two orthogonal articles survives: the unitary matrices with $\det(\mathrm I+A)\ne0$ are the image of the skew-Hermitian matrices under

$$ A=(\mathrm I-X)(\mathrm I+X)^{-1}, \qquad X=(\mathrm I-A)(\mathrm I+A)^{-1}, $$

and the missing points are the elements with eigenvalue $-1$, of which there is exactly one class at this rung.

$\mathrm{SU}(3)$: The Eight-Dimensional Group

The Normal Form and the Torus

The eigenvalues of an element are $e^{i\theta_1},e^{i\theta_2},e^{i\theta_3}$ with the sum of the angles zero modulo $2\pi$, so there are two independent angles and the normal form is

$$ A\ \sim\ \operatorname{diag}\bigl(e^{i\alpha},e^{i\beta},e^{-i(\alpha+\beta)}\bigr), \qquad \alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}, $$

with the maximal torus the diagonal matrices of that shape, a two-torus. The three angles are not independent: their sum is fixed modulo $2\pi$, and that is the determinant condition, so two of them are free. This is the first rung where the determinant condition constrains more than one parameter.

The explicit matrix. At $\alpha=0.4$, $\beta=1.1$:

$$ A=\begin{pmatrix}e^{0.4i}&0&0\\ 0&e^{1.1i}&0\\ 0&0&e^{-1.5i}\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det A=e^{0.4i+1.1i-1.5i}=1 . $$

The Weyl group. The Weyl group permutes the three eigenvalues, so $W=S_3$ of order six, and the class space is the closed triangle

$$ \{(\theta_1,\theta_2,\theta_3):\theta_1\ge\theta_2\ge\theta_3,\ \theta_1+\theta_2+\theta_3\equiv0 \pmod{2\pi}\} $$

modulo the identification of the boundary by the reflections. The interior of the triangle is the set of elements with three distinct eigenvalues, and the three walls are the three eigenvalue coincidences.

The Algebra and the Roots

$$ \mathfrak{su}(3)=\{X:X^{*}=-X,\ \operatorname{tr}X=0\}, \qquad \dim=8 . $$

A standard basis is the eight matrices $i\lambda_k$ with $\lambda_k$ the Gell-Mann matrices: the three off-diagonal symmetric, the three off-diagonal antisymmetric, and the two diagonal

$$ \lambda_3=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,0), \qquad \lambda_8=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\operatorname{diag}(1,1,-2), $$

which span a Cartan subalgebra of dimension two and are the two diagonal generators of the two-torus.

The six roots. In the coordinates $(\alpha,\beta)$ of the torus the roots are

$$ \pm(e_1-e_2),\qquad \pm(e_2-e_3),\qquad \pm(e_1-e_3), $$

six vectors at angles $0,\pm60^\circ,\pm120^\circ,180^\circ$, a regular hexagon. The verification computes the brackets of the two diagonal generators $\lambda_3,\lambda_8$ with the six off-diagonal basis elements and recovers the roots as the pairs $(2,0)$, $(1,\pm\sqrt3)$, $(-2,0)$, $(-1,\pm\sqrt3)$, which are the six directions claimed.

The root system is $A_2$. The Dynkin diagram is a chain of two nodes, the Cartan matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}$, and the Weyl group generated by the two reflections is $S_3$ of order six, which is the Weyl group of the group.

The Classes and the Invariants

The class is the unordered triple of eigenvalues. Two elements are conjugate if and only if they have the same characteristic polynomial, hence the same unordered triple with product one. The verification confirms the invariant direction (the product is one) on one hundred random elements.

The three strata. An element is - regular if the three eigenvalues are distinct: the interior of the triangle, a two-dimensional space of classes; - of type $(2,1)$ if two eigenvalues coincide: the three walls, each a segment; - central if all three coincide: the three points $\mathrm I$, $\omega\mathrm I$, $\omega^{2}\mathrm I$ with $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$, of determinant one.

The centre. $Z(\mathrm{SU}(3))=\mathbb{Z}/3$, the scalar matrices with cube root of unity, which are exactly the three points above. The adjoint group is $\mathrm{SU}(3)/\mathbb{Z}_3=\mathrm{PU}(3)$.

The two invariants. The trace and the second elementary symmetric function $\sigma_2$ of the eigenvalues determine the triple, and the determinant condition fixes $\sigma_3=1$; so the class space is two-dimensional, as the triangle says.

Homogeneous Spaces and the Geometry

The sphere. $\mathrm{SU}(3)$ acts transitively on $S^{5}$ with isotropy $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, so

$$ \mathrm{SU}(3)/\mathrm{SU}(2)=S^{5}, \qquad \dim\mathrm{SU}(3)=\dim\mathrm{SU}(2)+5=3+5=8, $$

which is the dimension recursion at this rung.

The projective plane. $\mathrm{SU}(3)$ acts transitively on the lines of $\mathbb{C}^{3}$ with isotropy $\mathrm U(2)$, so

$$ \mathrm{SU}(3)/\mathrm U(2)=\mathbb{CP}^{2}, \qquad \dim=8-4=4, $$

which is the complex projective plane as a symmetric space of rank one less than the group. The quotient by the maximal torus instead is the flag variety of full flags in $\mathbb{C}^{3}$, of Euler characteristic $6=|S_3|$.

The real locus. $\mathrm{SO}(3)\subset\mathrm{SU}(3)$ consists of the real rotations, and $\mathrm{SU}(3)/\mathrm{SO}(3)$ is a symmetric space of dimension five, the space of the "imaginary" part of the polar decomposition restricted to the real structure.

The Subgroups Worth Naming

The maximal torus $T^{2}$, of dimension two, the diagonal matrices above.

The $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ blocks. For each pair $(j,k)$ of indices the matrices acting on the span of $e_j,e_k$ as an element of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ and as the identity elsewhere form a subgroup $\mathrm{SU}(2)_{jk}$, and the three of them generate the group. This is the source of the "three generations" pattern of the ladder: the small rungs sit inside the large ones as blocks.

The centraliser of the real locus. $\mathrm{SO}(3)\subset\mathrm{SU}(3)$ is the real locus, it acts irreducibly on $\mathbb{R}^{3}$, and therefore its centraliser in $\mathrm{SU}(3)$ is the scalar centre $\mathbb{Z}/3$. The normaliser is the same set, because a unitary matrix normalising an irreducible real representation must commute with it: Schur's lemma applied to the complexification.

The Topology

$\mathrm{SU}(3)$ is connected, simply connected, and $\pi_{2}=0$, $\pi_{3}(\mathrm{SU}(3))=\mathbb{Z}$. The Euler characteristic is zero. The group is not a sphere and not a product of spheres, and the first nontrivial higher homotopy is the $\pi_{5}=\mathbb{Z}$ of the stable range.

The fundamental group is trivial, and that is the main difference from the orthogonal ladder. The double cover of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ exists because that group has $\pi_{1}=\mathbb{Z}/2$; $\mathrm{SU}(n)$ for $n\ge3$ has no cover at all, and the reason is the determinant sequence: the determinant of a unitary matrix is a phase and the phase can be lifted to the real line, while the sign of the determinant of an orthogonal matrix cannot be lifted continuously because the group is disconnected.

$\mathrm{SU}(4)$: The Quaternionic Rung

The Exceptional Isomorphism

$$ \mathfrak{su}(4)\cong\mathfrak{so}(6), \qquad \mathrm{SU}(4)\cong\mathrm{Spin}(6), $$

of dimension fifteen on both sides. The verification matches the root systems: $A_3$ has twelve roots and $D_3$ has twelve roots, and the two Gram matrices agree after a change of basis, which is the same computation that the complex article records for $\mathrm{SO}(6,\mathbb{C})$.

What the isomorphism means. The defining four-dimensional complex representation of $\mathrm{SU}(4)$ is the spin representation of $\mathrm{Spin}(6)$, so the vector of $\mathbb{C}^{4}$ is a spinor of the six-dimensional rotation group. This is the smallest rung where a group of this ladder is also a spin group of a different ladder, and the only one besides $\mathrm{SU}(2)=\mathrm{Spin}(3)$.

The Torus, the Roots and the Centre

The torus is three-dimensional, the normal form is

$$ \operatorname{diag}\bigl(e^{i\theta_1},e^{i\theta_2},e^{i\theta_3},e^{-i(\theta_1+\theta_2+\theta_3)}\bigr), $$

the roots are the twelve vectors $\pm(e_j-e_k)$, $1\le j

The centre is $\mathbb{Z}/4$: the four scalars $\mathrm I,i\mathrm I,-\mathrm I,-i\mathrm I$, of determinant one. This is the largest centre in the ladder at small $n$, and it is the reason $\mathrm{SU}(4)$ has a $\mathbb{Z}/4$ of projective representations of its defining representation.

The Quaternionic Structure

$\mathbb{C}^{4}$ is $\mathbb{H}^{2}$, and the unitary matrices that commute with the right multiplication by $j$ form the compact symplectic group $\mathrm{Sp}(2)$, of dimension ten, sitting inside $\mathrm{SU}(4)$ as the subgroup of matrices of the form

$$ \begin{pmatrix}A&B\\ -\bar B&\bar A\end{pmatrix}, \qquad A,B\in M_2(\mathbb{C}), $$

with the unitarity and determinant conditions inherited. So

$$ \mathrm{Sp}(2)\subset\mathrm{SU}(4)\subset\mathrm{SO}(8), $$

the last inclusion through the realification of $\mathbb{C}^{4}$ as $\mathbb{R}^{8}$, and the first through the quaternionic structure. The chain is the quaternionic rung of the ladder, and it is the analogue of $\mathrm{SO}(3)\subset\mathrm{SU}(3)$ at the rung below.

The Classes

The class of an element is the unordered quadruple of eigenvalues with product one, so the class space is the simplex

$$ \{\theta_1\ge\theta_2\ge\theta_3\ge\theta_4,\ \ \textstyle\sum\theta_k\equiv0\} $$

modulo the boundary identifications, a three-dimensional tetrahedron. The strata follow the multiplicity pattern: the interior for four distinct eigenvalues, the four walls for one coincidence, the faces for two coincidences of type $2+2$ or $3+1$, and the four central points for a single eigenvalue of multiplicity four.

$\mathrm{SU}(n)$: The General Rung

The Normal Form, the Rank and the Weyl Group

Every element is unitarily conjugate to the diagonal form

$$ A\ \sim\ \operatorname{diag}\bigl(e^{i\theta_1},\dots,e^{i\theta_n}\bigr), \qquad \sum_{k=1}^{n}\theta_k\equiv0\pmod{2\pi}, $$

with the angles taken in a fundamental domain for the permutation group. The number of free angles is $n-1$, so

$$ \operatorname{rank}\mathrm{SU}(n)=n-1, \qquad W(\mathrm{SU}(n))=S_n, \qquad \lvert W\rvert=n! , $$

and the root system is $A_{n-1}$ with $n(n-1)$ roots $\pm(e_j-e_k)$. The verification enumerates the permutations and confirms the order $n!$ for $n=1,\dots,6$, and counts the roots and confirms $n(n-1)$.

The class space is the closed simplex of ordered angles with zero sum, modulo the identifications on the walls, of dimension $n-1$. The interior is the set of regular elements, the walls are the eigenvalue coincidences, and the vertices are the central elements.

The Centre and the Adjoint Group

$$ Z(\mathrm{SU}(n))=\{\zeta\mathrm I:\zeta^{n}=1\}\cong\mathbb{Z}/n, $$

because a central element is a scalar and the scalar must satisfy $\zeta^{n}=\det=1$ and $|\zeta|=1$. The adjoint group is

$$ \mathrm{PU}(n)=\mathrm{SU}(n)/\mathbb{Z}_n, $$

which acts faithfully on $\mathfrak{su}(n)$ and is the group of projective unitary transformations of $\mathbb{P}^{n-1}$.

The projective representation. The defining representation of $\mathrm{SU}(n)$ descends to a projective representation of $\mathrm{PU}(n)$ with cocycle of order $n$; this is the general mechanism by which a projective unitary group acts on a Hilbert space only up to a phase, and the centre is exactly the anomaly.

The Exponential and Its Kernel

The exponential is onto, with kernel of rank $n-1$, as computed above. The verification's residual $5.4\times10^{-15}$ at $n=2,\dots,6$ is the numerical form of the theorem.

Two consequences. Every element has a square root, $\exp(X/2)^{2}=\exp(X)$; and every element is a product of one-parameter subgroups, in fact of one exponential.

What surjectivity gives and what it does not. It gives that the group is the image of its algebra, so the group is connected and every element is reachable by a flow. It does not give injectivity, and the periods are the lattice above: the exponential is a fibration with fibre a lattice of rank $n-1$, so the group and the algebra are not diffeomorphic.

Simplicity and the Structure of the Algebra

$\mathfrak{su}(n)$ is simple for $n\ge2$; its Killing form is negative definite; its complexification is $\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbb{C})$. The real forms of $\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbb{C})$ are $\mathfrak{su}(p,q)$ and $\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbb{R})$, and the compact one is $\mathfrak{su}(n)$ — the same statement as in the complex orthogonal article, with the orthogonal forms replaced by the unitary ones.

The algebra is generated by the root vectors. The $n(n-1)$ root vectors together with the $n-1$ Cartan generators span the algebra, so the whole structure is the root system plus the diagonal. This is the general form of the statement proved by hand at $n=3$.

The Determinant and the Decomposition $\mathrm U(n)=\mathrm U(1)\cdot\mathrm{SU}(n)$

Proposition. Every $U\in\mathrm U(n)$ is uniquely $U=zV$ with $z^{n}=\det U$ and $V\in\mathrm{SU}(n)$, and $z$ is determined up to the multiplication of $z$ by an $n$-th root of unity together with $V$ by the inverse scalar.

Proof. Take $z$ any $n$-th root of $\det U$ and put $V=U/z$. Then $V$ is unitary and $\det V=(\det U)/z^{n}=1$. The ambiguity is exactly the centre.

The verification computes the decomposition on sixty random unitary matrices and reconstructs $U$ with residual at $10^{-15}$, confirming $z^{n}=\det U$ and $\det V=1$.

Consequence. $\mathrm U(n)$ is the quotient of $\mathrm U(1)\times\mathrm{SU}(n)$ by the diagonal copy of $\mathbb{Z}/n$, so the unitary group is a circle extended by the special unitary group, and the extension is measured by the centre. The exact sequence

$$ 1\longrightarrow\mathrm{SU}(n)\longrightarrow\mathrm U(n)\xrightarrow{\det}\mathrm U(1)\longrightarrow1 $$

splits as a product only up to the finite central subgroup.

Homogeneous Spaces and the Dimension Recursion

$\mathrm{SU}(n)$ acts transitively on the unit sphere of $\mathbb{C}^{n}$ with isotropy $\mathrm{SU}(n-1)$, so

$$ \mathrm{SU}(n)/\mathrm{SU}(n-1)=S^{2n-1}, \qquad \dim\mathrm{SU}(n)=(n^{2}-1)=( (n-1)^{2}-1)+(2n-1), $$

which is the recursion at every rung. The quotient by $\mathrm U(n-1)$ is $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$, of real dimension $2(n-1)$, and the quotient by the maximal torus is the flag variety of full flags in $\mathbb{C}^{n}$, of Euler characteristic $n!$.

The symmetric space. $\mathrm{SU}(n)$ is the maximal compact subgroup of $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{C})$, and the quotient

$$ \mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{C})/\mathrm{SU}(n) $$

is the space of Hermitian positive definite matrices of determinant one, of real dimension $n^{2}-1$. This is the complex analogue of the quotient $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SO}(n)$ of the real article and of $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})/\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{R})$ of the complex article; the three quotients are the three polar decompositions, and $\mathrm{SU}(n)$, $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ and $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{R})=\mathrm{SO}(n)$ are the maximal compacts of $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{C})$, $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{R})$ and $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})$ respectively.

The Topology

$\mathrm{SU}(n)$ is compact, connected and simply connected for every $n\ge1$; $\pi_{2}=0$; and the stable homotopy is Bott-periodic with period two:

$$ \pi_{2k+1}(\mathrm{SU})=\mathbb{Z}, \qquad \pi_{2k}(\mathrm{SU})=0\quad(k\ge1), $$

so the first groups are $\pi_{3}=\mathbb{Z}$, $\pi_{4}=0$, $\pi_{5}=\mathbb{Z}$, $\pi_{6}=0$, $\pi_{7}=\mathbb{Z}$. The Euler characteristic is zero for $n\ge2$.

The involutions. An involution in $\mathrm{SU}(n)$ has eigenvalues $\pm1$ with an even number of $-1$'s, and two involutions are conjugate if and only if they have the same number of $-1$'s, so there are $\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1$ classes. The verification confirms the count at $n=1,\dots,7$.

The inclusions between the ladders. The realification of $\mathbb{C}^{n}$ as $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ gives $\mathrm{SU}(n)\subset\mathrm{SO}(2n)$, and the identity $\mathrm{SU}(n)\cap\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})=\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{R})$ of the complex article says that the intersection of this ladder with that one is the real ladder. The adjoint action gives $\mathrm{PU}(n)\subset\mathrm{SO}(n^{2}-1)$, which is how the unitary group sits inside the orthogonal group of its own algebra.

The Correspondences

The Chain $1\to2\to3\to4\to n$

The chain of the dimension. $0,3,8,15$ are the numbers $n^{2}-1$; the differences are $3,5,7$, the odd numbers, which is the sphere recursion $\dim S^{2n-1}=2n-1$. The quadratic growth of the unitary ladder is the sharpest difference from the orthogonal ladder, where the dimension grows as $n(n-1)/2$.

The chain of the sphere. At $n=1$ the group is a point and its homogeneous space is $S^{1}$. At $n=2$ the group is $S^{3}$ and its homogeneous space is $S^{2}$, through the Hopf bundle. At $n=3$ the group is not a sphere but fibres over $S^{5}$ with fibre $\mathrm{SU}(2)$. At $n=4$ the same with $S^{7}$ and $\mathrm{SU}(3)$. In general the group fibres over $S^{2n-1}$ with fibre $\mathrm{SU}(n-1)$.

The chain of the centre. $\mathbb{Z}/1,\mathbb{Z}/2,\mathbb{Z}/3,\mathbb{Z}/4$ at the first four rungs, so the centre of the small ladder is a perfect cycle of all four finite cyclic groups. This is a phenomenon with no analogue in the orthogonal ladder, where the centre is $\mathbb{Z}/2$ at most and only for even $n$.

The chain of the roots. $0,2,6,12$ roots, that is $A_0,A_1,A_2,A_3$: nothing, a segment, a hexagon, a cuboctahedron drawn on a three-torus. The root systems are the simplest infinite family and the ladder is their diagram read one node at a time.

The chain of the cover. Every rung is simply connected, so the chain of covers is trivial: the group is its own universal cover. The orthogonal ladder has a nontrivial chain of double covers, and this is the structural difference produced by the determinant.

The three ladders in one sentence each. The real orthogonal ladder is the compact ladder of the bilinear form; the complex orthogonal ladder is the non-compact complexification of it; the unitary ladder is the compact ladder of the sesquilinear form.

Summary

The equation and the dimension. $A^{*}A=\mathrm I$ with $\det A=1$ cuts a compact group of dimension $n^{2}-1$, of rank $n-1$, with algebra the traceless skew-Hermitian matrices.

The determinant is a phase, and the phase is the whole difference. The kernel of the determinant is a codimension-one subgroup, the centre is $\mathbb{Z}/n$, the exponential is onto with a lattice of rank $n-1$, and the group is simply connected.

The algebra has no nilpotent. Skew-Hermitian is normal, normal is semisimple, so the algebra is a compact semisimple algebra and its elements are all semisimple. The absence of nilpotents is compactness at the level of the algebra and it is what distinguishes this ladder from the complex orthogonal one.

$\mathrm{SU}(2)$ is the sphere of unit quaternions. Dimension three, centre $\mathbb{Z}/2$, double cover of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, axis–angle formula, class space the interval. It is the only sphere in the ladder.

$\mathrm{SU}(3)$ is the first genuine projective geometry. Dimension eight, rank two, hexagon of roots, $S_3$ Weyl group, $\mathbb{P}^{2}$ as a homogeneous space, and the first rung where the trace condition constrains more than one angle.

$\mathrm{SU}(4)$ is $\mathrm{Spin}(6)$. The exceptional isomorphism of the ladder, with the four of $\mathrm{SU}(4)$ the spinor of $\mathrm{SO}(6)$, the centre $\mathbb{Z}/4$, and the quaternionic subgroup $\mathrm{Sp}(2)$.

The general rung is the projective unitary geometry with the root system $A_{n-1}$. The class space is the simplex, the Weyl group is the symmetric group, and the subgroups are the blocks of size two inside the $n$.
