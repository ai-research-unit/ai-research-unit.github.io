The honest summary is that about one sixth of this note is established, one third is algebra, and the remainder is assumption. The previous version did not say which was which, and that was the defect.

1. The One Established Relation

[PUBLISHED] Koide's relation, stated in 1981 for the charged leptons and not derived since, is a statement about two mass ratios. Printed in masses it is a sum of masses over the square of a sum of their roots; dividing top and bottom by $m_1$ leaves the masses together, and what remains is a function of the two ratios. [MEASURED] Writing $\rho_i=m_i/m_1$ with $m_1=m_e$, the PDG masses give

$$ Q=\frac{1+\rho_2+\rho_3}{\left(1+\sqrt{\rho_2}+\sqrt{\rho_3}\right)^{2}}=\frac{2}{3}. $$

Every number in the display is dimensionless, and no mass unit appears: a change of mass unit, or of mass standard, cancels between top and bottom. This is the form used for the rest of the note.

The relation holds to six parts in a million for the one triple it was written for. That is the whole empirical content of the charged-lepton ladder, and it is worth more than everything else in this note.

So the relation is a one-off, or it is a rule that quarks violate for a reason nobody has supplied. Both readings are open and this note keeps both.

2. Exact Algebra

Four statements below are exact. None needs a medium, a spectrum or a dynamics. They are the entire content of the closure language, and stating them plainly is what removes the appearance of derivation.

2.1 The bound. [ALGEBRA] For positive $m_i$ and $a_i=\sqrt{m_i/m_1}$,

$$ Q=\frac{\sum a_i^{2}}{\left(\sum a_i\right)^{2}}\le 1, $$

by Cauchy–Schwarz, with equality if and only if exactly one $a_i$ is nonzero. The normalisation by $m_1$ is free, since $Q$ is invariant under $a_i\to\lambda a_i$; it is made once here so that $a_1=1$ and nothing below carries a unit.

2.2 The rung count. [ALGEBRA] Setting $Q=2/N$ in 2.1 gives $N\ge2$. The case $N=2$ requires $Q=1$, hence one massless rung by the equality condition, which is excluded by data. So

$$ N\ge3 . $$

This is the only place the closure touches the number of generations, and it is weak: it excludes two and permits any count above two. It is not a derivation of three, and this note does not present it as one.

2.3 The variance form. [ALGEBRA] With $\bar a=\frac1N\sum a_i$ and $\operatorname{var}=\frac1N\sum(a_i-\bar a)^{2}$,

$$ Q=\frac{2}{N}\iff\operatorname{var}=\bar a^{2}, $$

so the root vector $\vec a$ sits at $45^{\circ}$ to the uniform direction. Measured for the leptons, $\operatorname{var}/\bar a^{2}=0.999\,982$ against $1$, which is 2.1 restated with the same $6\times10^{-6}$.

2.4 The circle. [ALGEBRA] For any $\mu>0$ and any $\theta$,

$$ a_i=\mu\left(1+\sqrt2\cos\!\left(\theta+\frac{2\pi i}{3}\right)\right) \;\Longrightarrow\; Q=\frac{2}{3}\quad\text{identically}, $$

because $\sum\cos=0$ and $\sum\cos^{2}=3/2$ for three equally spaced phases. This is exact for every $\theta$ and every $\mu$, and $\mu$ is the mean of the three $a_i$. For the charged leptons $\mu=24.782\,49$ in the normalisation $a_1=1$.

2.4 is the reason this programme is hard to assess, and the note should say it once and plainly: the circle contains no information beyond Koide. Two parameters, three masses, and one degree of freedom left over; the fit consumes that degree of freedom by construction. A test that the leptons lie on the circle is a test of $Q=2/3$ and of nothing else. Any claim that the circle explains or reproduces the closure is a circular claim, and this version of the note does not make it.

3. The Circle as an Ansatz

[ANSATZ] The physical hypothesis is not the circle, which is algebra, but the equal spacing of the phases, $2\pi/N$ in $\theta$. Nothing in the corpus derives that spacing. It is an assumption about a medium, and it enters at exactly one place.

The table is the whole assessment. A two-parameter family through three points with one identity built in has one degree of freedom and no predictive power. What the ansatz buys is a picture: three rungs on a circle, one near a node. What it costs is that the picture explains nothing, because it cannot fail except by $Q$ failing.

4. Parity: True Inside the Ansatz, False as an Exclusion

[ALGEBRA, inside the ansatz] For even $N$ the phases are antipodal in pairs, and $\cos(\theta+\pi)=-\cos\theta$, so

$$ a_i+a_{i+N/2}=2\mu \qquad\text{exactly, for every }\theta . $$

An even count therefore produces masses in degenerate pairs, symmetric about $\mu$ in the root, and so symmetric about $\mu^{2}$ in mass.

[WITHDRAWN] The previous version said this excludes every even count by parity and not by $\chi^{2}$, and called it independent of any fit. Both halves are wrong. 9. The Fourth Rung: Three Routes, Three Failures

[ALGEBRA applied to data] The question is whether a fourth charged-lepton mass is predictable. Three routes were tried; all three fail, and the failure is the result.

So a fourth mass cannot be predicted from the leptons' own numbers. Three data, two parameters, one constraint: the circle is already saturated. A fourth number needs a fourth input, and the only candidates are a second tower's data, a dynamical rule (which is §8), or a symmetry (which the corpus does not have for flavour).

A fourth route is recorded in §15, the shifted Koide $(m_2,m_3,m_4)$. It fails like the other three, and it fails in a way worth keeping, because the arithmetic is explicit and the failure is a number rather than a judgement.

10. What I Think

Opinion, separated from the rest and marked as opinion. Each line carries my confidence.

  1. Koide is the only real fact here. Confidence high. Everything else is a reparametrisation of it, an algebra exercise, or an assumption.
  2. The circle is a coordinate change, not a mechanism. Confidence certain. §2.4 settles it: the circle is Koide, so it cannot be evidence for anything, including itself.
  3. The $N\ge3$ argument is true and nearly content-free. Confidence certain. It excludes two and permits ten.
  4. The $45^{\circ}$ form is the one idea worth keeping. Confidence moderate. It converts why $2/3$ into why this vector sits at $45^{\circ}$ to the uniform direction, which is a geometric question with a possible answer, unlike the number itself.
  5. The continuum pass is numerology until a dispersion exists. Confidence high. Four stacked assumptions to produce one exponent is not a derivation, and presenting it publicly would damage the corpus.
  6. If the programme is ever completed, the missing piece is a boundary condition, not a mass formula. Guess. Fixed phase spacing smells like a quantisation condition on a finite tower, and no amount of fitting produces one.
  7. If nothing derives the phase spacing, this should be recorded as a coincidence with a good parametrisation. Opinion. Six parts in a million is striking, and it is also what one triple out of three gives when you look for a two-parameter pattern.

14. Two Coordinates of One Vector: Foot's Angle $45^{\circ}$ and the Phase $2/9$

Scope. This section was added after the note was closed. It answers one question the note left in the air, whether Foot's $45^{\circ}$ and the phase $2/9$ are two facts or one. They are one vector read in polar and azimuthal form. The section changes nothing above it: where it disagrees with §3 or §6, the disagreement is recorded in 14.13 and the earlier text is left standing, so the two readings can be compared.

The object is Koide's ratio, written in the two mass ratios $\rho_i=m_i/m_1$,

$$ Q=\frac{1+\rho_2+\rho_3}{\left(1+\sqrt{\rho_2}+\sqrt{\rho_3}\right)^{2}}, $$

with a square root on each term of the denominator. The section shows that the numerator is a squared length, the denominator is the square of one dot product, and the ratio between them is a function of a single angle. Every step is written out, because the content of the geometric reading is the cancellation at 14.4.

14.1 The two vectors

[PUBLISHED] R. Foot (hep-ph/9402242, 1994) makes the reading. Put the three square roots at the coordinates of a vector, and put the symmetric direction beside it:

$$ \vec a=\left(1,\sqrt{\rho_2},\sqrt{\rho_3}\right), \qquad \vec u=(1,1,1), $$

with $\rho_i=m_i/m_1$ the mass ratios of §1, so that $a_i=\sqrt{\rho_i}$ and $a_1=1$. Dividing every coordinate by $\sqrt{m_1}$ changes no ratio of lengths and no angle, so the normalisation costs nothing and removes every unit.

Call $\vec a$ the root vector and $\vec u$ the democratic vector. The name is Foot's: a democratic vector gives the same weight to every rung. Their squared lengths follow from their definitions, and nothing else in the section needs an assumption.

vector components squared length value for the leptons
$\vec a$ $\left(1,\sqrt{\rho_2},\sqrt{\rho_3}\right)$ $1+\rho_2+\rho_3$ $3684.997$
$\vec u$ $(1,1,1)$ $3$ $3$

Three choices are made in those two lines, and each matters.

  1. The coordinates are square roots, not masses. Only then is the numerator of $Q$ the squared norm of the vector.
  2. The second vector is $(1,1,1)$, not $(1,1,-1)$ and not a weighted vector. Only an unweighted sum reproduces $1+\sqrt{\rho_2}+\sqrt{\rho_3}$.
  3. The same vector serves all three rungs. That is why the formula is symmetric in the three masses, and why it cannot tell which rung is which.

14.2 Step 1: the numerator is the squared length

[ALGEBRA] The squared length of $\vec a$ is the sum of the squares of its coordinates, and each coordinate is a square root:

$$ |\vec a|^{2}=1^{2}+\left(\sqrt{\rho_2}\right)^{2}+\left(\sqrt{\rho_3}\right)^{2}=1+\rho_2+\rho_3 . $$

So the numerator of $Q$ is $|\vec a|^{2}$ and nothing else. Three consequences follow at once.

  • The numerator is a norm, so it is positive for any triple of positive masses. $Q$ is positive.
  • The numerator is quadratic in the roots. A common rescaling of the three masses rescales it by the same factor as the denominator, which is why 14.4 cancels.
  • Numerically, $|\vec a|^{2}=1+206.768\,28+3477.228\,28=3684.997$, and $|\vec a|=60.704\,17$.

14.3 Step 2: the denominator is the square of one dot product

[ALGEBRA] The dot product of the two vectors is one scalar,

$$ \vec u\cdot\vec a=1\cdot 1+1\cdot\sqrt{\rho_2}+1\cdot\sqrt{\rho_3}=1+\sqrt{\rho_2}+\sqrt{\rho_3}, $$

because every component of $\vec u$ equals one. Its square is the denominator of $Q$:

$$ \left(\vec u\cdot\vec a\right)^{2}=\left(1+\sqrt{\rho_2}+\sqrt{\rho_3}\right)^{2}. $$

Two remarks, both needed for the next step.

  • The dot product is not the length of $\vec a$. It is the length of $\vec a$ times the cosine of the angle to $\vec u$, times $|\vec u|$. The deficit is the cosine, and that is the only place the angle can enter.
  • The denominator is the square of a scalar, so it is not $|\vec a|^{2}|\vec u|^{2}$. The two differ by $\cos^{2}\varphi$, and that difference is the whole content of the geometric reading.

For the leptons, $\vec u\cdot\vec a=74.347\,47$ and $\left(\vec u\cdot\vec a\right)^{2}=5527.546$.

14.4 Step 3: the angle enters, and the length cancels

[ALGEBRA] Let $\varphi$ be the angle between $\vec a$ and $\vec u$. The definition of the dot product in terms of that angle is

$$ \vec u\cdot\vec a=|\vec u|\,|\vec a|\cos\varphi . $$

Square it:

$$ \left(\vec u\cdot\vec a\right)^{2}=|\vec u|^{2}\,|\vec a|^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi . $$

Insert $|\vec u|^{2}=3$:

$$ \left(\vec u\cdot\vec a\right)^{2}=3\,|\vec a|^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi . $$

Divide $|\vec a|^{2}$ by that expression. The length appears in the numerator and in the denominator, so it cancels:

$$ Q=\frac{|\vec a|^{2}}{\left(\vec u\cdot\vec a\right)^{2}} =\frac{|\vec a|^{2}}{3\,|\vec a|^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi} =\frac{1}{3\cos^{2}\varphi}. $$

The cancellation is the entire geometric content. $Q$ does not depend on how long $\vec a$ is. It depends on the direction of $\vec a$ and on nothing else. Two triples with proportional root vectors have the same $Q$ whatever their masses, and a check on the leptons confirms it: scaling all three masses by $100$ leaves $Q$ at $0.6666605$.

The inverse form is used everywhere below:

$$ \cos\varphi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3Q}}, \qquad \cos^{2}\varphi=\frac{1}{3Q}. $$

Since $0<\cos^{2}\varphi\le1$, the identity also bounds $Q$ from below. With $\cos^{2}\varphi=1$ the three roots are equal and $Q=1/3$; with $\cos\varphi=1/\sqrt3$, the Cauchy–Schwarz edge of §2.1, one root vanishes and $Q=1$. So

$$ \frac13\le Q\le1 . $$

§2.1 states the upper bound only. The lower bound $Q\ge1/3$ is the same identity read at the other end, and it says that no triple has roots flatter than the degenerate one.

14.7 The angle is the variance

[ALGEBRA] The angle of 14.5 has a statistical name. For the three coordinates of $\vec v$,

$$ \vec a-\bar a\,\vec u=\vec v, \qquad \sigma_a^{2}=\frac13\sum_i\left(a_i-\bar a\right)^{2}=\frac{|\vec v|^{2}}{3}, $$

because the mean of the three components of $\vec v$ is zero by construction. Dividing by $a_{\parallel}=\sqrt3\,\bar a$,

$$ \tan\varphi=\frac{|\vec v|}{a_{\parallel}}=\frac{\sqrt3\,\sigma_a}{\sqrt3\,\bar a}=\frac{\sigma_a}{\bar a}, \qquad Q=\frac{1+\tan^{2}\varphi}{3}. $$

Measured, $\sigma_a/\bar a=0.9999908$ and $\varphi=44.9997^{\circ}$. This is the warrant that §2.3 asserted without a proof: §2.3 wrote $\operatorname{var}/\bar a^{2}=0.999982$ and said the vector sits at $45^{\circ}$; the identity here is the reason, and the two numbers differ only because one is squared.

The form is worth keeping because it is scale-free. $\sigma_a/\bar a$ is invariant under $a_i\to\lambda a_i$, so Koide says the three roots are as spread as their own mean. That is the only shape statement the closure makes.

14.9 The phase is the azimuth

[ALGEBRA] The angle $\varphi$ fixes the direction of $\vec a$ relative to $\vec u$ but not its position around $\vec u$. A second angle is needed, and one representation supplies it. Every triple of roots has exactly one representation

$$ a_i=\mu+R\cos\left(\delta+\frac{2\pi i}{3}\right), \qquad i=1,2,3, $$

with $\mu=\bar a$ and $R\ge0$. Three parameters, three roots, no assumption: this is a change of coordinates, and it reproduces any triple exactly. Checked on $[1,2,4]$ and on $[1,1,10]$, both to machine precision, and on the leptons with $\mu=24.78249$, $R=35.04741$, $\delta=0.2222296$.

Two sums carry the whole of the closure in this language, and each is checked at $\delta=0$, where the cosines are $1$, $-1/2$, $-1/2$:

$$ \sum_i a_i^{2}=3\mu^{2}+\tfrac32 R^{2}, \qquad \left(\sum_i a_i\right)^{2}=9\mu^{2}, $$

$$ Q=\frac{3\mu^{2}+\tfrac32R^{2}}{9\mu^{2}}=\frac13\left(1+\frac{R^{2}}{2\mu^{2}}\right). $$

$\delta$ does not appear. $Q$ is blind to the phase, and the closure is a statement about the radius:

$$ Q=\frac23\iff R=\sqrt2\,\mu . $$

The ratio $R/(\sqrt2\mu)$ is $\tan\varphi$ of 14.5, so the radius and the polar angle are one number. The circle of §2.4 is the locus $R=\sqrt2\mu$; the phase is the position on it. That is the exact form of §2.4's sentence "the circle contains no information beyond Koide".

14.10 The $120^{\circ}$ spacing is a projection, not an assumption

[ALGEBRA] §3 calls the equal spacing an assumption. At $N=3$ it is not one, and the proof is three lines. Let $\hat e_i$ be the three coordinate axes of $\mathbb R^{3}$ and project each onto the plane orthogonal to $\hat u$:

$$ \vec p_i=\hat e_i-\left(\hat e_i\cdot\hat u\right)\hat u =\hat e_i-\tfrac13(1,1,1). $$

Then, because $\hat e_i\cdot\hat e_j=\delta_{ij}$ and $|\hat u|^{2}=1$,

$$ \vec p_i\cdot\vec p_j=\delta_{ij}-\tfrac13, \qquad |\vec p_i|^{2}=\tfrac23 . $$

For $i\neq j$ the cosine between the projections is $\left(-\tfrac13\right)/\left(\tfrac23\right)=-\tfrac12$, which is $120^{\circ}$. So the three roots are the components of one vector along three directions forced to be equally spaced. Nothing else could happen: the spacing is the dimension of the space, not a choice about it.

14.12 What the two lenses do not do

  1. They do not explain $2/3$. The angle is $2/3$; the phase parametrisation is a coordinate change. Both are exact and neither is a mechanism.
  2. They are not two independent facts. $\varphi$ and $\delta$ are the polar and azimuthal angles of one vector. A fit to one is a fit to the same three masses as the other.
  3. $2/9$ is not derived. No argument here produces it. The corpus's candidates live outside this section: a Green's-function constant $2\pi^{2}/9$ on a $Z_3$-sampled circle, with a free normalisation, and a hypercharge rule $\delta=(1+|Y|)/9$ that fits the leptons and the up quarks and fails its own sum rule. Both are quoted in articles_external/Koide/ and neither is adopted.
  4. $45^{\circ}$ is not fundamental because it is round. The invariant is $|\vec v|=a_{\parallel}$, equivalently $\sigma_a=\bar a$.
  5. The phase is not evidence of order replacing chaos. Three numbers to three parameters is a reparametrisation. The only content is that $\delta$ is small and close to a short fraction, and small numbers of that kind are common in fitted triples.
  6. Neither coordinate fixes the other. $Q$ is blind to $\delta$ by 14.9, and 14.13 shows that $Q$ does not fix the distance from the node.

14.14 What the section leaves open

The root vector has three coordinates: a scale $\mu$, a polar angle $\varphi$, and an azimuth $\delta$. Koide fixes $\varphi=45^{\circ}$. Nothing in the note fixes $\delta$, and $\delta$ is where the remaining content sits, because $\varphi$ is $Q$ under another name.

That reframes the target of §11. A mechanism for flavour must produce two numbers and not one: a shape ratio $\sigma_a/\bar a$ equal to $1$, and a phase $\delta$ equal to a short fraction of a radian. The first is a ratio and can come from a projection or an alignment. The second is an angle and needs a boundary condition on a finite tower, which the corpus does not have. The gap named at the end of §10 is therefore unchanged in kind and narrower in statement.

14.16 Is the Value $2/3$ an Accident? A Counted Answer

§10 item 7 records the relation as "a coincidence with a good parametrisation". That verdict is too weak, and this subsection replaces it with a number. The question has an answer, the answer is about one in a thousand, and the standing difficulty is not improbability at all.

14.16.1 What has to be counted

Three triples are in the data: the charged leptons and the two quark sectors. One of them closes to six parts in a million and the other two miss by $0.18$ and $0.065$. The claim "accident" says that the closing triple is an unremarkable draw from the space of possible triples. That claim is testable, because the space of triples has a measure.

[ALGEBRA] Fix the direction of the root vector and let it be uniform in the positive octant, which is the natural measure for three positive masses with no scale. Since $Q$ depends on direction only (14.4), a direction has one coordinate, $c=\cos\varphi\in[1/\sqrt3,1]$, uniform in $c$. With $Q=1/(3c^{2})$,

$$ f_Q(q)=\frac{q^{-3/2}}{2\sqrt3\left(1-1/\sqrt3\right)}=0.6830\,q^{-3/2}, \qquad \int_{1/3}^{1}f_Q\,dq=1 . $$

The density falls from $3.55$ at $Q=1/3$ to $0.683$ at $Q=1$, so the democratic end is the crowded end. At $Q=2/3$ the density is $1.2548$.

The window is fixed by the data, not chosen: $Q$ is measured to $\pm6.2\times10^{-6}$ of $2/3$. The probability that one structureless triple lands in that window is

$$ P=2\,(6.2\times10^{-6})\,(1.2548)=1.56\times10^{-5}, $$

which is one in $64{,}000$. The answer moves by less than a factor of two under a different prior on $Q$: uniform in $Q$ gives one in $54{,}000$, and a Monte Carlo over a uniform simplex of roots gives one in $100{,}000$. The scale of the answer is $10^{-5}$, and it does not depend on the choice of measure.

14.16.2 The correction that has to be made, and the answer it gives

The figure of 14.16.1 is not yet the right one, because a hit on any short fraction would have been noticed and written up. The window has to be multiplied by the number of short fractions that sit in the range.

[ALGEBRA] The fractions with denominator at most nine inside $[1/3,1]$ number twenty:

$$ \tfrac13,\ \tfrac38,\ \tfrac25,\ \tfrac37,\ \tfrac49,\ \tfrac12,\ \tfrac59,\ \tfrac47,\ \tfrac35,\ \tfrac58,\ \tfrac23,\ \tfrac57,\ \tfrac34,\ \tfrac79,\ \tfrac45,\ \tfrac56,\ \tfrac67,\ \tfrac78,\ \tfrac89,\ 1 . $$

$$ P_{\text{corrected}}=3\times20\times1.86\times10^{-5}=1.1\times10^{-3}. $$

The honest figure is one in nine hundred, which is about three sigma. It is not one in $64{,}000$. A reader who quotes the smaller number has counted one candidate value after seeing which value won.

Three sigma is a real hit and not a proof. It is the level at which a result is worth pursuing and not the level at which a result is believed.

15. Increment: The Shifted Koide $(m_2,m_3,m_4)$, and Why Recovering the Electron Is Not Evidence

This section is an increment, not a result. It records one substitution, the algebra it forces, and the two questions the substitution raises. The first question is whether the relation still says anything once shifted; the answer is that it says the same thing twice and nothing more. The second is whether the shifted relation predicts a fourth mass; it does, and the prediction is dead on arrival. The arithmetic is kept because it took an afternoon and it settles both questions.

15.3 Is it circular? Three separate questions

The word circular hides three different questions, and they have three different answers.

Question 1: was the value $2/3$ fitted to the leptons? [PUBLISHED] No. Koide stated the rational $2/3$ in 1981, before any fit, and §14.16 counts what that costs. So the original agreement of $6.2\times10^{-6}$ is evidence, and this section does not touch it.

Question 2: is the recovery of the electron new evidence? [ALGEBRA] No, and this is provable in one line. Write the quadratic of 15.1 for an arbitrary third root $z$:

$$ z^{2}-4S z+3T-2S^{2}=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{a_2^{2}+a_3^{2}+z^{2}}{\left(a_2+a_3+z\right)^{2}}=\frac{2}{3}. $$

The left side is the statement "the pair $(a_2,a_3)$ completes to $z$ with $Q=2/3$"; the right side is "the triple satisfies $Q=2/3$". They are the same statement. So the recovery is not evidence additional to the original — but "not additional" is not "empty", and §15.8 corrects a draft of this paragraph that said otherwise. The shared statement is one genuine constraint on three masses, and it is worth what §1 says it is worth. A numerical check confirms the implication runs both ways: over $20\,000$ random triples, the completion never returns the third mass unless $Q=2/3$ holds, and over $2\,000$ constructed triples with $Q=2/3$ it returns it every time.

Question 3: is it circular in the vicious sense? [ALGEBRA] Not quite, and the distinction matters. A vicious circle is a test whose input contains its own output. Here the input is two measured masses and the output is a third measured mass that was not used. That is a legitimate consistency check, and it passes. What it is not is an independent one. A relation with three slots and one identity can be read from any two slots, and every reading is the same reading.

The counting, stated correctly. The flavour block has three charged-lepton masses and one relation on them. Two of the three are free at the level of raw data, and one is fixed by the relation. That is a real reduction of one parameter, and this section must say so plainly: it is the grade-two claim of the corpus, not a null result. What the shifted reading does not supply is a second constraint. Substitute $(m_2,m_3,m_4)$ and the relation is still one equation on one unknown, because it is the same equation. One constraint can remove one parameter. It cannot remove two, and it cannot be spent twice.

[ALGEBRA] If the reader wants the general statement: for $Q=2/N$ and a tower of $N$ rungs, the relation is one equation on $N$ masses, so the tower has $N-1$ free shapes and the relation fixes one. Reading the relation from any pair returns the remaining root, always. That is §2.3 in another coordinate, and it is why §3's table has "predictions beyond $Q$: none".

But "one constraint" is not "no constraint". The distinction is the whole of this section, and an earlier draft of it got the distinction wrong by writing that the shifted reading "predicts nothing". It predicts the third mass, it predicts it correctly, and it is worth the same as the original statement. §15.8 corrects the draft.

15.8 "Recoverable" Is What "Related" Means: correcting the draft of 15.3

[ALGEBRA] An earlier draft of 15.3 said that recovering the electron "adds exactly zero". That sentence was wrong, and it is worth putting the correction in the record rather than editing it away.

If $m_e$ follows from $(m_\mu,m_\tau)$, then the three masses are related. The previous draft used a parameter count to argue the recovery was empty, and the count says the opposite of what I claimed: one relation on three masses removes one parameter. 16. The $Z_3$ Cosine: Three Roots as One Circle

Scope. This section develops the parametrisation

$$ a_i=\mu\left(1+\sqrt2\cos\left(\delta+\frac{2\pi i}{3}\right)\right), \qquad i=1,2,3, $$

which §14 used in 14.9 to 14.11 without deriving. Three things change when it is written out. The form is a change of basis and not an assumption. Its two shape parameters are the second and third moments of the roots, which is why Koide fixes one and is blind to the other. And the standard version, with the $\sqrt2$ written inside, is not a general parametrisation at all: it is the Koide surface, with $k=1$ already imposed.

16.1 The general form, and the one the literature quotes

[PUBLISHED] The parametrisation and its normalisation are Koide's, and the version with a free coefficient is the one used in the papers. Ženczykowski writes it as

$$ \sqrt{m_j}=\sqrt{M_f}\left(1+\sqrt2\,k_f\cos\left(\frac{2\pi j}{3}+\delta_f\right)\right), \qquad j=1,2,3, $$

with three parameters: the scale $\sqrt{M_f}$, the coefficient $k_f$, and the phase $\delta_f$. Read in this note's variable the same statement is $a_j=\bar a\left(1+\sqrt2\,k_f\cos\left(2\pi j/3+\delta_f\right)\right)$ with $a_j=\sqrt{m_j/m_1}$, and the scale $\sqrt{M_f}$ is what drops out of every ratio; the two forms carry one statement and differ only in where the scale is written. He states the two properties that matter. Any triple needs three parameters, so the form is general. And Koide's relation becomes $\frac13\left(1+k_f^{2}\right)$, so it fixes $k_f$ and is independent of $\delta_f$. The measured values are $k_L=1$ for the leptons, $k_D\approx1.08$ and $k_U\approx1.25$ for the down and up quarks at $2$ GeV, and $k_\nu\le0.81$ for neutrinos.

the modulus of the first mode $R$ $R$ $R=\sqrt2\,k\,\mu$
the coefficient $k$ — $k=R/(\sqrt2\mu)=\sqrt{3Q-1}=\tan\varphi$

So $k_f$ is exactly $\tan\varphi$ of 14.4 and exactly $\sigma_a/\bar a$ of 14.7. The literature's coefficient and this note's angle are one number, and neither paper says so, because neither computes the variance. The form quoted at the head of this section has $k=1$ absorbed into the $\sqrt2$; that is legitimate only because the leptons satisfy it to $10^{-5}$. For the quarks the coefficient is not one and cannot be absorbed.

16.2 It is a change of basis, and it is complete

[ALGEBRA] Three parameters for three roots. The map is invertible and the inverse is explicit:

$$ \mu=\bar a,\qquad R=\sqrt{\tfrac23\sum_i\left(a_i-\bar a\right)^{2}}=\sqrt2\,\sigma_a,\qquad \tan\delta=\frac{\sqrt3\left(a_2-a_1\right)}{2a_3-a_2-a_1}, $$

with the rungs ordered $a_1<a_2<a_3$ as in 14.11. Checked on $[1,2,4]$ and on $[1,1,10]$ the reconstruction is exact to machine precision, and on the leptons the error is $1.6\times10^{-14}$.

The reason the form is natural is that it is a discrete Fourier transform. For a function on $Z_3$ the real Fourier basis has exactly three functions, $1$, $\cos$, $\sin$; there is no third distinct mode. So writing three real roots as one constant plus one sinusoid is the only decomposition available, and it is forced rather than chosen. With $\hat a_k=\frac13\sum_{j=0}^{2}a_j e^{-2\pi ijk/3}$ and $(a_0,a_1,a_2)=(a_e,a_\mu,a_\tau)$,

$$ \hat a_0=\mu,\qquad \left|\hat a_1\right|=\frac{R}{2},\qquad \arg\hat a_1=\delta+\frac{2\pi}{3}, $$

verified to ten digits: $\mu=24.782\,489$, $|\hat a_1|=17.523\,705=R/2$, and $\arg\hat a_1=2.316\,6247=\delta+2\pi/3$. The offset $2\pi/3$ is the labelling convention of 14.11 and carries no content.

So $\mu$ is the mean and $(\frac{R}{2},\delta)$ are the modulus and argument of the first Fourier coefficient of the root vector. The $2/9$ is the argument of a Fourier mode. This is a sharper statement of 14.9's "$\delta$ is the azimuth", and it is the cleanest form of the claim that $\delta$ is a coordinate.

16.3 The two shape parameters are the second and third moments

[ALGEBRA] Shift to the mean and compute the moments of the three roots:

$$ \sum_i\left(a_i-\mu\right)^{2}=\tfrac32R^{2}, \qquad \sum_i\left(a_i-\mu\right)^{3}=\tfrac34R^{3}\cos3\delta . $$

Both are verified on the leptons: $1842.481$ against $\frac32R^{2}=1842.481$, and $25373.6$ against $\frac34R^{3}\cos3\delta=25373.6$. Dividing by three,

$$ \sigma_a^{2}=\frac{R^{2}}{2}, \qquad \cos3\delta=\frac{4}{3R^{3}}\sum_i\left(a_i-\mu\right)^{3}. $$

This is the sentence the whole section turns on. The coefficient $k$ is the variance of the roots in units of their mean; the phase $\cos3\delta$ is their third moment. Koide is a statement about the second moment; $\delta$ is the skewness direction, and it is the third moment because a symmetric triple has none.

16.5 The cubic whose roots are the three $a_i$

[ALGEBRA] The three roots $a_i$ are the roots of one cubic, and writing it out shows exactly how much the phase can do. Using the three symmetric sums,

$$ \prod_i a_i=\mu^{3}-\tfrac34\mu R^{2}+\tfrac14R^{3}\cos3\delta, $$

the polynomial is

$$ t^{3}-3\mu\,t^{2}+\left(3\mu^{2}-\tfrac34R^{2}\right)t-\left(\mu^{3}-\tfrac34\mu R^{2}+\tfrac14R^{3}\cos3\delta\right)=0 . $$

For the leptons, in the normalisation $a_1=1$, it is $t^{3}-74.347470\,t^{2}+921.274719\,t-847.927249$, and each root evaluates it to order $10^{-11}$.

$\delta$ enters only through $\cos3\delta$. So the unordered triple $\{a_1,a_2,a_3\}$ is a function of $(\mu,R,\cos3\delta)$ and of nothing else. The phase has a sixfold ambiguity, and it is the symmetry group of three objects:

$$ \delta\sim\pm\delta+\frac{2\pi n}{3},\qquad n=0,1,2, $$

six images, verified numerically: $\delta=0.22223$, $-\delta$, $\delta+120^{\circ}$, $-\delta+120^{\circ}$ and $\delta+240^{\circ}$ all give the same three roots in some order, and all give $\cos3\delta=0.7858735$.

The two generators have a meaning worth stating. Adding $120^{\circ}$ relabels the rungs cyclically and changes nothing physical. Sending $\delta\to-\delta$ mirrors the triple, which is why the sign of $\delta$ is not a measurable quantity. That is the origin of 14.11's warning that $2/9$ means nothing without a convention: the convention is a choice of one point in a sixfold orbit.

16.6 Positivity, the window, and the cone

[ALGEBRA] Each root is $\mu\left(1+\sqrt2k\cos\left(\delta+2\pi i/3\right)\right)$, so all three are positive exactly when

$$ \cos\left(\delta+\frac{2\pi i}{3}\right)>-\frac{1}{\sqrt2\,k} \qquad\text{for } i=1,2,3 . $$

At $k=1$ the threshold is $-1/\sqrt2$ and the window is $\delta\in(-15^{\circ},15^{\circ})$ modulo $120^{\circ}$, as 14.13 states, with the node at $15^{\circ}$. At $k=0$ the window is everything and the triple is degenerate. So increasing $k$ widens the hierarchy and narrows the allowed phase, and $k=1$ sits between the two limits.

Two consequences.

  • $k\le\sqrt2$ is the point at which the window closes at $\delta=0$; beyond it no triple of positive roots exists. That is 14.4's $Q\le1$ again.
  • Roots may be allowed to go negative, as 1111.7232 does for the strange–charm–bottom tuple. In this language that is $\delta$ outside the window, and the triple then sits on the far side of the cone of 14.8. The parametrisation does not care; only positivity does.

One measurement of the distance to the boundary is worth recording, since it is exact and slightly surprising. With the up-quark masses at $2$ GeV the coefficient is $k=1.2430$, the phase is $\delta=0.0743$ rad, and the boundary sits at $0.0816$ rad. The up sector lies $0.4^{\circ}$ from the value of $\delta$ at which $m_u$ vanishes. That is not a new coincidence: it is another way of saying $m_u/m_t\approx1.3\times10^{-5}$, seen from the node. It does explain why the up-sector phase is the most sensitive number in the table of 16.7: it is read off a root that is nearly zero.

16.8 The one systematic attempt: $\delta$ from hypercharge

[PUBLISHED] Ženczykowski proposes that the phase is fixed by two quantum numbers,

$$ \delta\left(I_3=-\tfrac12,Y\right)=\frac{1}{9}\left(1+\left|Y\right|\right), \qquad \delta\left(I_3=+\tfrac12,Y\right)=\frac{1}{9}\left(1-\left|Y\right|\right), $$

with the sum rule $\delta_L+\delta_\nu=\delta_U+\delta_D$. The convention on $Y$ is part of the claim, and it is the doubled one, $Y=2\left(Q-T_3\right)$; in that convention

And it does so without any dynamics: it is a numerology of the Standard Model's own labels, exact for three sectors and wrong for the fourth, and it predicts $\delta_\nu=0$, which the form then forces onto a degenerate neutrino pair.

16.9 The second relation $\kappa=1/486$ is not independent

[ALGEBRA] Koide's review states a second relation beside the first,

$$ \kappa\equiv\frac{\sqrt{m_em_\mu m_\tau}}{\left(\sqrt{m_e}+\sqrt{m_\mu}+\sqrt{m_\tau}\right)^{3}}=\frac{\det\Phi}{[\Phi]^{3}}=\frac{1}{2\cdot3^{5}}=\frac{1}{486}, $$

that is, in the two mass ratios of §1,

$$ \kappa=\frac{\sqrt{\rho_2\rho_3}}{\left(1+\sqrt{\rho_2}+\sqrt{\rho_3}\right)^{3}}, $$

where every quantity is a ratio and the mass unit cancels as it does in $Q$. The value $486=2\cdot3^{5}$ is offered as a combinatorial value. In the $Z_3$ language $\kappa$ has a closed form, because it is a symmetric function of the roots:

$$ \kappa=\frac{1}{27}\left(1-\tfrac32k^{2}+\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}k^{3}\cos3\delta\right), $$

verified exactly: the formula gives $0.00206329$ against the direct $0.00206329$.

Three consequences follow, and the third is the important one.

  1. The measured value is $1/484.66$, not $1/486$. The gap is $0.28\%$, which is two hundred times the $1.4\times10^{-5}$ of the first relation. On the same data, $\kappa$ is a weak statement where $Q=2/3$ is a strong one.
  2. $\kappa=1/486$ is a statement about $\delta$, not a new fact. Setting $k=1$ and $\kappa=1/486$ gives $\cos3\delta=0.78567$, hence $\delta=0.222337$, against the measured $0.222230$ and $2/9=0.222222$. So $\kappa=1/486$ picks out a phase between the measured one and $2/9$, at a precision of $10^{-4}$.
  3. It cannot be independent. A triple has two shape parameters, so $\kappa$ is a function of $(k,\delta)$ and nothing else. There are at most two independent relations among three masses beyond the choice of scale, and $Q$ and $\kappa$ use both. A third would be a consequence.

That is the count 14.16 needed: the two relations are not two coincidences, they are the two coordinates. $Q=2/3$ fixes $k$; $\kappa$ fixes $\cos3\delta$; together they fix the triple up to scale. Calling both "amazing" double-counts the same evidence, and calling $2/9$ a third coincidence over-counts it again.

16.10 What the form does not do

  1. It does not produce $2/9$. The parametrisation is exact for every triple, so nothing in it selects one phase. Everything in 16.7 and 16.8 is a fit or a rule, and the rule misses a sector.
  2. The $\sqrt2$ in the quoted form is Koide, not dynamics. Written as $\mu\left(1+\sqrt2\cos(\cdots)\right)$ the formula contains the relation it is used to test; the general form needs $k$, and $k$ is the thing to be explained.
  3. It is not evidence for a tower. The $2\pi/3$ spacing is the dimension of the space, by 14.10, so the presence of $2\pi/3$ in the formula says nothing about three generations being generated dynamically.
  4. It does not remove the ordering puzzle. By 16.5 the unordered triple depends on $\cos3\delta$ only, so which rung carries which $i$ is pure convention; the physical content of the phase is one number in $[0,\pi/3]$.
  5. The one route that computes $2/9$ is outside this language. Shulga's Green's function on a $Z_3$-sampled circle gives $\sum_{n\ge1}\left(1-\cos2\pi n/3\right)/n^{2}=2\pi^{2}/9$, an exact lattice constant, and then $\theta_\ell=-2\lambda_B/9$ with $\lambda_B$ free. That is the only derivation in the folder, and it is a derivation up to one coefficient.

17. The General Form: $N$ Rungs, and Why the Phase Is a Circle Only at $N=3$

Scope. §16 wrote the parametrisation for three roots and said, in passing, that the $Z_3$ cosine is a Fourier transform. This section does the same job for any number of rungs. The general form is not an extrapolation of the $Z_3$ formula: it is the same Fourier decomposition written once, with $N$ in place of $3$, and the $Z_3$ cosine is the special case $N=3$. Two results come out that §16 could not state. The first is that Koide's condition is dimension-free: $\sigma_a=\bar a$ in every $N$, and $2/N$ follows. The second is a reason why $2/9$ exists at all, and why it cannot be generalised.

17.1 The general form

[ALGEBRA] Write the $N$ roots $a_i=\sqrt{\rho_i}$, $i=0,\dots,N-1$. The real Fourier basis on $Z_N$ has exactly $N$ functions, so the decomposition

$$ a_i=\mu+\sum_{r=1}^{\lfloor (N-1)/2\rfloor}R_r\cos\left(\phi_r+\frac{2\pi r i}{N}\right) \;+\; \begin{cases} \beta\,(-1)^{i}, & N \text{ even},\\[2pt] 0, & N \text{ odd}, \end{cases} $$

is exact for every spectrum, with $1+2\lfloor (N-1)/2\rfloor+\left[N\text{ even}\right]=N$ real parameters. Here $\mu$ is the mean of the roots, $R_r\ge0$, and $\beta\in\mathbb{R}$ is signed. I verified the round trip on random spectra for $N=1,\dots,7$: the largest error is $1.1\times10^{-14}$.

Three remarks on the shape of this.

  1. The last term is real, so the even case has no phase in its top mode, only a sign. Fixing the sign convention is what makes $N=2$ a one-parameter family, below.
  2. The modes are orthogonal over $Z_N$: $\sum_i\cos\left(\phi_r+2\pi r i/N\right)\cos\left(\phi_s+2\pi s i/N\right)=\frac{N}{2}\delta_{rs}$ for $r,s<N/2$, and the Nyquist mode has norm $N$. That is what makes 17.2 a two-line proof.
  3. $N=3$ is the only case with one mode. Then $\lfloor (N-1)/2\rfloor=1$, there is no relative root to choose, and the shape is $\mu$, one $R_1$, one $\phi_1$. Dividing out the scale leaves a point on a circle. That is where $\delta$ comes from, and it is where it stops.

Normalising the shape out of the radial parameters gives the form that carries the physics:

$$ \boxed{\;a_i=\bar a\left(1+k\,n_i\right),\qquad \sum_i n_i=0,\quad \sum_i n_i^{2}=N,\quad k=\frac{\sigma_a}{\bar a}\;} $$

with $\sigma_a$ the standard deviation of the roots. For $N=3$ this is $\mu\left(1+\sqrt2k\cos\left(\delta+2\pi i/3\right)\right)$ times nothing else, which is 16.1 with the relation between $k$ and $R$ made explicit.

17.2 The proof of $Q_N$, in two lines, for every $N$

[ALGEBRA] By the parallel-axis identity, which needs only $\sum_i\left(a_i-\bar a\right)=0$,

$$ \sum_i a_i^{2}=N\bar a^{2}+\sum_i\left(a_i-\bar a\right)^{2}=N\bar a^{2}\left(1+\frac{\sigma_a^{2}}{\bar a^{2}}\right), \qquad \left(\sum_i a_i\right)^{2}=N^{2}\bar a^{2}. $$

Therefore

$$ Q_N\equiv\frac{\sum_i a_i^{2}}{\left(\sum_i a_i\right)^{2}}=\frac{1}{N}\left(1+k^{2}\right), \qquad k=\frac{\sigma_a}{\bar a}=\sqrt{NQ_N-1}. $$

Verified on $500$ random spectra for each $N$ from $1$ to $7$: the largest relative error is $5\times10^{-16}$.

This is the general form of Koide's relation, and it is dimension-free. Everything §14 derived for $N=3$ is the case $N=3$ of one statement.

statement $N=3$ (§14, §16) general $N$
Koide $Q=2/3$ $Q_N=2/N$
coefficient $k=1$ $k=1$
variance $\sigma_a=\bar a$ $\sigma_a=\bar a$
angle to the diagonal $\varphi=45^{\circ}$ $\varphi=45^{\circ}$
the value $Q$ $(1+k^{2})/3$ $(1+k^{2})/N$

So Koide's relation is exactly "the spread of the roots equals their mean", and the value $2/N$ is a consequence of the $1/N$ in the normalisation, not a separate fact. The $2$ in $2/N$ is $k^{2}=1$.

17.3 The bounds, and why $N=2$ cannot close

[ALGEBRA] Two limits bracket the coefficient.

  • $k=0$ is the degenerate spectrum, $Q_N=1/N$.
  • $k$ is largest when one root carries everything, $a_0\to\infty$ with the other $N-1$ equal. Then $\sigma_a/\bar a\to\sqrt{N-1}$, and $Q_N\to1$.

$$ 0\le k\le\sqrt{N-1}, \qquad \frac{1}{N}\le Q_N\le 1 . $$

Verified: with $a=(A,1,\dots,1)$ and $A=10^{6}$, $k$ equals $\sqrt{N-1}$ to six digits for $N=2,\dots,7$ — $0.999998$, $1.414209$, $1.732044$, $1.999990$, $2.236055$, $2.449473$. The upper bound on $k$ is the bound $Q\le1$ of 14.4, now for every $N$.

At $N=2$ the parametrisation is explicit and the closure is empty:

$$ a_1=\bar a(1+k),\qquad a_2=\bar a(1-k),\qquad k=\frac{\left|a_1-a_2\right|}{a_1+a_2}, \qquad Q_2=\frac{1+k^{2}}{2}. $$

So $Q_2=2/2=1$ requires $k=1$ exactly, and $k=1$ means $a_2=0$. Verified: at $k=1$ the roots are $(2\bar a,0)$.

Hence $Q=2/N$ with all masses strictly positive requires $N\ge3$, and this is a cleaner proof than 2.2's. The reason is not the arithmetic of $2/N$ but the fact that a two-point set cannot have a spread equal to its mean without losing a member. For $N\ge3$ it can: at the most uniform direction, $k=1$ leaves a smallest root of $0.2929\bar a$ at $N=3$ and $0.5528\bar a$ at $N=6$.

17.4 The closure is $45^{\circ}$ in every dimension

[ALGEBRA] With $\vec u=(1,\dots,1)$ and $\vec a$ in $N$ dimensions, $\vec u\cdot\vec a=\sqrt{N}\left|\vec a\right|\cos\varphi$, so

$$ Q_N=\frac{\left|\vec a\right|^{2}}{\left(\vec u\cdot\vec a\right)^{2}}=\frac{1}{N\cos^{2}\varphi}. $$

So Foot's angle is not a three-dimensional accident:

$$ Q_N=\frac{2}{N}\iff\cos^{2}\varphi=\frac12\iff\varphi=45^{\circ}, $$

in every dimension. Verified at the closure point for $N=2,\dots,6$: $Q_N=2/N$ to $10^{-8}$ and $\varphi=45.0000^{\circ}$ each time.

What changes with $N$ is the range of $\varphi$. It runs from $0$ on the diagonal to $\arccos\left(1/\sqrt N\right)$ in the fully hierarchical limit, which is $45.0000^{\circ}$ at $N=2$, $54.7356^{\circ}$ at $N=3$, $60.0000^{\circ}$ at $N=4$ and tends to $90^{\circ}$. So $45^{\circ}$ is interior for $N\ge3$ and is the endpoint at $N=2$, which is a third way to reach 17.3: the closure sits exactly where the two-point spectrum loses a member. The cone 14.4 drew is the locus $\varphi=45^{\circ}$, and it exists in every dimension; the leptons sit on it at $N=3$.

The $N=3$ row is 16.1 exactly. The $N=4$ row is the first case with two independent shape numbers beyond $k$, and it shows why nothing like $2/9$ can be expected there: there is a second angle to be fixed, and no single number plays its role.

17.6 The phase lives on $S^{N-2}$

[ALGEBRA] The direction vector $\vec n$ in 17.1 has a zero sum, which puts it in an $(N-1)$-dimensional hyperplane, and a fixed norm, which cuts a sphere there. So it ranges over $S^{N-2}$:

$N$ hyperplane direction on angles what the phase is
$1$ $0$-dimensional a point $0$ nothing to fix
$2$ $1$-dimensional $S^{0}$ $0$ a sign, not an angle
$3$ $2$-dimensional $S^{1}$ $1$ $\delta$, a circle

$5$ $4$-dimensional $S^{3}$ $3$ three angles

$\delta$ is a phase because $N-2=1$, and $N-2=1$ happens only at $N=3$. This is the sharp form of the point §16 made about the Fourier basis, and it answers a question the literature leaves implicit: why is there a single phase angle to be explained at all?

  1. At $N=2$ there is no continuous shape parameter besides $k$, so a "phase" would have to be a sign.
  2. At $N=3$ there is exactly one angle, and the relation $Q=2/3$ fixes $k$ without touching it. So the phase is free, one-dimensional, and measurable — the three conditions for something like $2/9$ to be a puzzle.
  3. At $N\ge4$ the shape is a point on a sphere of dimension at least two, so even if some analogue of the closure held, its "phase" would be a pair of numbers, and a claim like $\delta=2/9$ would be incomplete rather than wrong.

So $2/9$ is a quantity that exists only because there are three generations. That is a different statement from §14's, which is that the $120^{\circ}$ spacing is a projection. Both are true: the spacing comes from the dimension of the space, and the phase is one coordinate of a circle that exists only in that dimension.

17.7 What the general form settles

[ALGEBRA] Four items from earlier sections now have one-line proofs.

  1. $N\ge3$ (2.2, 17.3). $Q=2/N$ with positive masses needs $k=1$, and $k=1$ at $N=2$ kills a root. Arithmetic, not dynamics.
  2. The midpoint (14.16.3). $Q_N\in\left[1/N,1\right]$, so the midpoint is $\left(1+1/N\right)/2$, and $2/N$ equals it only when $\frac{2}{N}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{N}\right)$, i.e. $N=3$. The uniqueness claim of 14.16.3 is now a one-line check rather than a numerical observation. As an aside, $N=4$ has its own distinction — there $2/N=1/\sqrt N$, the geometric mean of the endpoints — which shows the pattern of 14.16 is not that $2/N$ is privileged, but that $N=3$ is.
  3. The count in 14.16.7 and 16.9. A spectrum with $N$ members has $N-1$ shape parameters. At $N=3$ that is two, and $Q$ plus $\kappa$ use both. The count was done at $N=3$; it is now a special case of "$N-1$ shape parameters, of which $Q$ uses one".
  4. Foot's angle (14.4, 17.4). $\varphi=45^{\circ}$ is the closure in every dimension, so the cone is not a feature of three-dimensional space.

17.8 What it does not settle

  1. It does not produce $2/9$ at $N=3$. The general form is exact for every spectrum in every dimension, so it selects no phase. The phase count changes, the derivation does not appear.
  2. The number of rungs is put in by hand. $N$ is an input, so the statement "$2/9$ exists only because $N=3$" says nothing about why $N=3$. The relation between the two facts is one of consistency, not of explanation.
  3. It says nothing about which sector closes. $Q=2/N$ at $N=3$ is satisfied by the leptons and not by either quark triplet, and the general form has no way to prefer one sector: all three are $N=3$ spectra.
  4. The $N\to\infty$ limit is not a continuum limit here. Sending $N$ large with $k$ fixed gives $Q_N\to0$, so the closure value degenerates rather than tending to anything; §8's continuum pass is a different construction.

18. The Mechanism: Why the Lepton Has No Flavour to Change

Scope. Sections 14 to 17 compute the shape of a closed tower of roots. They take the rungs as given and ask how their masses are spaced. None of them asks a different question, which this section does: what turns one rung into another? For the leptons the answer is nothing, and the reason is not dynamical. It is that the only rotation in the gauge sector is a rotation of the spatial triple, and the lepton is the axis of that rotation and not a member of it.

This is the mechanism behind the statement in Lepton Flavour in the Four-Colours Model that there is no flavour change. It is short, it is exact given one declaration, and its exactness is the point: a claim of "no violation" should rest on a computed stabiliser, not on a null measurement.

18.1 The split, and the one declaration it needs

[ALGEBRA] The carrier is four-dimensional over $\mathbb C$, spanned by the biquaternion units in the order $(e_t,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z)$. The algebra supplies exactly one canonical splitting into a line and a hyperplane:

$$ \mathbf 4=\mathbb C e_t\oplus B_V,\qquad B_V=\operatorname{span}\{\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z\}. $$

It is canonical because $e_t=1$ is the identity: it commutes with everything, so the line it spans is invariant under every inner derivation the algebra has, and its orthogonal complement is invariant with it. No other unit has that property — the three $\vec e_a$ square to $-e_t$ and permute among themselves, so no line they span survives the action.

[ANSATZ] The declaration is (A) of the corpus: the line is the lepton and the hyperplane is the three quark colours. Nothing in the algebra prefers this labelling; it is the adoption the model rests on, and it is tagged here for the same reason it is tagged in The Four Colours Left–Right Model.

[ALGEBRA] The two halves are not the same kind of object, and their asymmetry is the asymmetry between the scalar unit and the vector units. $e_t$ is real, central, and squares to $+1$. The $\vec e_a$ are imaginary, anticommuting, and square to $-e_t$. So the lepton direction is temporal and the three colour directions are spatial, and everything below is a consequence of that one difference.

18.2 Colour is the stabiliser of the line

[ALGEBRA] Count the generators that annihilate $e_t$. Written on the quartet, the fifteen generators of $\mathfrak{su}(4)$ split by what they do to the line:

$$ \operatorname{Stab}_{\mathfrak{su}(4)}(\mathbb C e_t)=\mathfrak{su}(3),\qquad \dim=8 . $$

These eight act on $B_V$ and give zero on the line. They are the colour group, and the identification is not a choice: the subgroup of $\mathrm{SU}(4)$ that fixes a line is $\mathrm{SU}(3)$, which is why the frame is $\mathrm{SU}(4)$ and not $\mathrm{SU}(3)\times\mathrm U(1)$.

The reading matters. The statement "the three colours turn into each other" is exactly the statement that eight generators rotate $B_V$ and leave $\mathbb C e_t$ alone. A rotation of the triple is a rotation about the line, and an axis of rotation does not move. The leptons are the axis.

18.3 The other seven: one phase and six mixings

[ALGEBRA] The remaining seven generators do not fix the line, and they do not all do the same thing to it.

Of the other seven generators, one is the phase $iD$ with eigenvalues $(-3,1,1,1)$ — abelian, so it multiplies and never mixes — and six are the leptoquark directions, which take $e_t$ into $B_V$. So

$$ 15=8+1+6 . $$

The three pieces, with what each does to the lepton line:

block $\dim$ what it does to $\mathbb C e_t$ name
$\mathfrak{su}(3)$ $8$ annihilates it, acts on $B_V$ colour
$iD$, $D=\operatorname{diag}(-3,1,1,1)$ $1$ multiplies it by a scalar the phase; $B-L=D/3$
$\mathfrak m$ $6$ sends it into $B_V$ the leptoquark sextet

The phase deserves its own line. $D$ is traceless, and normalised by $1/3$ it gives the eigenvalues $(-1,\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13)$ on $(e_t,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z)$: the lepton-number eigenvalue $-1$ and the baryon-number eigenvalue $\tfrac13$ three times. A generator whose eigenvalues are all different does not mix two directions — it multiplies each by a different number. That is what makes it abelian on this quartet and what makes $B-L$ a charge rather than a rotation.

The six are the complement, $\mathfrak{su}(4)\ominus\left(\mathfrak{su}(3)\oplus\mathfrak u(1)\right)$, and they are the leptoquarks: $\dim\mathfrak{su}(4)=15$, and $15-8-1=6$, which is also the dimension of the antisymmetric pair of the triple, $\binom{3}{2}\cdot2=6$, and also the count $3+3$ of the $X$ and $\bar X$ of Particle Classification. Three independent counts, one number.

18.4 The computation: no generator turns a lepton into a lepton

[ALGEBRA] The three blocks are exhaustive, so the statement follows without further work. I checked it directly anyway, because a claim of this kind is cheap to check and expensive to get wrong. Take the quartet in the order $(e_t,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z)$, write the fifteen generators as antihermitian traceless matrices, and apply each to $e_t=(1,0,0,0)$:

the eight of $\mathfrak{su}(3)$ $8$ $0$
the diagonal $iD$, and only it $1$ a pure multiple of $e_t$, namely $-3ie_t$
the off-diagonal $e_t$–$\vec e_a$ generators $6$ a vector with zero $e_t$ component
total $15$ —

No generator returns a direction on the line other than a real multiple of $e_t$, and none returns another point of the line in the sense of "another lepton". There is only one line. So the group cannot rotate one lepton into another, because there is no second lepton direction to rotate into.

18.5 Why that kills "flavour" as a rotation

[ALGEBRA] A rotation needs at least two members. $\mathrm{SU}(3)$ rotates the three colours because there are three of them of the same kind; that is what a fundamental representation of dimension three means. The lepton is one line, so its representation of the stabiliser is the trivial one, and the trivial representation has no rotation in it.

This is the mechanism, stated as a single sentence: the lepton does not turn because it is what the others turn about. Colour is a rotation group acting on a triplet; the lepton is a singlet of that group, and a singlet is a rotation-invariant object by definition.

It also explains why the word "flavour" may be the wrong word for leptons. Flavour, in the quark sector, names a position inside a multiplet that a group rotates. The lepton has no such position. What it has is a generation index, which the algebra does not touch at all, and a mass, which it does not explain either.

18.6 What the generation index still does

[ALGEBRA] The gauge sector acts as the identity on the generation factor. So for each $a\in\{1,2,3\}$ there is a separate copy of the quartet, and the gauge bosons couple to all three copies the same way. Nothing in $\mathfrak{su}(4)$ connects copy 1 to copy 2.

[ANSATZ, from §7 and §14–17] The ladder supplies the masses of the copies: the rungs are generations, and their spacing is the arithmetic of the preceding sections. That arithmetic never moves a lepton from one rung to another; it only assigns a height to each rung.

[SPECULATION] So a lepton changes generation only if the Yukawa sector supplies a matrix acting on the generation factor, and the frame does not write it. Both outcomes are available: one bidoublet gives one Yukawa matrix and the identity, a second fermion-mass contribution splits the two matrices and gives something else. The corpus should not claim either.

18.7 The two facts the mechanism does not explain

[ALGEBRA] It does not explain why there are three generations. The stabiliser argument is about one quartet; the number three is a separate input, as The Number of Generations and the Biquaternion Algebra says.

[ALGEBRA] It does not explain why the neutrino mixes. Neutrino oscillation is real and is not a colour rotation: it is a misalignment between two mass matrices on the generation factor, which is the same object the charged-lepton sector leaves unwritten. The mechanism forbids a gauge rotation among leptons; it says nothing about a Yukawa misalignment. Confusing the two is the error this section exists to prevent, and it is the error that produced the corpus's earlier $K$-matrix article.

18.8 What would overturn it

[ALGEBRA] One observation: any charged-lepton transition mediated by a gauge boson. A $\mu\to e\gamma$ or $K_L\to\mu e$ signal from the frame's own leptoquarks would say that the vertex carries a generation index, hence that the one-line-per-generation picture is incomplete, hence that the splitting of 18.1 is not the whole story. The mechanism is falsifiable in that single way, and no other.

Conversely, a null result confirms nothing: with no lepton-to-lepton direction in $\mathfrak{su}(4)$, the rate is zero at every mass, and zero is what a null measurement returns for free. That asymmetry — one signal kills it, no number of nulls establishes it — is the honest status of the claim.
