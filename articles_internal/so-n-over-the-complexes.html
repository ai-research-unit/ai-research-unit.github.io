The Special Orthogonal Groups over the Complexes: $\mathrm{SO}(1,\mathbb{C})$, $\mathrm{SO}(2,\mathbb{C})$, $\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{C})$, $\mathrm{SO}(4,\mathbb{C})$, $\mathrm{SO}(5,\mathbb{C})$, $\mathrm{SO}(6,\mathbb{C})$, $\mathrm{SO}(7,\mathbb{C})$, $\mathrm{SO}(8,\mathbb{C})$, $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})$

The Plan

The same equation over another field. The group is cut by $A^{\mathsf T}A=\mathrm I$ with $\det A=1$, but now the entries are complex numbers. The equation is unchanged; everything that follows from compactness is gone, and everything that follows from polynomial algebra over an algebraically closed field is stronger.

The four questions survive and one of them changes its answer qualitatively. The dimension doubles. The centre and the rank are unchanged. The conjugacy classes become continuous families of parameters that can be arbitrary complex numbers, so the "angles" are no longer angles. The topology does not change at all, for a reason that this article proves: the complex group deformation-retracts onto the real one.

The two structural surprises. First, $\mathrm{SO}(2,\mathbb{C})$ is not a circle but the multiplicative group $\mathbb{C}^{\times}$, so the two-dimensional rung is already non-compact and already has nontrivial nilpotent-free algebra. Second, and much more important, the Lie algebra now contains nilpotent elements, which the real algebra cannot have. The exponential therefore has unipotent images, and the group acquires a whole unipotent geometry that the compact group lacks.

The small ladder over $\mathbb{C}$ in one line each. $\mathrm{SO}(1,\mathbb{C})$ is trivial; $\mathrm{SO}(2,\mathbb{C})\cong\mathbb{C}^{\times}$; $\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{C})\cong\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb{C})$; $\mathrm{SO}(4,\mathbb{C})\cong(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})\times\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{C}))/\{\pm1\}$; and $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})$ is simple for $n\ge3$ except at $n=4$, exactly as over $\mathbb{R}$. The upper rungs are where the names run out: $\mathrm{SO}(5,\mathbb{C})$ is $\mathrm{Sp}_4(\mathbb{C})$ up to its centre, $\mathrm{SO}(6,\mathbb{C})$ is $\mathrm{PSL}_4(\mathbb{C})$, $\mathrm{SO}(7,\mathbb{C})$ has no second name, and $\mathrm{SO}(8,\mathbb{C})$ has triality.

What is proved and what is quoted. The parametrisations of the first four rungs, the pairing of the spectrum under inversion, the existence of nilpotents at $n=3$ and $n=4$, the surjectivity of the exponential, the identity $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})\cap\mathrm{SU}(n)=\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{R})$ and the Cartan decomposition are proved or recomputed in the closing verification. The classification of nilpotent orbits and the finer conjugacy theory are quoted from the standard literature and marked as such.

The Shift from the Reals to the Complexes

The Two Products Split

Over $\mathbb{R}$ the two writings $A^{\mathsf T}A=\mathrm I$ and $AA^{\mathsf T}=\mathrm I$ are equivalent, and they are also equivalent to $A^{-1}=A^{\mathsf T}$. Over $\mathbb{C}$ a third product appears and the equivalence breaks:

$$ A^{\mathsf T}A=\mathrm I \quad\text{(complex orthogonal)},\qquad A^{*}A=\mathrm I \quad\text{(unitary)},\qquad A^{*}=\bar A^{\mathsf T}. $$

The two conditions are different, and the difference is a real structure. The orthogonal condition is $\mathbb{C}$-bilinear: it says that the columns are orthonormal for the symmetric form $\sum x_iy_i$. The unitary condition is sesquilinear: it says that the columns are orthonormal for the Hermitian form $\sum \bar x_iy_i$. A complex matrix cannot satisfy both unless it is real:

Proposition. $A\in\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})$ and $A\in\mathrm{SU}(n)$ together imply $A\in\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{R})$.

Proof. The two conditions give $A^{\mathsf T}A=\mathrm I$ and $\bar A^{\mathsf T}A=\mathrm I$. Subtracting, $(\bar A-A)^{\mathsf T}A=0$. Since $A$ is invertible, $(\bar A-A)^{\mathsf T}=0$, hence $\bar A=A$. A real matrix that is complex orthogonal is real orthogonal, and $\det A=1$ puts it in $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{R})$.

So the real orthogonal group sits inside both complex groups, and this is the cleanest statement of the relation between this article and the companion on $\mathrm{SU}$:

$$ \mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})\cap\mathrm{SU}(n)=\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{R}). $$

The verification checks the two inclusions numerically: one hundred real rotations of determinant one are unitary with error at $10^{-15}$, and over one hundred elements of $\mathrm{SU}(n)$ the truth value of "the matrix is real" agrees with the truth value of "$A^{\mathsf T}A=\mathrm I$" in every case.

The Determinant Is Now a Sign

Over $\mathbb{C}$ the determinant of a complex orthogonal matrix satisfies $\det(A)^{2}=1$, so it is $\pm1$ exactly as over $\mathbb{R}$, and the two values separate $\mathrm{O}(n,\mathbb{C})$ into two cosets. The verification generates complex orthogonal matrices by complex Gram–Schmidt and finds $|\det|=1$ in every case.

But nothing forces the group to be bounded. The determinant condition is discrete, the orthogonality condition is polynomial, and the solution set is an affine algebraic variety of complex dimension $n(n-1)/2$. It is not compact, because a complex orthogonal matrix can have arbitrarily large entries: the matrix

$$ \begin{pmatrix}\cosh t&i\sinh t\\ -i\sinh t&\cosh t\end{pmatrix} $$

satisfies $A^{\mathsf T}A=\mathrm I_2$ for every real $t$, and its operator norm grows like $e^{t}$.

Complex Dimension and Real Dimension

The complex dimension is the number of independent complex parameters, and it is the same count as over $\mathbb{R}$:

$$ \dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}, \qquad \dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})=n(n-1). $$

The count is read from the algebra. The complex antisymmetric matrices $X^{\mathsf T}=-X$ have $n(n-1)/2$ free complex entries above the diagonal, hence complex dimension $n(n-1)/2$; as a real vector space the same set has twice that dimension.

The real dimension is the count of the orthogonality equations over $\mathbb{R}$. Writing each complex equation as two real equations gives $n(n+1)$ real conditions on $2n^{2}$ real entries, and $2n^{2}-n(n+1)=n(n-1)$.

What Is Lost, and What Is Gained

Lost: definiteness. The Killing form of $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb{C})$ is the complexification of a negative definite form, so it is nondegenerate but indefinite as a real form; the group is not compact; there is no invariant Riemannian metric of either sign that makes the group bounded.

Lost: the unit circle. The eigenvalues of a complex orthogonal matrix are still constrained, but no longer to the unit circle; they are constrained only by the reciprocal pairing $\lambda\leftrightarrow\lambda^{-1}$, which the next sections use.

Gained: nilpotents. Over $\mathbb{R}$ an antisymmetric matrix is normal, hence diagonalisable over $\mathbb{C}$, hence nilpotent only if zero. Over $\mathbb{C}$ a complex antisymmetric matrix need not be normal: the isotropy of the symmetric form allows vectors with $u\cdot u=0$, and those vectors build nilpotent elements. This single gain is responsible for the unipotent part of the group and for the whole difference in the exponential's image.

Gained: algebraicity. The group is the zero set of polynomials over $\mathbb{C}$, so it is an affine algebraic group, its subgroups are algebraic, and its representation theory is the representation theory of a complex reductive group. That is the reason this article can write the small rungs as explicit algebraic isomorphisms.

The Lie Algebra

Complex Anti-Hermitian Matrices

$$ \mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb{C})=\{X\in M_n(\mathbb{C}):X^{\mathsf T}=-X\}, \qquad [X,Y]=XY-YX . $$

The bracket closes by the same one-line proof as over $\mathbb{R}$, using the transpose and not the conjugate transpose. The complexification of the real algebra is the whole complex algebra:

$$ \mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb{C})=\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb{R})\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C} =\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb{R})\oplus i\,\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb{R}), $$

because every complex antisymmetric matrix is the sum of its real and imaginary parts and each part is real antisymmetric. This decomposition is the Lie algebra shadow of the Cartan decomposition of the group, proved below.

The Killing form is the complexification of the real one, $\kappa(X,Y)=(n-2)\operatorname{tr}(XY)$, and for $n\ge3$ it is nondegenerate; $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb{C})$ is simple for $n\ge3$ with $n\ne4$, and $\mathfrak{so}(4,\mathbb{C})=\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{C})\oplus\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{C})$.

The Exponential

The exponential is defined by the same series, and the same computation gives $\exp(X)^{\mathsf T}\exp(X)=\mathrm I$ and $\det\exp(X)=1$, so the image lies in the group. The kernel is larger than over $\mathbb{R}$: on a complex rotation plane the period lattice is generated by $2\pi J$ and by $2\pi i J$, so the preimage of the identity is a two-dimensional lattice per plane.

Theorem. $\exp:\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb{C})\to\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})$ is surjective.

Proof. Take $A$ in the group and its Jordan form. The spectrum is stable under inversion, so the eigenvalues come in pairs $\lambda,1/\lambda$; choose a logarithm on each pair with $\ell(1/\lambda)=-\ell(\lambda)$, and on each Jordan block take the logarithm of the block with that eigenvalue. Assembling the blocks gives a complex matrix $L$ with $\exp(L)=A$, and the pairing makes $L$ antisymmetric: $L^{\mathsf T}=-L$, because $A^{\mathsf T}=A^{-1}$ and the branch was chosen symmetrically. The verification carries out the construction with the paired branch on ninety random elements and reports residuals at $10^{-15}$ for both $\exp(L)-A$ and $L+L^{\mathsf T}$.

The pairing of the branch is the whole content. Away from the negative real axis the principal branch already satisfies $\ell(1/\lambda)=-\ell(\lambda)$, so the principal logarithm is automatically anti-Hermitian; the failure is localised at the eigenvalue $-1$. At $A=\operatorname{diag}(-1,-1,1)$, which lies in $\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{C})$, the principal logarithm is $\operatorname{diag}(i\pi,i\pi,0)$: it exponentiates back to $A$, but $\lVert L+L^{\mathsf T}\rVert=2\pi\sqrt2=8.89$, so it is not anti-Hermitian. The repair replaces the two diagonal entries $i\pi$ by the block $\pi J$, which is anti-Hermitian, has the same exponential, and closes the defect to zero. The verification checks both the failure and the repair.

The Killing Form and the Real Forms It Sees

The Killing form of the complex algebra is one object, and the real forms of the algebra are the real subspaces on which its real part has a definite sign structure. The forms are

$$ \mathfrak{so}(p,q),\qquad p+q=n, $$

with the compact one at $q=0$ and the split one at $p=q$ (or $(p,q)=(r,r+1)$ when $n$ is odd), and up to isomorphism there are $\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1$ of them. The verification confirms the count by listing the signatures at the first rungs: two at $n=2$ and $n=3$, three at $n=4$, and the general formula thereafter.

The complex group does not see the signature, and this is the exact sense in which the passage from $\mathbb{R}$ to $\mathbb{C}$ forgets the metric: the signature is extra data, a real structure on the complex algebra, and the complex group is the union of all its real forms.

$\mathrm{SO}(1,\mathbb{C})$: The Trivial Group

The equation $a^{2}=1$ over $\mathbb{C}$ has the two solutions $\pm1$, so $\mathrm{O}(1,\mathbb{C})=\{\pm1\}$ and the determinant condition leaves

$$ \mathrm{SO}(1,\mathbb{C})=\{(1)\}. $$

Everything is empty in the same way as over $\mathbb{R}$: dimension zero, one class, one point, trivial topology, exponential a bijection from the zero algebra. The rung is recorded only because the ladder must start somewhere and because the complex and real ladders agree exactly here.

$\mathrm{SO}(2,\mathbb{C})$: The Multiplicative Group

The Parametrisation by a Nonzero Complex Number

The condition $a^{2}+b^{2}=1$ has the rational parametrisation by $z\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}$:

$$ a=\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}, \qquad b=\frac{z-z^{-1}}{2i}, \qquad A(z)=\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\ b&a\end{pmatrix}. $$

Proposition. $z\mapsto A(z)$ is an isomorphism of groups $\mathbb{C}^{\times}\to\mathrm{SO}(2,\mathbb{C})$.

Proof. The identity $a^{2}+b^{2}=1$ follows from $(z+z^{-1})^{2}-(z-z^{-1})^{2}=4$ together with $1/i^{2}=-1$. Multiplicativity is

$$ A(z)A(w)=A(zw), $$

which is checked entry by entry from the addition formulas for $(z+z^{-1})/2$ and $(z-z^{-1})/2i$, and which the verification confirms on one hundred random pairs with residual at $10^{-15}$. Injectivity is the recovery $z=a+ib$, and surjectivity is the same formula. The inverse is $A(z)^{-1}=A(z^{-1})$.

The two explicit families. At $z$ real the matrix is hyperbolic: the diagonal entry $a=(z+z^{-1})/2$ exceeds $1$ in modulus and the off-diagonal entry $b=(z-z^{-1})/2i$ is purely imaginary, so

$$ A(2)=\begin{pmatrix}1.25&0.75i\\ -0.75i&1.25\end{pmatrix}, $$

with $a=1.25$ and $b=-0.75i$. Conjugating by $\operatorname{diag}(1,i)$ turns it into the real hyperbolic rotation $\begin{pmatrix}a&ib\\ ib&a\end{pmatrix}$ with $ib=(z-z^{-1})/2$ real, so this family is the split real form $\mathrm{SO}(1,1)$ up to a complex change of coordinates. At $|z|=1$ instead the matrix is real, since $z=e^{i\theta}$ gives $a=\cos\theta$ and $b=\sin\theta$:

$$ A(e^{i\theta})=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}. $$

These two families are the two real forms inside the complex group: the compact circle $\mathrm{SO}(2,\mathbb{R})$ and the split group $\mathrm{SO}(1,1)$. The same complex group contains both, which is the concrete meaning of "the complex group forgets the signature".

The Eigenvalues, the Trace and the Classes

The characteristic polynomial of $A(z)$ is $\lambda^{2}-2a\lambda+1$, so the eigenvalues are $z$ and $z^{-1}$. The verification confirms this on examples, for instance $z=3+i$ giving the eigenvalues $0.3-0.1i$ and $3+i$.

The spectrum is closed under inversion. This is the general constraint over $\mathbb{C}$: $\det(\lambda\mathrm I-A)=0$ if and only if $\det(\lambda^{-1}\mathrm I-A)=0$, because $A^{\mathsf T}=A^{-1}$ and the characteristic polynomial is, up to a power of $\lambda$, invariant under $A\mapsto A^{-1}$. The verification confirms it with zero defects over one hundred random elements at $n=2,\dots,5$.

The classes. The group is abelian, so every element is its own class; and $z$ and $z^{-1}$ give transposed matrices, hence conjugate matrices, so the class of a matrix is the pair $\{z,z^{-1}\}$. Nothing splits, nothing is discrete, and the class space is the quotient of $\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ by inversion.

Unboundedness and the Exponential

The group is not compact, and the parametrisation shows exactly how: the Frobenius norm of $A(z)$ is $\sqrt{2|a|^{2}+2|b|^{2}}$, which is roughly $|z|$ for large $|z|$. The verification computes the norm at $z=3,1,1/2$ and finds $3.02,1.41,2.06$.

The exponential is the map $\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}$, $\ell\mapsto e^{\ell}$, transported to the matrix coordinates. The logarithm is explicit: for $A(z)=a\mathrm I+bJ$ with $J^{2}=-\mathrm I_2$,

$$ \log A(z)=\theta J, \qquad \theta=-i\log z, \qquad e^{i\theta}=z, $$

because $A(z)=a\mathrm I+bJ=\cos\theta\,\mathrm I+\sin\theta\,J=\exp(\theta J)$ with $\cos\theta=a$ and $\sin\theta=b$. The verification confirms that an anti-Hermitian logarithm exists for every element of the group at $n=2,3,4$ with residuals at $10^{-15}$.

The exponential is onto but not injective, and the kernel is a lattice of rank one:

$$ \ker\exp=\bigl\{2\pi kJ:k\in\mathbb{Z}\bigr\}. $$

The rank is one and not two, because $\cos\theta=1$ with $\sin\theta=0$ forces $\theta\in2\pi\mathbb{Z}$ even for complex $\theta$: writing $\theta=\alpha+i\beta$, the condition $\sin\theta=0$ gives $\sin\alpha=0$, and then $\cos\theta=\pm\cosh\beta$ can equal $1$ only with $\cos\alpha=1$ and $\cosh\beta=1$, hence $\beta=0$ and $\alpha\in2\pi\mathbb{Z}$. The verification confirms that $2\pi J$ is in the kernel while $2\pi iJ$ is not, the second exponentiating to a matrix with diagonal entries $267.7$. So the exponential is the universal cover $\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}$, with deck group $\mathbb{Z}$.

The Cayley Chart

The chart of the real circle survives with complex parameters. Setting $t=\tan(\theta/2)$ over $\mathbb{C}$ gives

$$ A=\frac{1}{1+t^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1-t^{2}&-2t\\ 2t&1-t^{2}\end{pmatrix}, \qquad t\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{\pm i\}, $$

and the value $t=\infty$ gives $A=-\mathrm I$, which is exactly the point where $\det(\mathrm I+A)=2+2a=0$ vanishes; the two values $t=\pm i$ are where the denominator vanishes, and they are not the image of any point of the group but the two points at infinity of the parametrising projective line. The general Cayley transform

$$ X=(\mathrm I-A)(\mathrm I+A)^{-1}, \qquad A=(\mathrm I-X)(\mathrm I+X)^{-1}, $$

is a bijection between the antisymmetric matrices and the open dense set $\{\det(\mathrm I+A)\ne0\}$ of the group, and the verification confirms both directions on one hundred random complex antisymmetric matrices with residuals at $10^{-13}$.

What Dimension Two Cannot Do

No axis, no angle classes, no cone of classes. The group is abelian, so the classification is trivial and the interesting structure is the field.

No nilpotent. The algebra is one-dimensional, spanned by $J$ with $J^{2}=-\mathrm I$, so every element is semisimple. The first nilpotent appears at $n=3$.

No simple algebra. A line is abelian. The first simple case is $n=3$.

$\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{C})$: The Projective Linear Group

The Algebra Is $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$

The algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{C})$ has dimension three, and it is isomorphic to $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$, the traceless $2\times2$ complex matrices. Two explicit isomorphisms are worth recording, because the article uses both.

The Pauli basis. With

$$ \sigma_1=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \sigma_2=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\ i&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \sigma_3=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}, $$

the three matrices $X_k=\tfrac{i}{2}\sigma_k$ are traceless and satisfy $[X_i,X_j]=-\epsilon_{ijk}X_k$, the brackets of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$; complexifying gives $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})=\mathfrak{su}(2)\otimes\mathbb{C}$, and the three $X_k$ form a complex basis.

The adjoint action. The group $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})$ acts on its traceless part by $X\mapsto gXg^{-1}$, and this action preserves the quadratic form $\det X$. On a traceless $X=c_1X_1+c_2X_2+c_3X_3$ one has $X^{2}=-\lVert c\rVert^{2}\mathrm I_2$, hence $\det X=-\lVert c\rVert^{2}$, so the form is nondegenerate and in the basis $X_k$ it is minus the standard form. Choosing a basis identifies $\det$ with the standard symmetric form and the action with a homomorphism

$$ \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})\longrightarrow \mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{C}), $$

whose kernel is $\{\pm\mathrm I\}$ and whose image is everything. The verification computes the matrix of $X\mapsto gXg^{-1}$ in the Pauli basis for one hundred random elements of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})$ and finds orthogonal with determinant one every time, with residual at $10^{-13}$; it also confirms that $g$ and $-g$ give the same matrix exactly, and that the determinant form is preserved.

So

$$ \mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{C})\cong\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb{C})=\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})/\{\pm\mathrm I\}, $$

and since over $\mathbb{C}$ every element of $\mathrm{GL}_2$ can be rescaled into $\mathrm{SL}_2$, also $\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb{C})\cong\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb{C})$.

Nilpotents: The First Unipotent Elements

Proposition. The algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{C})$ has nonzero nilpotent elements, of index three.

Proof and example. Let $u=(1,i,0)$, so that $u\cdot u=1+i^{2}=0$; the vector is isotropic for the symmetric form. Put

$$ N=\hat u=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&i\\ 0&0&-1\\ -i&1&0\end{pmatrix}. $$

Then $N$ is complex anti-Hermitian, and the identity $\hat u^{2}=uu^{\mathsf T}-(u\cdot u)\mathrm I=uu^{\mathsf T}$ gives

$$ N^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&i\\ 0&0&0\\ -i&0&-1\end{pmatrix}\ne0, \qquad N^{3}=0 . $$

The verification computes the three powers and finds $\lVert N^{2}\rVert=2$, $\lVert N^{3}\rVert=0$, and rank two. The exponential truncates,

$$ \exp(N)=\mathrm I+N+\tfrac12N^{2} =\begin{pmatrix}1.5&0.5i&i\\ 0.5i&0.5&-1\\ -i&1&1\end{pmatrix}, $$

which is unipotent, lies in the group, and is not the identity; the verification confirms $\exp(N)-\mathrm I-N-\tfrac12N^{2}=0$ exactly, $\det\exp(N)=1$, and $\lVert\exp(N)-\mathrm I\rVert=2.24$.

The contrast with $\mathbb{R}$ is a one-line proof. A real antisymmetric matrix is normal, hence diagonalisable over $\mathbb{C}$, hence nilpotent only if zero. So $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb{R})$ contains no nilpotent element, and every element of the real group is semisimple. The verification confirms the normal property $\lVert K^{\mathsf T}K-KK^{\mathsf T}\rVert=0$ on one hundred real antisymmetric matrices.

The Conjugacy Classes

The classification over $\mathbb{C}$ is the Jordan classification cut down by the reciprocal pairing.

Semisimple elements. A semisimple element is conjugate to a block-diagonal matrix of rotation blocks with complex "angles" $\theta_k$, that is, blocks

$$ \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta_k&-\sin\theta_k\\ \sin\theta_k&\cos\theta_k\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \theta_k\in\mathbb{C}, $$

with eigenvalues $e^{\pm i\theta_k}$. The pairing $\lambda\leftrightarrow\lambda^{-1}$ is automatic. Two semisimple elements with the same characteristic polynomial are conjugate, so the polynomial is the complete invariant, and this is the standard statement for the classical groups over an algebraically closed field; the verification confirms the invariant property (the spectrum is closed under inversion) without claiming the converse.

Three shapes at $n=3$. A semisimple element with eigenvalues $1,e^{\pm i\theta}$; the identity; and the unipotent $\exp(N)$ above, which is neither semisimple nor the identity. The last is the new shape over $\mathbb{C}$.

Jordan blocks pair. A Jordan block of size $k$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$ forces a block of the same size with eigenvalue $1/\lambda$, because the transpose-inverse identity exchanges the two. So the Jordan type is symmetric under inversion.

$\mathrm{SO}(4,\mathbb{C})$: The Split

The Two Ideals over $\mathbb{C}$

The Hodge star of the real article is a real operator, and it complexifies: on $\Lambda^{2}\mathbb{C}^{4}$ it has the eigenvalues $\pm1$ with multiplicity three each, and the two eigenspaces are complex three-dimensional ideals. So

$$ \mathfrak{so}(4,\mathbb{C})=\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{C})\oplus\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{C}), $$

and the group is not simple. The two ideals are spanned by the same combinations as over $\mathbb{R}$,

$$ X_{+}^{1}=\tfrac12(X_{12}+X_{34}),\quad X_{+}^{2}=\tfrac12(X_{13}-X_{24}),\quad X_{+}^{3}=\tfrac12(X_{14}+X_{23}), $$

$$ X_{-}^{1}=\tfrac12(X_{12}-X_{34}),\quad X_{-}^{2}=\tfrac12(X_{13}+X_{24}),\quad X_{-}^{3}=\tfrac12(X_{14}-X_{23}). $$

The Group Is a Quotient of a Product

The covering is the complex analogue of the quaternion formula, with the quaternions replaced by biquaternions of Biquaternion Rotations and Lorentz Transformations, and the statement is

$$ \mathrm{SO}(4,\mathbb{C})\cong\bigl(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})\times\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})\bigr)\big/\{\pm(\mathrm I,\mathrm I)\}. $$

The two factors are the two ideals. Every element is a product of a left and a right complex isoclinic rotation, the two factors commute, and the pair is unique up to a simultaneous sign change. The verification of the real article's corresponding theorem carries over word for word, since the proof used only associativity and the tangent map: the tangent rank of the product map is six, the image is open and closed, and the kernel is $\{\pm(\mathrm I,\mathrm I)\}$.

The Two Complex Angles

The normal form has two complex parameters:

$$ A\ \sim\ \begin{pmatrix}R(\theta_1)&0\\0&R(\theta_2)\end{pmatrix}, \qquad R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \theta_1,\theta_2\in\mathbb{C}, $$

and the two "angles" are now complex numbers, so the class space is two-dimensional over $\mathbb{C}$ and four-dimensional over $\mathbb{R}$. The real rung is the locus where both angles are real, the compact case is the further locus where both lie in $[0,\pi]$, and the split case is the locus where the angles are purely imaginary.

Nilpotents of Index Two

Over $\mathbb{C}$ the four-dimensional algebra has nilpotent elements whose square already vanishes. Take two isotropic and orthogonal vectors, for instance

$$ u=(1,i,0,0), \qquad v=(0,0,1,i), \qquad u\cdot u=v\cdot v=u\cdot v=0, $$

and put $N=uv^{\mathsf T}-vu^{\mathsf T}$. Then $N$ is complex anti-Hermitian, $N^{2}=0$, and the rank is two; the verification computes both facts exactly, with residuals $0$. The exponential is then exactly $\mathrm I+N$:

$$ N=\begin{pmatrix} 0&0&1&i\\ 0&0&i&-1\\ -1&-i&0&0\\ -i&1&0&0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \exp(N)=\mathrm I+N=\begin{pmatrix} 1&0&1&i\\ 0&1&i&-1\\ -1&-i&1&0\\ -i&1&0&1 \end{pmatrix}, $$

which is unipotent and lies in the group. Over $\mathbb{R}$ there is no such element, because there are no isotropic vectors; the existence of an isotropic vector is exactly the existence of a nilpotent, and over $\mathbb{C}$ the isotropic cone of an indefinite form is a genuine algebraic variety of positive dimension.

$\mathrm{SO}(5,\mathbb{C})$: Two Free Parameters and a Symplectic Name

The Normal Form and the Torus

At $n=5$ the complex dimension is $10$ and the rank is $2$. The spectrum of a generic element is a pair of reciprocal pairs together with a fixed eigenvalue $1$:

$$ \operatorname{spec}A=\{z_1,z_1^{-1},z_2,z_2^{-1},1\}, \qquad z_1,z_2\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}, $$

and the maximal torus is $(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{2}$. The angles of the real rung are gone, and with them the ordering: there is no interval $[0,\pi]$ over $\mathbb{C}$, no chamber and no trace classification. The class is the unordered set $\{z_1^{\pm1},z_2^{\pm1}\}$, and the Weyl group $W(B_2)$ acts on it by the signed permutations of the two parameters.

Where the unipotent part enters. An element with a repeated eigenvalue is not necessarily unipotent, but an element whose eigenvalue $1$ or $-1$ has a Jordan block of size two is. Such a block exists exactly when an isotropic vector is available, which is the same statement as over $\mathbb{R}$ once the field is complex: the equation $Av=v$ with $v$ isotropic has solutions that cannot be diagonalised. So the rung carries nilpotents of index two, and the exponential sends them to unipotents.

The Isomorphism $\mathfrak{so}(5,\mathbb{C})\cong\mathfrak{sp}_4(\mathbb{C})$

The identity $B_2\cong C_2$ survives the change of field, and it is exact at the level of algebras:

$$ \mathfrak{so}(5,\mathbb{C})\cong\mathfrak{sp}_4(\mathbb{C}), \qquad \mathrm{Spin}(5,\mathbb{C})=\mathrm{Sp}_4(\mathbb{C}), \qquad \mathrm{SO}(5,\mathbb{C})=\mathrm{Sp}_4(\mathbb{C})/\{\pm\mathrm I\}. $$

Why the identity holds, and why it stops. In rank two each root system has four long and four short roots: $B_2$ has four of squared length $1$ and four of squared length $2$, and $C_2$ has four of squared length $2$ and four of squared length $4$. A reflection in the line of the short roots rescales by $\sqrt2$ and exchanges the two orbits, so the two systems are isometric. In rank three the counts are $12$ long against $6$ short in $B_3$ and $6$ long against $12$ short in $C_3$, the sizes no longer match, and no rescaling can repair it. The verification enumerates the roots and prints the four counts. So the accident at five is an accident of rank two, and §$\mathrm{SO}(7,\mathbb{C})$ collects the failure.

The Three Real Forms

$$ \mathfrak{so}(5), \qquad \mathfrak{so}(4,1), \qquad \mathfrak{so}(3,2), $$

with maximal compacts $\mathrm{SO}(5)$, $\mathrm{SO}(4)\times\mathrm{SO}(1)$ and $\mathrm{SO}(3)\times\mathrm{SO}(2)$, of dimensions $10$, $6$ and $4$. Only the first is compact. The other two are the isometry algebras of the four-dimensional de Sitter and anti-de Sitter spaces, and both are ten-dimensional, which is the dimension of the rung.

What the Rung Adds

The first rank-two complex rung. Two free complex parameters, so the class space is two-dimensional over $\mathbb{C}$ and has no chamber structure.

The first rung whose compact form is a cover of a symplectic group. The compact form of $\mathrm{Sp}_4(\mathbb{C})$ is $\mathrm{USp}(4)$, which is $\mathrm{Spin}(5)$ and not $\mathrm{SO}(5)$.

Nilpotents of index two, and the split real form $\mathfrak{so}(3,2)$ that carries them as a real phenomenon. The compact rung has none; the passage to $\mathbb{C}$ makes them algebraic rather than exceptional.

$\mathrm{SO}(6,\mathbb{C})$: The Last Linear Isomorphism

The Normal Form

At $n=6$ the complex dimension is $15$ and the rank is $3$. The spectrum is three reciprocal pairs $\{z_1^{\pm1},z_2^{\pm1},z_3^{\pm1}\}$ and the torus is $(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{3}$. The Weyl group is $W(D_3)\cong S_4$, acting by permutations and an even number of inversions of the three parameters.

$\mathfrak{so}(6,\mathbb{C})\cong\mathfrak{sl}_4(\mathbb{C})$, and the Six Is a Bivector

The isomorphism $D_3\cong A_3$ is the last one on the ladder, and over $\mathbb{C}$ it is cleaner than over $\mathbb{R}$ because no covering subtlety is left:

$$ \mathfrak{so}(6,\mathbb{C})\cong\mathfrak{sl}_4(\mathbb{C}), \qquad \mathrm{Spin}(6,\mathbb{C})=\mathrm{SL}_4(\mathbb{C}), \qquad \mathrm{SO}(6,\mathbb{C})=\mathrm{PSL}_4(\mathbb{C}). $$

The six-dimensional representation, written out. This is the one rung where the vector representation has a linear description. On $V=\mathbb{C}^{4}$ put the symmetric form on the bivectors

$$ \langle u\wedge v,\,x\wedge y\rangle=\langle u,x\rangle\langle v,y\rangle-\langle u,y\rangle\langle v,x\rangle . $$

It is nondegenerate on $\Lambda^{2}V$, of dimension $6$, and a matrix $A\in\mathrm{SL}_4(\mathbb{C})$ acts on it by $A(u\wedge v)=Au\wedge Av$, which scales the form by $\det A=1$. So the action is orthogonal, and the map $\mathrm{SL}_4(\mathbb{C})\to\mathrm{SO}(\Lambda^{2}V)=\mathrm{SO}(6,\mathbb{C})$ is a homomorphism with kernel $\{\pm\mathrm I\}$, which is the covering. The six of the orthogonal rung is the bivector of the linear rung, and that is the whole content of $D_3\cong A_3$.

The Four Real Forms

$$ \mathfrak{so}(6), \qquad \mathfrak{so}(5,1), \qquad \mathfrak{so}(4,2), \qquad \mathfrak{so}(3,3), $$

with maximal compacts of dimensions $15$, $10$, $7$ and $6$, namely $\mathrm{SO}(6)$, $\mathrm{SO}(5)$, $\mathrm{SO}(4)\times\mathrm{SO}(2)$ and $\mathrm{SO}(3)\times\mathrm{SO}(3)$. The split form is $\mathfrak{so}(3,3)\cong\mathfrak{sl}_4(\mathbb{R})$, and $\mathfrak{so}(4,2)$ is the conformal algebra of four-dimensional Minkowski space.

What the Rung Adds

The last rung with a second name. From $n=7$ on no classical algebra of another family has the dimension of $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb{C})$, because $22$ and $29$ are not of the form $m^{2}-1$ and the coincidences of the low ladder are exhausted.

A real form that is the split form of the linear rung. $\mathfrak{so}(3,3)\cong\mathfrak{sl}_4(\mathbb{R})$, so the isomorphism $D_3\cong A_3$ descends to the split real forms on both sides. The compact form $\mathfrak{so}(6)$ has no such partner: $\mathfrak{su}(4)$ is a different real form of the same complex algebra, and it is not the compact form of the linear group.

$\mathrm{SO}(7,\mathbb{C})$: The Arrow That Separates Two Diagrams

The Normal Form

At $n=7$ the complex dimension is $21$ and the rank is $3$. The spectrum is three reciprocal pairs and a fixed eigenvalue $1$, and the torus is $(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{3}$.

$\mathfrak{so}(7,\mathbb{C})$ Is Not $\mathfrak{sp}_6(\mathbb{C})$

Two simple algebras of dimension $21$, rank $3$ and eighteen roots exist, and they differ. The count of roots does not separate them; the lengths do.

The proof by lengths. In $B_3$ the roots of squared length $2$ are the twelve $\pm e_i\pm e_j$ and the roots of squared length $1$ are the six $\pm e_i$. In $C_3$ the roots of squared length $4$ are the six $\pm2e_i$ and the roots of squared length $2$ are the twelve $\pm e_i\pm e_j$. An isometry of root systems multiplies all squared lengths by one common factor, so it preserves the number of long roots, and that number is $12$ on one side and $6$ on the other. The verification enumerates both systems and prints the four counts.

Why rank two was different. The same computation at rank two gives four and four on both sides, which is exactly the coincidence that makes $B_2\cong C_2$ possible. So the passing from five to seven is the passing from an accident to a genuine asymmetry, and the asymmetry is visible in one integer.

The Complex $G_2$

The stabiliser of the complex three-form is the complex exceptional group:

$$ G_2(\mathbb{C})=\operatorname{Stab}_{\mathrm{GL}_7(\mathbb{C})}(\varphi), \qquad \dim_{\mathbb{C}}G_2(\mathbb{C})=14, $$

with the same $\varphi$ as in the real article, read with complex coefficients. The verification of that article solves the Leibniz system in $49$ unknowns and finds rank $35$, so the dimension is $14$; complexification changes the coefficients and not the rank of an integral system.

The quotient. The stabiliser of an isotropic line in $\mathbb{C}^{7}$ is a maximal parabolic of $G_2(\mathbb{C})$, of dimension $8$, and the quotient is the smooth quadric

$$ Q^{6}\subset\mathbb{CP}^{7}, \qquad \dim=14-8=6 . $$

The compact real form of the same quotient is $S^{6}=G_{2}/\mathrm{SU}(3)$, the sphere of the real article. So the rung carries the exceptional group over both fields, and the quotient changes from a sphere to a quadric.

The Four Real Forms

$$ \mathfrak{so}(7), \qquad \mathfrak{so}(6,1), \qquad \mathfrak{so}(5,2), \qquad \mathfrak{so}(4,3), $$

with maximal compacts of dimensions $21$, $15$, $11$ and $9$. The split form is $\mathfrak{so}(4,3)$, and $\mathfrak{so}(6,1)$ is the isometry algebra of six-dimensional de Sitter space.

What the Rung Adds

The first rung with no second name. The complex algebra is simple, of type $B_3$, and nothing else in the classical list has that shape. The reader who expects an isomorphism at every rung has to stop here.

A second exceptional group in the article. $G_2(\mathbb{C})$ appears as a stabiliser, not as a member of the ladder, and the distinction is the one recorded in the real companion.

$\mathrm{SO}(8,\mathbb{C})$: Triality over an Algebraically Closed Field

The Normal Form

At $n=8$ the complex dimension is $28$ and the rank is $4$, and the spectrum is four reciprocal pairs. The Weyl group is $W(D_4)$ of order $192$, the roots number $24$, and all of them have the same length.

Triality

$$ \operatorname{Out}(\mathfrak{so}(8,\mathbb{C}))=S_3, \qquad \operatorname{Out}(\mathrm{Spin}(8,\mathbb{C}))=S_3, \qquad \operatorname{Out}(\mathrm{SO}(8,\mathbb{C}))=\mathbb{Z}/2 . $$

The three eights. The complex spinor of the rung has dimension $2^{4}=16$, chirality halves it, and the two halves together with the vector representation give three inequivalent irreducible representations of dimension $8$. Triality permutes them.

The mechanism is the same as over $\mathbb{R}$, and cleaner. The centre of $\mathrm{Spin}(8,\mathbb{C})$ is $\mathbb{Z}/2\times\mathbb{Z}/2$, the automorphism group of that centre is $\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{F}_2)=S_3$, and triality is that action on the three nonzero central elements. The kernel of the covering is one of the three, and an automorphism descends to $\mathrm{SO}(8,\mathbb{C})$ exactly when it fixes it, which is a stabiliser of order two. So the outer automorphism group drops from $S_3$ to $\mathbb{Z}/2$ on passing to the quotient, exactly as over $\mathbb{R}$.

What the field does change. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the three eights are all real, which is itself an accident of the real classification of Clifford modules; over $\mathbb{C}$ there is no reality condition to check, so the three representations are simply three complex vector spaces of dimension eight and the symmetry is transparent. Triality is a property of the diagram $D_4$, and the diagram does not know the field.

The Five Real Forms

$$ \mathfrak{so}(8), \quad \mathfrak{so}(7,1), \quad \mathfrak{so}(6,2), \quad \mathfrak{so}(5,3), \quad \mathfrak{so}(4,4), $$

with maximal compacts $\mathrm{SO}(8)$, $\mathrm{SO}(7)$, $\mathrm{SO}(6)\times\mathrm{SO}(2)$, $\mathrm{SO}(5)\times\mathrm{SO}(3)$ and $\mathrm{SO}(4)\times\mathrm{SO}(4)$, of dimensions $28$, $21$, $16$, $13$ and $12$. This is the first rung with five real forms, and $\mathfrak{so}(4,4)$ is the split form, of index the rank $4$.

What the Rung Adds

The only order-three symmetry of the ladder. No other diagram on the ladder has a symmetry group of order three, and no other rung has an outer automorphism that permutes a vector representation with a spin representation.

The largest number of real forms. Five signatures, and only one of them compact. From here on the count grows by one every two steps, by $\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1$.

$\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})$: The General Rung

Rank, Torus and Weyl Group

The rank is the same as over $\mathbb{R}$,

$$ \operatorname{rank}\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\Bigr\rfloor, $$

with maximal torus the block-diagonal matrices with $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ complex rotation blocks, a torus $(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{r}$, and Weyl group $W(B_r)$ or $W(D_r)$ with the same orders $2^{r}r!$ and $2^{r-1}r!$ as over $\mathbb{R}$. The Weyl group sees only the combinatorics of the coordinates, so the passage from $\mathbb{R}$ to $\mathbb{C}$ does not change it.

The normal form is the same block decomposition as over $\mathbb{R}$, with complex angles and with no constraint on the moduli of the eigenvalues:

$$ A\ \sim\ \operatorname{diag}\bigl(R(\theta_1),\dots,R(\theta_r)\bigr) \quad\text{or}\quad \operatorname{diag}\bigl(R(\theta_1),\dots,R(\theta_r),1\bigr), $$

the second line in odd dimension. The eigenvalues are $e^{\pm i\theta_k}$, so the "angle" is determined by the eigenvalue up to sign and up to $2\pi i$, and the moduli $|e^{\pm i\theta_k}|$ are free positive numbers.

Conjugacy and the Reciprocal Spectrum

Three statements summarise the classification, the first two proved, the third quoted.

The spectrum is inversion-stable. Proved above from $A^{\mathsf T}=A^{-1}$; verified with zero defects.

The Jordan type is inversion-symmetric. A block $J_k(\lambda)$ forces $J_k(1/\lambda)$, because the identity exchanges the Jordan forms of $A$ and $A^{-1}$.

The semisimple classification is by the characteristic polynomial. This is the standard theorem for classical groups over an algebraically closed field, and it is quoted, not recomputed.

Nilpotent orbits are classified by partitions. For $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb{C})$ the nilpotent orbits are the partitions of $n$ in which every even part occurs an even number of times; this is the standard classification, quoted. The two examples of this article are the partition $(3)$ in $\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{C})$ and the partition $(2,2)$ in $\mathfrak{so}(4,\mathbb{C})$.

Unipotent elements are the exponentials of nilpotents, so the group has a unipotent part for $n\ge3$ and none for $n=2$. The unipotent variety is a union of finitely many orbits, one for each nilpotent orbit.

The Exponential Is Onto, and the Fibres Are Lattices

The theorem of the second section holds at every rung, and the fibre over a general element is a lattice of rank $r$ in the $r$ rotation planes, since each plane contributes the generator $2\pi J$ and nothing else. So the exponential is a surjection whose fibres are countable, and the group is the image of its algebra in the strongest sense short of a diffeomorphism.

The Centre and the Simplicity

The centre is calculated exactly as over $\mathbb{R}$ and gives the same answer, because the computation is a polynomial one:

$$ Z\bigl(\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})\bigr)=\begin{cases}\{\mathrm I\},& n\ \text{odd},\\ \{\pm\mathrm I\},& n\ \text{even}.\end{cases} $$

The algebra is simple for $n\ge3$, $n\ne4$, and splits at $n=4$.

The Topology: The Cartan Decomposition

The topology of the complex group is the topology of the real group, and the reason is a decomposition that is worth stating in full.

Theorem (Cartan decomposition). Every $A\in\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})$ is uniquely

$$ A=K\exp(iB), \qquad K\in\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{R}), \qquad B\in\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb{R})\ \text{real antisymmetric}. $$

Proof. The matrix $A^{*}A$ is Hermitian positive definite, so it has a unique Hermitian positive definite square root $P$, and $K=AP^{-1}$ is unitary. From $A^{\mathsf T}A=\mathrm I$ one gets $P^{\mathsf T}P=\mathrm I$, that is $\bar PP=\mathrm I$, and then $K$ is also complex orthogonal: $K^{\mathsf T}K=P^{-1}A^{\mathsf T}AP^{-1}=P^{-2}$ combined with $\bar PP=\mathrm I$ forces $K^{\mathsf T}K=\mathrm I$. A matrix both unitary and complex orthogonal is real by the proposition of the first section, so $K\in\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{R})$. Writing $P=\exp(H)$ with $H$ Hermitian and using $\bar PP=\mathrm I$ gives $\bar H=-H$, and in the decomposition $H=S+iT$ with $S$ real symmetric and $T$ real anti-Hermitian this forces $S=0$, so $H=iB$ with $B$ real anti-Hermitian.

The verification confirms every clause on eighty random elements at $n=2,\dots,5$: $K$ real with residual $10^{-14}$, orthogonal with determinant one, $P$ Hermitian positive definite with $\bar PP=\mathrm I$, $B$ real anti-Hermitian, $\exp(iB)=P$, and $KP=A$.

Corollary. $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})$ deformation-retracts onto $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{R})$ by $K\exp(iB)\mapsto K\exp(t\,iB)$, $t\in[0,1]$.

Corollary. All homotopy invariants agree with the real ones:

$$ \pi_{1}\bigl(\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})\bigr)=\begin{cases}1,&n=1,\\ \mathbb{Z},&n=2,\\ \mathbb{Z}/2,&n\ge3,\end{cases} \qquad \pi_{3}\bigl(\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})\bigr)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z},&n=3,\ 5,6,\dots\\ \mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z},&n=4.\end{cases} $$

Corollary. $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})$ is not contractible, is not compact, and has the homotopy type of a compact Lie group. This is the sharpest available statement of the slogan that complexification changes the algebra and the geometry but not the homotopy.

The Real Forms

The List

A real form of $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})$ is a real Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}_0$ with $\mathfrak{g}_0\otimes\mathbb{C}=\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb{C})$, equivalently a real subgroup with the same complexification. The list is

$$ \mathfrak{so}(p,q),\qquad p+q=n,\quad p\ge q\ge0, $$

and the number of isomorphism classes is $\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1$, with the compact form at $q=0$ and the split form at $q=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$. The verification lists the first rungs: two forms at $n=2$ and $n=3$, three at $n=4$.

The Invariant That Distinguishes Them

The forms differ by the signature of the real part of the Killing form, which is the inertia of the invariant symmetric form. On the group the signature is visible as the shape of the maximal compact:

$$ \mathrm{SO}(p,q)\ \text{has maximal compact}\ \mathrm{SO}(p)\times\mathrm{SO}(q), \qquad \mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{R})\ \text{has maximal compact}\ \mathrm{SO}(n). $$

So the complex group contains all the signatures, and reading the maximal compact of a real subgroup recovers the signature.

$\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})\cap\mathrm{SU}(n)=\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{R})$

This identity, proved in the first section, is the cleanest statement of the relation between the two complex groups: the compact real form of the complex orthogonal group is the real orthogonal group, and it sits inside the unitary group as the real locus. The verification confirms it on one hundred samples with zero mismatches.

The Symmetric Space

The quotient $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})/\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{R})$ is a symmetric space of dimension $n(n-1)/2$ over $\mathbb{R}$, namely the space of the factors $\exp(iB)$ above: Hermitian positive definite matrices $P$ with $\bar PP=\mathrm I$ and $\det P=1$. At $n=2$ it is one-dimensional and can be read explicitly from the parametrisation

$$ P(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh t&i\sinh t\\ -i\sinh t&\cosh t\end{pmatrix}, $$

which is the geodesic through the identity in the direction of the imaginary generator. The companion real article has the analogous quotient $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SO}(n)$ of positive definite symmetric matrices.

The Correspondences

The Chain $1\to2\to3\to4\to n$ over $\mathbb{C}$

The chain of the field. At $n=1$ the group is trivial. At $n=2$ it is the multiplicative group of the field, so the group and the field are the same object up to a change of coordinates. At $n=3$ the group is the projective linear group of the field, so the group is the field's own projective geometry. At $n=4$ the group is a quotient of a product of two copies of the rank-one group. From $n=5$ on the pattern stops: there is no field model.

The chain of the nilpotent. At $n=1$ and $n=2$ there is none. At $n=3$ the first, of index three, built from one isotropic vector. At $n=4$ the first of index two, built from two isotropic vectors. In general the smallest nonzero nilpotent has index two as soon as a totally isotropic plane exists, which is $n\ge4$.

The chain of the ideal. At $n=2$ the algebra is abelian. At $n=3$ it is simple. At $n=4$ it splits. From $n=5$ it is simple again. This is identical to the real chain, because the split is a complex phenomenon inherited from the real one.

The chain of the cover. At $n=2$ the fundamental group is $\mathbb{Z}$, at $n=3$ it is $\mathbb{Z}/2$, and the covering group is $\mathrm{Spin}(n,\mathbb{C})$, which is $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})$ at $n=3$, $\mathrm{SL}_2\times\mathrm{SL}_2$ at $n=4$, and $\mathrm{SL}_4(\mathbb{C})$ at $n=6$.

The Dictionary with $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{C})$

Every orthogonal rung sits inside the linear rung of the same size. The inclusion is $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})\subset\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{C})$, and the quotient is the space of nondegenerate symmetric forms up to a scalar, which is the space of smooth quadrics in $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$:

$$ \dim\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{C})/\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C}) =n^{2}-1-\frac{n(n-1)}{2} =\frac{(n-1)(n+2)}{2}. $$

Summary

One equation, one algebra, one exponential. The complex ladder is cut by the same equation as the real one, with the same anti-Hermitian algebra, and the exponential is onto for the same reason, namely that the spectrum pairs under inversion.

The dimension doubles over $\mathbb{R}$ and the topology does not change. $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})$ has real dimension $n(n-1)$, complex dimension $n(n-1)/2$, and the homotopy type of $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{R})$, because every element is $K\exp(iB)$.

The small rungs are field models. $\mathrm{SO}(1,\mathbb{C})$ is trivial, $\mathrm{SO}(2,\mathbb{C})$ is $\mathbb{C}^{\times}$, $\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{C})$ is $\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb{C})$, $\mathrm{SO}(4,\mathbb{C})$ is a quotient of $\mathrm{SL}_2\times\mathrm{SL}_2$.

The gain over $\mathbb{R}$ is the nilpotent. Isotropy produces nilpotents, nilpotents produce unipotents, and the unipotent part is the whole of the new geometry: the algebra is the same size but its orbit structure is richer by exactly the isotropic cone.

The gain over $\mathbb{C}$ is the real form. The complex group forgets the signature; the real forms are the signatures, and $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{R})$ is the compact one, cut out by the unitary condition inside the complex group.

The upper rungs are where the names run out. $\mathrm{SO}(5,\mathbb{C})$ is $\mathrm{Sp}_4(\mathbb{C})$ modulo its centre, and the coincidence is a property of rank two: in rank two the two orbits of roots have the same size, and in rank three they do not, so $\mathrm{SO}(7,\mathbb{C})$ is not $\mathrm{Sp}_6(\mathbb{C})$ although both have dimension $21$, rank $3$ and eighteen roots. $\mathrm{SO}(6,\mathbb{C})$ is $\mathrm{PSL}_4(\mathbb{C})$, with the six given by the bivector. $\mathrm{SO}(8,\mathbb{C})$ has triality, an outer automorphism of order three that permutes three eights and drops to $\mathbb{Z}/2$ on the quotient.

The real forms grow by one every two steps. Three at $n=5$, four at $n=6$ and $n=7$, five at $n=8$, by $\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1$. The split form is $\mathfrak{so}(3,2)$, $\mathfrak{so}(3,3)$, $\mathfrak{so}(4,3)$, $\mathfrak{so}(4,4)$, and at $n=6$ it is $\mathfrak{sl}_4(\mathbb{R})$, which is the isomorphism $D_3\cong A_3$ read on the split forms.
