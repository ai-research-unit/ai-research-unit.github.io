Biquaternion Correspondences: the Sets, their Names, and their Places in the Algebra

What a Row Claims

A row of these tables is a claim, not a translation. The first column names a set in the standard notation of the subject. The third column says where that set sits in biquaternion letters. The row is only as good as the kind of identification it states.

Four kinds are used, and every row names its own.

  • Equal. After the dictionary $e_t\mapsto I_2$, $\vec e_k\mapsto-i\sigma_k$ the two sets are the same set of matrices.
  • Isomorphic. A map is given, and the map is not the identity of sets.
  • Fixed, or level. The set is the fixed set of a named conjugation, or the level set of $N(\tilde Q)$ or of $\tilde Q^{*}\tilde Q$.
  • Quotient, cover, or group of operators. The set is a quotient, a twofold cover, or a group of operators built from elements of $\mathbb{B}$.

Three tests kill a row, and every row below survived all three. The third column contains only $e_t$, $\vec e_k$, their $i$-multiples, and the operations $N$, $\natural$, $*$, $\bar{}$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{split}}$, $\tilde Q\wedge\tilde P$, $\circ$, $\rho_L$, $\rho_R$, $\mathrm{Hp}$. The real dimensions agree. The product, the bracket, or the Jordan product closes with defect zero.

The two errors the tests catch. The first is to put a matrix model in the third column. $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ is not a biquaternion equivalence of $\mathbb{B}$; it is the same algebra under another name, and that row belongs the other way round, with $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ in the first column and $\mathbb{B}$ in the third. In the same way $M_4(\mathbb{C})$ is not biquaternion. Its biquaternion interpretation is the endomorphism algebra of $\mathbb{B}$, and $\mathbb{B}$ itself sits inside it as the left regular copy.

The second error is to treat a word as a biquaternion. "Traceless" is not a property of an abstract matrix algebra in these tables. The scalar part doubled is $2\,\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde Q)=2Q_t$, so the condition behind the word is $Q_t=0$, which is $\mathbb{B}_V$. The scalar line is the centre $\mathbb{B}_C$, and the sentence "traceless, hence centre-free, hence the vector part" states one condition in three vocabularies. The word occurs in a row about $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})$ at the element level and in a row about $\mathfrak{sl}(4,\mathbb{C})$ at the operator level, and it means the biquaternion condition in the first case and the tracelessness of a $4\times4$ matrix in the second. No row may pass between the two without saying which one it uses.

Conventions

The element is $\tilde Q=Q_te_t+Q_x\vec e_x+Q_y\vec e_y+Q_z\vec e_z$ with $Q_\mu\in\mathbb{C}$, the scalar part is $Q_t$, the vector part is $\vec Q$. The norm is $N(\tilde Q)=\sum_\mu Q_\mu^2$. The conjugations are the quaternion one $\natural$, with $\tilde Q^{\natural}=Q_te_t-\vec Q$, the coefficient one $\bar{\tilde Q}=\bar Q_te_t+\bar Q_x\vec e_x+\bar Q_y\vec e_y+\bar Q_z\vec e_z$, and their composite $\tilde Q^{*}=\natural\bar{\tilde Q}=\bar Q_te_t-\bar Q_x\vec e_x-\bar Q_y\vec e_y-\bar Q_z\vec e_z$.

The six subspaces.

$$ \mathbb{B}_C=\mathbb{C}e_t,\qquad \mathbb{B}_V=\{Q_t=0\},\qquad \mathbb{B}_H=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{e_t,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z\}, $$

$$ \mathbb{B}_{iH}=i\mathbb{B}_H,\qquad \mathbb{B}_{\Psi}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{e_t,i\vec e_x,i\vec e_y,i\vec e_z\},\qquad \mathbb{B}_{\Phi}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{ie_t,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z\}. $$

The last two are the fixed and the anti-fixed sets of $*$: $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ is the Hermitian subspace and $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ is the anti-Hermitian one.

The two real forms. The split form is

$$ \mathbb{B}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{split}}}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{e_t,\vec e_y,i\vec e_x,i\vec e_z\}, $$

the fixed set of the conjugate-linear involution $\sigma_{\mathrm{split}}(\tilde Q)=\vec e_y\bar{\tilde Q}\vec e_y^{-1}$, and it is isomorphic to $M_2(\mathbb{R})$: the four generators have the real matrix images $I_2$, $-i\sigma_2$, $\sigma_1$, $\sigma_3$. The quaternion form is $\mathbb{B}_H$, the fixed set of coefficient conjugation, isomorphic to $\mathbb{H}$. Both are eight-dimensional real subalgebras of the eight-dimensional real algebra $\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{R}}$. One is split, the other is a division algebra, and no other real form exists. The split form is written $\mathbb{B}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{split}}}$ after its involution, and the short form $\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{spl}}$ is used where the line is narrow.

One factor, fixed once. The bracket is the half-commutator,

$$ \tilde Q_1\wedge\tilde Q_2=\tfrac12\bigl(\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2-\tilde Q_2\tilde Q_1\bigr)=\vec Q_1\times\vec Q_2 , $$

so that $\vec e_x\wedge\vec e_y=\vec e_z$ and the structure constants are the Levi-Civita symbol. The classification articles of the corpus use the full commutator, which is twice this one. The Jordan product is $\tilde Q_1\circ\tilde Q_2=\tfrac12(\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2+\tilde Q_2\tilde Q_1)$.

The operator layer. The complex operator algebra is $\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}=\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{B})=M_4(\mathbb{C})$, of real dimension $32$, and the real one is $\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{R}}=\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{B})=M_8(\mathbb{R})$, of real dimension $64$. The left and right regular operators are $\rho_L(\tilde Q)(\tilde X)=\tilde Q\tilde X$ and $\rho_R(\tilde Q)(\tilde X)=\tilde X\tilde Q$. They commute, and $\rho_L(\mathbb{B})\cap\rho_R(\mathbb{B})=\mathbb{C}\,\mathrm{id}$. The Hermitian pair is $\mathrm{Hp}_{\tilde Q}=\rho_L(\tilde Q)\rho_R(\tilde Q^{*})$, that is $\mathrm{Hp}_{\tilde Q}(\tilde X)=\tilde Q\tilde X\tilde Q^{*}$.

A letter that cannot be skipped. Every row is written in the base $e_t,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z$. No row uses $\sigma_k$ in its third column. The Pauli letters belong to the matrix image $\mathsf{M}_2$, which is the dictionary, not the answer.

Why the first row is written as it is. Three facts fix it, and each is a computation. First, $\mathrm{Hp}_{\tilde U}$ is an isometry of $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ whenever $N(\tilde U)=1$, because $N(\mathrm{Hp}_{\tilde U}\tilde X)=\lvert N(\tilde U)\rvert^{2}N(\tilde X)$. Second, it is a rotation of $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ and not a reflection, its determinant being $+1$ on every sample. Third, the kernel on the level set is $\{\pm e_t\}$, so the group of operators is exactly the image of the twofold cover, that is $SO^{+}(1,3)$ and not its universal cover. The row is therefore a quotient row: the set that generates the group is $G$, of dimension $6$, and the group of operators is $G/\{\pm e_t\}$, of dimension $6$ as well but with $\pi_1=\mathbb{Z}_2$. Table 3b writes that quotient out and separates it from the three names that are $G$ itself.

Why $O(1,3)$ and $SO(1,3)$ are not rows of the same kind. The full orthogonal group of the form has four components, represented by the identity, the space reversal $P$, the time reversal $T$ and their product $PT$; the special group $SO(1,3)$ keeps the two components of determinant $+1$, namely the identity and $PT$.

Two facts stop the biquaternion formula from reaching them. Every $\mathrm{Hp}_{\tilde U}$ has determinant $+1$, so no reflection is available from a two-sided product. And the element $PT$ is represented by the scalar $-1$, which acts trivially, $\mathrm{Hp}_{-e_t}=\mathrm{Hp}_{e_t}$, because the scalars are central. Hence $SO(1,3)$ is not the group of any condition on $\tilde U$: the second of its two components collapses to the identity. What the formula does give is $SO^{+}(1,3)$, one component. Adjoining the single element $\natural$ supplies one reflection with determinant $-1$, since $\natural$ fixes $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ as a set and acts there as $\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$; the result is the two-component group $SO^{+}(1,3)\rtimes\langle\natural\rangle$, of index two in $O(1,3)$, and the two remaining components stay out of reach.

Three distinctions that the table keeps. The unit-norm group is a level set and therefore a subset of $\mathbb{B}$; the Lorentz group is a quotient of it and therefore not a subset; the conformal group is a group of operators and therefore lives one level up. The map $\mathrm{Hp}$ is not injective on $\mathbb{B}^{\times}$: since $(i\tilde U)\tilde X(i\tilde U)^{*}=\tilde U\tilde X\tilde U^{*}$, the kernel is the full circle $U(1)$, and it is only on the level set $N=1$ that the kernel shrinks to $\{\pm e_t\}$.

A group with the algebra $\mathfrak{u}(2)$ that is not $U(2)$. $\mathbb{H}^{\times}$ is a connected four-dimensional group with Lie algebra $\mathbb{B}_H$, and $\mathbb{B}_H$ is $\mathfrak{u}(2)$. The group is not $U(2)$: its centre is $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$, it is not compact, and $U(2)$ is a quotient of the simply connected cover $SU(2)\times\mathbb{R}$ instead.

The covering, written once. The map onto the Lorentz group is the congruence

$$ \mathrm{Hp}\colon G\longrightarrow SO^{+}(1,3),\qquad \tilde U\mapsto\mathrm{Hp}_{\tilde U},\qquad \mathrm{Hp}_{\tilde U}(\tilde X)=\tilde U\tilde X\tilde U^{*}, $$

and it is two-to-one:

$$ \mathrm{Hp}_{-\tilde U}=\mathrm{Hp}_{\tilde U}\ \ \text{identically},\qquad \ker\mathrm{Hp}=\{\pm e_t\}\cong\mathbb{Z}_2,\qquad SO^{+}(1,3)\cong SL(2,\mathbb{C})/\mathbb{Z}_2 . $$

The two are therefore not isomorphic, and no bijection can make them so: $G$ is simply connected, so $\pi_1(G)=0$, while $\pi_1(SO^{+}(1,3))=\mathbb{Z}_2$, and a homeomorphism carries one fundamental group to the other. The relation is a covering and nothing weaker.

The rotor and the transformation it performs. A rotor is a unit-norm biquaternion, so the group of Lorentz rotors is $SL(2,\mathbb{C})=\mathbb{B}^{\times}_1=G$. Every rotor is a boost rotor times a rotation rotor,

$$ \tilde U=\tilde U_b\tilde u_r,\qquad \tilde U_b=\cosh s\,e_t+i\sinh s\,\hat u,\qquad \tilde u_r=\cos t+\sin t\,\hat v, $$

with $N(\tilde U_b)=N(\tilde u_r)=1$, $\tilde U_b$ Hermitian and of positive scalar part, $\tilde u_r\in Sp(1)$. The boost rotor performs the boost of rapidity $2s$ along $\hat u$ and the rotation rotor the rotation of angle $2t$ about $\hat v$, both by the congruence $\tilde X\mapsto\tilde U\tilde X\tilde U^{*}$. Two rotors perform each transformation, $\pm\tilde U$, so a Lorentz transformation and the rotor that performs it correspond two-to-one: $SU(2)$ is the double cover, and the Lorentz group is its quotient. Written as an equality of groups of transformations of $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$,

$$ \text{rotors}/\{\pm e_t\}\;=\;SO^{+}(1,3), $$

and not rotors $=$ $SO^{+}(1,3)$. The decomposition is the polar decomposition of $G$, and it is global and unique on the positive sheet; it is not a direct product of groups, since the product of two boost rotors is not a boost rotor, its rotation factor being the Wigner rotation.

What the difference is, in one sign. $\mathrm{Hp}_{\tilde U}$ does not see the sign of $\tilde U$; $\mathsf{M}_2$ does. The pair $\pm\tilde U$ is one point of the Lorentz group and two points of $G$, and a spinor is a vector of the second kind. That is why the Dirac equation is written with the cover and not with the group: the covariance holds on $G$, the invariance holds on the quotient, and the difference is the sign that acts on a spinor as $-1$. The Lorentz group acts on $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$, of real dimension $4$; the cover acts on $\mathbb{C}^{2}$, of complex dimension $2$. Forgetting the cover loses the sign, and the sign is the whole content of spin.

$Sp(2,\mathbb{C})$ is the same group, and the reason is dimension two. The symplectic form of rank two is the element $\vec e_y$ itself: its matrix image is $\mathsf{M}_2(\vec e_y)=-i\sigma_2=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\ 1&0\end{pmatrix}$, which squares to $-I_2$. The transpose is the $\mathbb{C}$-linear anti-automorphism

$$ \sigma_{\mathrm{tr}}(\tilde Q)=\vec e_y\,\tilde Q^{\natural}\,\vec e_y^{-1}, $$

the twist of quaternion conjugation by $\vec e_y$; it acts by $Q_y\mapsto-Q_y$ with the other three coefficients fixed, which is exactly the transpose of $\mathsf{M}_2$, since $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_3$ are symmetric and $\sigma_2$ is not. It is none of $\natural$, coefficient conjugation and $*$. The symplectic condition $\mathsf{M}_2(\tilde Q)^{T}J\mathsf{M}_2(\tilde Q)=J$ then reads

$$ \sigma_{\mathrm{tr}}(\tilde Q)\,\vec e_y\,\tilde Q=\vec e_y , $$

and the left side collapses in one step: $\vec e_y\tilde Q^{\natural}\vec e_y^{-1}\vec e_y\tilde Q=\vec e_y\tilde Q^{\natural}\tilde Q=N(\tilde Q)\vec e_y$. The condition is $N(\tilde Q)=1$, with no further equation, so $Sp(2,\mathbb{C})$ is the level set itself. The matrix statement is the same one in another language:

$$ A^{T}JA=\det(A)J\qquad\text{for }2\times2, $$

because a two-form on $\mathbb{C}^{2}$ has a single component, so the symplectic condition reads $\det A=1$. In dimension two there is only one symplectic form up to scale, so the alternating form pairing the two spinor components is the same object as the determinant. This is an identity, not a coincidence of small groups.

$Spin(1,3)$ is the same group, and no separate computation is needed. $SO^{+}(1,3)$ is connected with $\pi_1=\mathbb{Z}_2$, so it has exactly one connected double cover, and that cover is simply connected, which is the definition of the spin group. The cover is $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$. In biquaternion letters the versor condition $\tilde U\tilde U^{\natural}=e_t$ is the spin-group condition, and $N$ is the norm whose polarisation is the Clifford form; the Clifford reading is therefore a re-reading of the level set and not a new group.

One element, two twists, two different involutions. $\sigma_{\mathrm{tr}}=\operatorname{Ad}_{\vec e_y}\circ\natural$ is $\mathbb{C}$-linear and is the transpose. $\sigma_{\mathrm{split}}=\operatorname{Ad}_{\vec e_y}\circ(\text{coefficient conjugation})$ is conjugate-linear and its fixed set is the split real form. The same element $\vec e_y$ produces both, by twisting the linear conjugation in one case and the conjugate-linear one in the other. $\vec e_y$ has a third role, as the symplectic unit: it is at once the $J$ of $Sp(2,\mathbb{C})$, the twist of the split form, and one of the three generators of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})$.

A naming trap: unit-norm is not unitary. $N(\tilde U)=1$ is a condition on a complex bilinear form; $A^{*}A=I$ is a condition on a Hermitian form. They differ, and they select different groups. Every one of $60$ random unit-norm elements fails $A^{*}A=I$, with a failure of order $10$, so $G$ is not a unitary group. The unitary group of the algebra is

$$ U(\mathbb{B})=\{\tilde Q:\tilde Q^{*}\tilde Q=e_t\}\cong U(2), $$

of real dimension $4$ and compact. It is not a subgroup of $G$: for $A\in U(2)$ one has $\lvert\det A\rvert=1$, that is $\lvert N\rvert=1$, and $N=1$ holds only when $\det A=1$. Hence

$$ U(\mathbb{B})\cap G=SU(2), $$

the rotations, of real dimension $3$. The name "the unitary group of the biquaternion algebra" therefore points at $U(2)$ and not at $G$, and the two must not be interchanged. It is also why $G$ of dimension $6$ cannot be called compact: it contains $SU(2)$, but it also contains the boosts $\exp(\mathbb{R}i\vec e_z)$, and those are unbounded.

What each name carries in use. $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$ carries the two-component spinors and the covariance of the Dirac equation, since a Dirac spinor is a pair of such objects and the Clifford generators are the images of a basis of $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$. $Sp(2,\mathbb{C})$ carries the same objects with the symplectic pairing made explicit, which is how a mass term is built from two spinors rather than from a spinor and its conjugate. $Spin(1,3)$ carries the Clifford reading, and that is the reading which generalises to a curved manifold, where a bundle of versors replaces the single group, and then to supergravity and string theory, where the spin group is part of the local structure. $SO^{+}(1,3)$ carries only the vector action, on $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$.

How the Tables Read Together

Three readings of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})$ in one algebra. The compact form is $\mathbb{B}_H\cap\mathbb{B}_V$, the real pure quaternions, with $\vec e_x\wedge\vec e_y=\vec e_z$ and Killing signature $(0,3)$. The split form is $\mathbb{B}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{split}}}\cap\mathbb{B}_V$, spanned by $i\vec e_x$, $\vec e_y$, $i\vec e_z$, with signature $(2,1)$. The realification is $\mathbb{B}_V$ itself, of real dimension $6$, with signature $(3,3)$, and it is $\mathfrak{so}(3,1)$. The first two have real dimension $3$ and are the two real forms; the third has real dimension $6$ and is the complex algebra read over $\mathbb{R}$. All three are visible at once because $\mathbb{B}$ carries a complex structure and three real structures, and a subspace is pinned down by which of them it respects.

Two copies of $\mathfrak{u}(2)$, for two different reasons. $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ is $\mathfrak{u}(2)$ because its elements are skew for the positive-definite form $\operatorname{Sc}(\tilde X^{*}\tilde Y)$. $\mathbb{B}_H$ is $\mathfrak{u}(2)$ because the quaternions are a division algebra: the commutator of two real pure quaternions is again pure, the centre is the real line, and the resulting algebra is $\mathfrak{su}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{u}(1)$. The two subspaces are different subsets of $\mathbb{B}$ with the same Killing signature, and a statement about "the compact real form" must say which one it means.

One condition carries three names. $2\,\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde Q)=2Q_t$ is the scalar part doubled, read at $e_t$. Hence $Q_t=0$, $\mathbb{B}_V$, and "no central part" are the same condition, and the scalar line is the centre. The word traceless names it only when a matrix is in hand. This is why $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})$ is the Hermitian traceless part of the split form and why $\mathfrak{gl}(2,\mathbb{R})=\mathbb{R}e_t\oplus\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})$ is the split form split along the centre. At the operator level the same word means the tracelessness of a $4\times4$ matrix, and the two uses are in different tables for that reason.

The operator rows that a subspace row cannot replace. $\mathfrak{so}(4)$, $\mathfrak{so}(2,2)$ and $\mathfrak{so}(4,\mathbb{C})$ have no place in Table 2. Each is a two-sided pair, one copy attached to $\mathbb{B}_H\cap\mathbb{B}_V$, one to $\mathbb{B}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{split}}}\cap\mathbb{B}_V$, one to $\mathbb{B}_V$. Their Killing signatures $(0,6)$, $(4,2)$, $(6,6)$ separate them, and the split case is the compact one with the sign changed in exactly the two directions that the split form changes.

One sentence that mixes two levels, and its repair. The sentence "$\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})_L\oplus\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})_R$ on the split form $M_4(\mathbb{R})$ traceless" mixes three things. The algebra $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})$ is the traceless part of the split form $\mathbb{B}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{split}}}$, so the elements are $N$-real and have $Q_t=0$. The operators act on $\mathbb{B}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{split}}}\cong\mathbb{R}^{4}$, not on $M_4(\mathbb{R})$. And the resulting algebra is $\mathfrak{so}(2,2)$, the orthogonal algebra of the form $N$ of signature $(2,2)$ on that four-dimensional space. $M_4(\mathbb{R})$ is the real operator algebra of sixteen dimensions and appears nowhere in the row.

Summary

The tables collect the correspondences in one place and keep the level of each one visible. At the element level the biquaternion algebra is $M_2(\mathbb{C})$, its two real forms are $\mathbb{B}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{split}}}$ and $\mathbb{B}_H$, and the six subspaces carry the six classical readings: the centre is the scalar line, the vector part is the traceless part and the Lorentz algebra, the quaternion subspace is $\mathbb{H}$ and $\mathfrak{u}(2)$ and $\mathbb{R}^{4}$, the antiquaternion subspace is $i\mathbb{H}$, the Hermitian subspace is $H_2(\mathbb{C})$ and $\mathbb{R}^{1,3}$ and the scene of the Hermitian pair, and the anti-Hermitian subspace is $\mathfrak{u}(2)$ and $\mathbb{R}^{3,1}$. The three readings of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})$ appear as $\mathbb{B}_H\cap\mathbb{B}_V$, as $\mathbb{B}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{split}}}\cap\mathbb{B}_V$, and as $\mathbb{B}_V$ read over $\mathbb{R}$.

At the group level there is one group with four names and one quotient. The group is $G=\{\tilde U:N(\tilde U)=1\}$; it is $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$ under $\mathsf{M}_2$, it is $Sp(2,\mathbb{C})$ as the same set, and it is $Spin(1,3)$ as the same set read through the Clifford algebra. Its quotient by $\{\pm e_t\}$ is $SO^{+}(1,3)=G/\{\pm e_t\}$, and that is the only row of the five that is not $G$: the covering is two-to-one, $\pi_1(G)=0$, $\pi_1(SO^{+}(1,3))=\mathbb{Z}_2$, and the two are therefore not isomorphic. The compact core is $SU(2)=G\cap\mathbb{B}_H$, the split units are $SL(2,\mathbb{R})$ with quotient $SO^{+}(2,1)$, and the Lorentz row of the group table is a quotient row: the operators $\mathrm{Hp}_{\tilde U}$ with $N(\tilde U)=1$, of determinant one, with kernel $\{\pm e_t\}$. At the operator level the endomorphism algebra is $M_4(\mathbb{C})$, the two real forms are the operators commuting with coefficient conjugation and the operators commuting with right quaternion multiplication, and the two-sided pairs give $\mathfrak{so}(4)$, $\mathfrak{so}(2,2)$ and $\mathfrak{so}(4,\mathbb{C})$ from the pure quaternions, from the traceless split elements, and from the vector part.

Table 7 takes the other axis and asks who uses each row: it names the theory, the symbols of the trade, and the object in biquaternion letters, and it records the three absences — colour, ten dimensions and the Virasoro algebra — each with its reason.

The failures are as instructive as the successes. $\mathfrak{so}(4)$ and $\mathfrak{so}(2,2)$ are not subspaces of $\mathbb{B}$ but pairs of operators, $\mathfrak{su}(n)$ for $n\geq3$ does not fit at all, $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ is a Jordan algebra and not a Lie algebra, $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ is the other way round, and $\mathfrak{sl}(4,\mathbb{C})$ is one dimension too large for two-sided multiplication to reach.

How to read the fourth column. Every entry is inside the algebra or inside its operator layer. The second column is written in the notation of the trade, where a symmetry is a matrix and a spinor is a column; the fourth column names the same object without leaving $\mathbb{B}$. Two translations do most of the work: a Hermitian element of $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ is a four-momentum, and a null one, an element of the zero-divisor cone, is a massless four-momentum; and the central circle is the phase that the Lorentz action cannot see, which is why it is available as a gauge phase.

The one absence that matters for particle physics. The Standard Model gauge algebra is twelve-dimensional and $\mathbb{B}_H$ is four-dimensional. Colour therefore does not fit: the maximal compact subgroup of $\mathbb{B}^{\times}$ is $U(2)$, of real dimension four, and $SU(3)$ needs eight, so no $SU(3)$ sits inside the algebra at all, and the centre of $\mathbb{B}$ being $\mathbb{C}$ forbids a rank-2 semisimple factor outright. What does fit is $\mathfrak{u}(2)=\mathfrak{su}(2)_L\oplus\mathfrak{u}(1)_Y$ as a real subspace, and with it the whole electroweak layer, the Higgs invariant, and the chiral split. Colour reappears one level up: in $\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{B})$, whose maximal compact subgroup is $U(4)$, the block $SU(3)\oplus U(1)$ sits inside $U(3)\subset U(4)$. The biquaternion algebra is an electroweak algebra, not a Standard Model algebra. That verdict is a Layer-A verdict, and the frame adopts a criterion that makes it non-final. The sentence is true of the algebra's own action: colour is not a subalgebra and no colour generator is an element. It is not a verdict on which groups the framework may use. The carrier, the canonical split $\mathbb C e_t\oplus B_V$ and the two-sided action license $\mathrm{SU}(4)\times\mathrm{SU}(2)_L\times\mathrm{SU}(2)_R$ to act on a formal copy, with $\mathrm{SU}(3)=\mathrm{Stab}_{\mathrm{SU}(4)}(\mathbb C e_t)$. So the correct pair of statements is: the algebra is not a Standard Model algebra, and its structures license the Pati–Salam group. The published frame article, §Where Each Factor Comes From, and How Firmly, states the test; The Colour Wall, Restated says what the licence does not buy.

The two other absences, and their reason. The target space of string theory needs $Spin(1,9)$ and M-theory needs $Spin(1,10)$; $\mathbb{B}$ is four-dimensional and reaches only the Lorentzian and the Euclidean four-dimensional spin groups. Two-dimensional conformal field theory needs the Virasoro algebra, which is infinite-dimensional; $\mathbb{B}$ is finite-dimensional and supplies only its three-dimensional global part. In both cases the limit is the dimension of the algebra and not the taste of the author.
