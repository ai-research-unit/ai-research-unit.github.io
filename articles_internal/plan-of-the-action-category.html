Plan of the Category Biquaternion Universe Action

What this file is. The eight articles the category names and does not have, written down so that a writing pass does not have to rediscover them. Each entry gives the title, the section it belongs to, the object, the claim, and the warrant the claim needs. Nothing here is an article: the menu states each of these in the comment of its section, in one sentence, and no entry of the menu links to them, because a link to a file that does not exist is a broken link and the build reads physics.md for its sitemap.

The rule these titles obey. The title names the object and the result. A reader who has not read the abstract must be able to say what is studied. One colon at most, left of it the object, right of it the construction or the result.

The Chain-Completing Four

These do not add physics. They fill a place the chain already uses.

1. The Inverse Problem of the Calculus of Variations: Helmholtz's Conditions and Which Equations Have an Action

Section. The Lagrangian, as the second entry, after The Invariant Density.

Object. A second-order system of equations and the question whether it is some Lagrangian's Euler–Lagrange system.

Claim. Helmholtz's conditions, stated and proved for the four-dimensional case and then read on the corpus's own equations: the integrability conditions on the variational derivative are necessary and locally sufficient, and they fail for exactly the equations the corpus says have no action. That makes "the viscous term has no action" a theorem instead of an assertion, and it grades the fluid criterion, which asks the same question of one polar coordinate that Helmholtz asks of the system.

Warrant. The Helmholtz conditions written out; the check on the damped oscillator and on the Navier–Stokes viscous term, both failing; the check on Klein–Gordon, passing. The criterion's article is the cross-reference, not the duplicate.

Why it is worth writing. Three sections assert the absence of an action and none cites the theorem. A criterion that grades equations should rest on the theorem that grades them.

2. The Dirac–Bergmann Algorithm: First- and Second-Class Constraints and Which of the Algebra's Actions Are Singular

Section. The Lagrangian, last entry, after Hamiltonian Field Theory, whose irregular case it supplies.

Object. A Lagrangian whose Legendre transform is not regular, and the constraint algorithm that replaces it.

Claim. The algorithm in four steps — primary constraints, the consistency condition, the classification into first and second class, the Dirac bracket — and then the corpus's own actions classified: which have a singular Hessian, which constraints are first class, and what the gauge freedom of each is. The Gauss law of the electromagnetic action is the worked case, since the corpus already states it as a constraint rather than a choice of representative.

Warrant. The Hessian computed for the scalar, the Proca and the connection actions; the constraint count matching the degrees-of-freedom count the corpus states elsewhere; the Dirac bracket's Jacobi identity checked.

Why it is worth writing. The Hamiltonian article calls the Legendre transform regular and the corpus states constraints in five separate places. The general statement is missing, and it is the one that makes the five consistent.

3. Belinfante–Rosenfeld: Why the Canonical Stress Tensor Is Not Symmetric and What the Improvement Adds

Section. From the Action to the Field Equation, immediately after Stress–Energy, Conservation Laws and the Field Action.

Object. The canonical Noether tensor, its antisymmetric part, and the improvement term built from the spin density.

Claim. The canonical tensor is conserved but not symmetric, and it is not symmetric in proportion to the spin the field carries. The improvement term makes it symmetric without changing the conserved charges, and the symmetric tensor is the one whose spatial block is a stress — which is the object the fluid's closure imports as $p\,\delta_{ij}$ plus a shear. The corpus's Frenkel condition and its spin-half tensor are the two instances.

Warrant. The improvement term constructed for the Dirac field and for the Proca field; the conservation of the improved tensor checked; the charges shown unchanged; the spin-half article's tensor recovered as the instance.

Why it is worth writing. The stress–energy article gives $T_{tt}$ and $T_{j\imath}$ for a scalar, and the closure takes a pressure. For a field with spin the two are not the same object, and nothing in the corpus says so.

4. The Chemical Potential Is the Phase's Frequency: $\mu=-\partial_t\varphi$ and the Charge It Is Conjugate To

Section. From the Fluid Equations to Thermodynamics Equations, in The Local Thermal State and the Three Tests, after Covariant Local Equilibrium and Tolman Temperature.

Object. The chemical potential, which the closure writes and never derives.

Claim. In this framework $\mu$ is not a new parameter. The phase of the polar word is the action, $-\partial_t\varphi$ is its energy, and the Noether charge of the central phase symmetry is the particle number the potential is conjugate to. The Gibbs–Duhem relation the closure's consistency test needs then holds identically, and the sign that the test is sensitive to is the sign of $\partial_t\varphi$ and nothing else.

Warrant. The identification made on the charged scalar and on the Dirac field, where the phase symmetry is already stated; the Gibbs–Duhem relation derived rather than assumed; the sign checked against the Tolman article.

Why it is worth writing. The three tests have a parameter they take as given. One of them, consistency, is about the relation between the two. Deriving $\mu$ turns the test into a check.

The Four New Statements

These are not missing steps. They are results the category's own material makes available and no article states.

5. The Free-Energy Functional at Finite Temperature: The Effective Potential Whose Minimum Is the State

Section. From the Fluid Equations to Thermodynamics Equations, in The State and Its Functionals, after The Partition Function.

Object. The effective potential at $T\neq0$, and the statement that its minimum is the equilibrium state.

Claim. The zero-temperature Coleman–Weinberg potential exists in the corpus, in the electroweak sector. At finite temperature the same construction with the thermal propagator gives a potential whose minimum is the state the partition function selects, and whose curvature is the mass. That turns the closure's equation of state from a stipulation into a variation: the pressure is the potential's value at the minimum, not an input.

Warrant. The one-loop thermal potential computed for the scalar quartic; the minimum located; the pressure and the entropy density read off the potential and compared with the closure's own expressions; the high-temperature expansion checked against the standard $T^4$ behaviour.

Why it is worth writing. Without it the closure assumes its equation of state. With it the closure has a variational principle, and the category has one variational principle per arrow.

6. The Closed-Time-Path Action: One Action on Two Branches and Why Dissipation Needs It

Section. From the Fluid Equations to Thermodynamics Equations, opening The Departures from Equilibrium.

Object. The in-in action on the Schwinger–Keldysh contour.

Claim. A dissipative flow does have an action — on a contour that runs forward and back — and the dissipation is the imaginary part of the effective action. That resolves a contradiction the corpus states in three places: the viscous term has no action (Rayleigh, the criterion), and the Schwinger–Keldysh formalism keeps a real-time history. Both are true, and the object that makes them compatible is the doubled action.

Warrant. The doubled action written for the scalar; the influence functional obtained by integrating out the environment; its imaginary part shown positive and identified with the Rayleigh dissipation function; the Israel–Stewart relaxation time recovered as the coefficient of the quadratic term.

Why it is worth writing. It is the only addition that converts the departures from a list into a derivation, and it removes a contradiction rather than adding a topic.

7. The Three Scales: $c$, $\hbar$ and $k_B$ as the Three Continuations

Section. Other aspects.

Object. The category's own shape, stated as a result.

Claim. Each of the three constants is one continuation of the single premise, and each belongs to one arrow: $c$ to the relativistic structure, which is the algebra's own signature; $\hbar$ to the promotion, where the bracket becomes a commutator and $k_m=mc/\hbar$ appears; $k_B$ to the thermal closure, where the inverse temperature is a rapidity of the modular flow. The three are independent because no two of them compose, which is why the corpus has three arrows and not one, and why the non-quantum and non-relativistic limits are different operations.

Warrant. The three appearances of each constant collected from the articles that state them; the claim that no pair composes argued from the failure of the corresponding limits to commute, which two articles already record; the map from constant to arrow drawn once.

Why it is worth writing. The category's shape is currently a layout. This makes it a result, and it is the natural closing article.

8. Ostrogradsky, or Why No Rung Goes Past Two Derivatives

Section. Other aspects.

Object. Higher-derivative Lagrangians and the instability they carry.

Claim. Every rung's action is second order in the field, and the fluid's Korteweg stress is second order in the density. A third derivative buys a ghost, by Ostrogradsky's theorem, and in this algebra the ghost is sharper than usual: the Hermitian form is indefinite, so the negative-norm mode is not a technicality but a state the corpus already carries. The second variation, read as Legendre–Hadamard, then says which of the corpus's stationary points are minima.

Warrant. Ostrogradsky's construction in the two-derivative-plus case; the ghost's energy computed; the indefinite form shown to make the ghost a negative-norm state; the second variation of the scalar action's stationary points classified.

Why it is worth writing. It explains a uniformity the corpus exhibits everywhere and never justifies, and it gives the stability question a home.

What This Plan Does Not Do

It does not decide the order of writing beyond the section each article joins. It does not restate the corpus's conventions: an article written from this plan follows the template, reads its section's neighbours, and cites them. It does not create menu entries: when one of these is written, its entry goes into the section named above, with its comment, in the position named above.
