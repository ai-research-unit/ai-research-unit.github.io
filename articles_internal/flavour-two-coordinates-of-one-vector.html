Two Coordinates of One Vector: Foot's Angle, the Phase $2/9$, and the $N$-Rung Form

This is the second of four notes that replace Flavour Research: The Ladder Arithmetic. It carries the geometry: why the ratio is one coordinate of a vector and not a number, where the second coordinate comes from, and what the two coordinates can and cannot explain. The measurement, the purity reading and the fourth rung are in Koide's Ratio: The Bound, the Purity, and the Fourth Rung; the neutrino sector is in The Neutrino Closure; the stabiliser argument is in The Stabiliser of the Line.

Introduction

The ratio $Q$ has a geometric reading, and Foot gave it in 1994: put the three square roots of the masses at the coordinates of a vector, and the ratio is the cosine of the angle between that vector and the diagonal. On the leptons the angle is $45^{\circ}$, and $45^{\circ}$ is the statement that the spread of the roots equals their mean.

That reading uses one coordinate. A vector in three dimensions has a direction and a position around the diagonal, and the second coordinate has a name: the phase $\delta$, the azimuth of the triple on its cone. Measured on the leptons, $\delta=0.222\,229\,6$ rad, and $2/9=0.222\,222\,2$ rad.

This note does three things.

It writes the geometry out step by step, because the content of the reading is a single cancellation and the cancellation is worth seeing rather than quoting.

It shows that the phase is not a second assumption. Every triple has a representation with a mean, a radius and a phase, exactly, as a change of coordinates; the closure fixes the radius and leaves the phase free. So the geometry contains one number, not two, and the free one is $\delta$.

It collects the candidates for $\delta$, including the only one that is a mathematical identity: on a circle sampled at three equal points the Green's function contributes $2\pi^2/9$, so $2/9$ is a lattice constant of the circle and not a fitted fraction.

The last part of the note is the general form for $N$ rungs, where the same $45^{\circ}$ appears in every dimension and the closure value $2/N$ is shown to be the normalisation rather than a separate fact.

1. The Two Vectors

[PUBLISHED] R. Foot (hep-ph/9402242, 1994) makes the reading. Put the three square roots at the coordinates of a vector, and put the symmetric direction beside it:

$$ \vec r=\left(\sqrt{\rho_1},\sqrt{\rho_2},\sqrt{\rho_3}\right), \qquad \vec u=(1,1,1), \qquad \rho_i=\frac{m_i}{m_1}. $$

The vector $\vec r$ carries the spectrum and no scale, because the normalisation by $m_1$ cancels in every ratio below. The vector $\vec u$ carries nothing and is the reference direction.

[ALGEBRA] Two vectors and one angle are enough, because $Q$ depends on the direction of $\vec r$ and not on its length. This section shows that, and the showing is the point: a ratio of two sums turns out to be a function of one angle.

2. The Numerator Is a Squared Length

[ALGEBRA] The numerator of $Q$ is

$$ 1+\rho_2+\rho_3=\vec r\cdot\vec r=\lVert\vec r\rVert^{2}, $$

because the coordinates of $\vec r$ are the square roots and squaring them returns the masses. Nothing is assumed; this is what the coordinates are.

3. The Denominator Is the Square of One Dot Product

[ALGEBRA] The denominator is the sum of the coordinates, squared:

$$ \left(1+\sqrt{\rho_2}+\sqrt{\rho_3}\right)^{2}=\left(\vec u\cdot\vec r\right)^{2}, $$

because $\vec u$ has three unit entries and the dot product therefore collects the three coordinates.

4. The Angle Enters, and the Length Cancels

[ALGEBRA] With $\varphi$ the angle between $\vec r$ and $\vec u$,

$$ \vec u\cdot\vec r=\lVert\vec u\rVert\,\lVert\vec r\rVert\cos\varphi=\sqrt3\,\lVert\vec r\rVert\cos\varphi . $$

Dividing the numerator of §2 by the denominator of §3,

$$ Q=\frac{\lVert\vec r\rVert^{2}}{3\lVert\vec r\rVert^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi}=\frac{1}{3\cos^{2}\varphi}. $$

The length cancels. So the closure ratio is a function of the angle and of nothing else, and the statement $Q=2/3$ is the statement

$$ \cos^{2}\varphi=\frac12, \qquad \varphi=45^{\circ}. $$

This is the whole content of the geometric reading, and it is one line of algebra. Everything below is the consequences of that line.

The cone. Inverting the relation, $\varphi=\arccos\left(1/\sqrt{3Q}\right)$. As $Q$ runs over its allowed range $[1/3,1]$, the angle runs from $0$ to $54.736^{\circ}$, so the closure sits at a definite latitude on a cone about the diagonal. The leptons sit on that latitude; the quarks sit above it.

6. The Angle Is the Variance

[ALGEBRA] The angle has a statistical name. Write $\mu$ for the mean of the three coordinates and split $\vec r$ into the part along the diagonal and the part orthogonal to it:

$$ \vec r=\mu\,\vec u+\vec v, \qquad \lVert\vec v\rVert^{2}=\sum_i\left(\sqrt{\rho_i}-\mu\right)^{2}=3\sigma^{2}, $$

where $\sigma^{2}$ is the variance of the roots and the factor three is the number of coordinates. Then

$$ \tan\varphi=\frac{\lVert\vec v\rVert}{\lVert\mu\vec u\rVert}=\frac{\sqrt3\,\sigma}{\sqrt3\,\mu}=\frac{\sigma}{\mu}, \qquad Q=\frac{1+\tan^{2}\varphi}{3}. $$

Measured, $\sigma/\mu=0.999\,990\,8$ and $\varphi=44.9997^{\circ}$.

So the closure says the three roots are as spread as their own mean. That is the only shape statement the closure makes, and it is scale-free: $\sigma/\mu$ is invariant under $\sqrt{\rho_i}\to\lambda\sqrt{\rho_i}$.

8. The Phase Is the Azimuth

[ALGEBRA] The angle $\varphi$ fixes the direction of $\vec r$ relative to $\vec u$ but not its position around $\vec u$. A second angle is needed, and one representation supplies it. Every triple of roots has exactly one representation

$$ \sqrt{\rho_i}=\mu+R\cos\left(\delta+\frac{2\pi i}{3}\right), \qquad i=1,2,3, $$

with $\mu$ the mean of the roots and $R\ge0$. Three parameters, three roots, no assumption: this is a change of coordinates, and it reproduces any triple exactly. Checked on $[1,2,4]$ and on $[1,1,10]$, both to machine precision, and on the leptons with $\mu=24.782\,49$, $R=35.047\,41$, $\delta=0.222\,229\,6$.

Two sums carry the whole of the closure in this language, and each is checked at $\delta=0$, where the cosines are $1$, $-1/2$, $-1/2$:

sum value at $\delta=0$
$\sum_i\cos\left(\delta+2\pi i/3\right)$ $0$ $1-\tfrac12-\tfrac12=0$
$\sum_i\cos^{2}\left(\delta+2\pi i/3\right)$ $3/2$ $1+\tfrac14+\tfrac14=1.5$

With those two, the numerator and the denominator follow in one line each:

$$ \sum_i \rho_i=3\mu^{2}+\tfrac32 R^{2}, \qquad \left(\sum_i \sqrt{\rho_i}\right)^{2}=9\mu^{2}, $$

so

$$ Q=\frac{3\mu^{2}+\tfrac32R^{2}}{9\mu^{2}}=\frac13\left(1+\frac{R^{2}}{2\mu^{2}}\right). $$

$\delta$ does not appear. $Q$ is blind to the phase, and the closure is a statement about the radius:

$$ Q=\frac23\iff R=\sqrt2\,\mu . $$

The ratio $R/(\sqrt2\mu)$ is $\tan\varphi$ of §6, so the radius and the polar angle are one number. The circle is the locus $R=\sqrt2\mu$; the phase is the position on it.

9. The $120^{\circ}$ Spacing Is a Projection

[ALGEBRA] It is often said that the circle ansatz assumes equal spacing. At $N=3$ it does not, and the proof is three lines. Let $\hat e_i$ be the three coordinate axes of $\mathbb R^{3}$ and project each onto the plane orthogonal to $\hat u$:

$$ \vec p_i=\hat e_i-\left(\hat e_i\cdot\hat u\right)\hat u=\hat e_i-\tfrac13(1,1,1). $$

Then, because $\hat e_i\cdot\hat e_j=\delta_{ij}$ and $\lVert\hat u\rVert=1$,

$$ \vec p_i\cdot\vec p_j=\delta_{ij}-\tfrac13, \qquad \lVert\vec p_i\rVert^{2}=\tfrac23 . $$

For $i\neq j$ the cosine between the projections is $\left(-\tfrac13\right)/\left(\tfrac23\right)=-\tfrac12$, which is $120^{\circ}$. So the three roots are the components of one vector along three directions forced to be equally spaced, and nothing else could happen: the spacing is the dimension of the space, not a choice about it.

The same construction in $N$ dimensions gives $\cos\theta_{ij}=-1/(N-1)$:

$N$ projected angle
$2$ $180^{\circ}$
$3$ $120^{\circ}$
$4$ $109.47^{\circ}$
$5$ $104.48^{\circ}$
$\to\infty$ $90^{\circ}$

So the $2\pi/N$ spacing used in the rung fits is not this projection for any $N\neq3$; at $N=4$ the projected spacing is $109.47^{\circ}$, not $90^{\circ}$. What the circle ansatz assumes is the radius condition $R=\sqrt2\mu$, which is $Q=2/3$ itself, together with the value of $\delta$. The spacing it does not assume.

The convention is part of the number and is stated once. $\delta$ is defined by $i=1,2,3$ attached to $e,\mu,\tau$ and by the plus sign in $\cos(\delta+2\pi i/3)$, so the $e$ rung carries the argument $\delta+120^{\circ}$ and the $\tau$ rung carries $\delta$. Relabelling the rungs shifts $\delta$ by $120^{\circ}$, and using a minus sign replaces $\delta$ by $-\delta$. The number $2/9$ means nothing without the convention.

11. The $Z_3$ Cosine: A Change of Basis

[PUBLISHED] The form used in the literature for the same content is

$$ m_k=\left(a_0+2a_8\cos\left(\delta+\frac{2\pi k}{3}\right)\right)^{2}, $$

with $a_0$ the singlet amplitude and $a_8$ the octet amplitude of a $U(3)_F$ flavon. Written with the mean root $\mu$ and the coefficient $k$ of §12, this is

$$ \sqrt{\rho_i}=\mu\left(1+\sqrt2\,k\cos\left(\delta+\frac{2\pi i}{3}\right)\right), $$

so $\mu=a_0$ and the cosine coefficient is $\sqrt2k=2a_8/a_0$.

[ALGEBRA] The closure is the value of one coefficient. Summing,

$$ \sum_i\sqrt{\rho_i}=3\mu, \qquad \sum_i\rho_i=3\mu^{2}\left(1+k^{2}\right), $$

hence

$$ Q=\frac{1+k^{2}}{3}, \qquad Q=\frac23\iff k=1 . $$

Koide is $k=1$. The coefficient is not fitted; it is $Q$ under another name, which is why the form is a change of basis and not a derivation.

12. The Two Shape Parameters Are Moments

[ALGEBRA] Shift to the mean and compute the moments of the three roots:

$$ \sum_i\left(\sqrt{\rho_i}-\mu\right)^{2}=\tfrac32R^{2}, \qquad \sum_i\left(\sqrt{\rho_i}-\mu\right)^{3}=\tfrac34R^{3}\cos3\delta . $$

Both are verified on the leptons: $1842.481$ against $\tfrac32R^{2}=1842.481$, and $25373.6$ against $\tfrac34R^{3}\cos3\delta=25373.6$. Dividing by three,

$$ \sigma^{2}=\frac{R^{2}}{2}, \qquad \cos3\delta=\frac{4}{3R^{3}}\sum_i\left(\sqrt{\rho_i}-\mu\right)^{3}. $$

The coefficient is the variance of the roots in units of their mean; the phase is their third moment. Koide is a statement about the second moment; $\delta$ is the skewness direction, and it is the third moment because a symmetric triple has none.

moment value in this language what fixes it measured (leptons)
first $\sum_i\left(\sqrt{\rho_i}-\mu\right)=0$ the definition of $\mu$ $0$ by construction
second $\sum_i\left(\sqrt{\rho_i}-\mu\right)^{2}=\tfrac32R^{2}$ $k$, hence $Q$ $1842.481$
third $\sum_i\left(\sqrt{\rho_i}-\mu\right)^{3}=\tfrac34R^{3}\cos3\delta$ $\cos3\delta$ $25373.6$

A triple has two shape degrees of freedom, so two moments exhaust it. Nothing else is free, and no fourth relation can be independent of the first three.

13. Positivity, the Window, and the Cone

[ALGEBRA] Requiring all three masses positive requires all three roots positive, which confines $\delta$. With $R=\sqrt2\mu$ and $\sqrt{\rho_i}/\mu=1+\sqrt2\cos(\delta+2\pi i/3)$, the smallest root vanishes at $1+\sqrt2\cos(\delta+120^{\circ})=0$, that is at $\delta=\pm15^{\circ}$ modulo $120^{\circ}$. So

$$ \delta\in\left(-15^{\circ},15^{\circ}\right), $$

and the window is exactly the set of phases for which the lightest rung still has a positive mass. The measured $\delta=12.7328^{\circ}$ sits $2.267^{\circ}$ inside the boundary.

14. The $N$-Rung Form: $45^{\circ}$ in Every Dimension

14.1 The shape vector

[ALGEBRA] Write the $N$ roots as $\sqrt{\rho_i}$, $i=0,\dots,N-1$. Normalising the shape out of the radial parameters,

$$ \sqrt{\rho_i}=\mu\left(1+k\,n_i\right), \qquad \sum_i n_i=0, \qquad \sum_i n_i^{2}=N, \qquad k=\frac{\sigma}{\mu}, $$

with $\sigma$ the standard deviation of the roots. For $N=3$ this is $\mu\left(1+\sqrt2k\cos\left(\delta+2\pi i/3\right)\right)$ and nothing else, which is §11 with the relation between $k$ and $R$ made explicit.

The real Fourier basis on $Z_N$ has exactly $N$ functions, so the decomposition

$$ \sqrt{\rho_i}=\mu+\sum_{r=1}^{\lfloor (N-1)/2\rfloor}R_r\cos\left(\phi_r+\frac{2\pi r i}{N}\right) \;+\; \begin{cases} \beta\,(-1)^{i}, & N \text{ even},\\[2pt] 0, & N \text{ odd}, \end{cases} $$

is exact for every spectrum, with $N$ real parameters. Verified on random spectra for $N=1,\dots,7$: the largest error is $1.1\times10^{-14}$.

Two structural remarks matter. The last term is real, so an even count has no phase in its top mode, only a sign. And $N=3$ is the only case with one mode: there is no relative amplitude to choose, the shape is one angle, and dividing out the scale leaves a point on a circle. That is where $\delta$ comes from, and it is where it stops.

14.2 The proof, in two lines, for every $N$

[ALGEBRA] By the parallel-axis identity, which needs only $\sum_i\left(\sqrt{\rho_i}-\mu\right)=0$,

$$ \sum_i \rho_i=N\mu^{2}\left(1+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right), \qquad \left(\sum_i \sqrt{\rho_i}\right)^{2}=N^{2}\mu^{2}, $$

so

$$ Q_N\equiv\frac{\sum_i \rho_i}{\left(\sum_i \sqrt{\rho_i}\right)^{2}}=\frac{1}{N}\left(1+k^{2}\right), \qquad k=\sqrt{NQ_N-1}. $$

Verified on $500$ random spectra for each $N$ from $1$ to $7$: the largest relative error is $5\times10^{-16}$.

statement $N=3$ general $N$
closure $Q=2/3$ $Q_N=2/N$
coefficient $k=1$ $k=1$
variance $\sigma=\mu$ $\sigma=\mu$
angle to the diagonal $45^{\circ}$ $45^{\circ}$
value $\left(1+k^{2}\right)/3$ $\left(1+k^{2}\right)/N$

So Koide's relation is exactly "the spread of the roots equals their mean", and the value $2/N$ is a consequence of the $1/N$ in the normalisation, not a separate fact. The $2$ in $2/N$ is $k^{2}=1$.

14.3 The bounds, and why two rungs cannot close

[ALGEBRA] Two limits bracket the coefficient: $k=0$ is the degenerate spectrum, and $k$ is largest when one root carries everything, which gives $k\to\sqrt{N-1}$. Hence

$$ 0\le k\le\sqrt{N-1}, \qquad \frac1N\le Q_N\le1 . $$

At $N=2$ the parametrisation is explicit:

$$ \sqrt{\rho_1}=\mu(1+k), \qquad \sqrt{\rho_2}=\mu(1-k), \qquad Q_2=\frac{1+k^{2}}{2}. $$

So $Q_2=1$ requires $k=1$, and $k=1$ means $\sqrt{\rho_2}=0$. The closure at $N=2$ forces a massless member, so it is not a closure at all. For $N\ge3$ a spread equal to the mean is available with every member positive: at the most uniform direction $k=1$ leaves a smallest root of $0.2929\mu$ at $N=3$ and $0.5528\mu$ at $N=6$.

14.4 The closure is $45^{\circ}$ in every dimension

[ALGEBRA] With $\vec u=(1,\dots,1)$ and $\vec r$ in $N$ dimensions,

$$ Q_N=\frac{\lVert\vec r\rVert^{2}}{\left(\vec u\cdot\vec r\right)^{2}}=\frac{1}{N\cos^{2}\varphi}, $$

so $Q_N=2/N$ is $\cos^{2}\varphi=1/2$, that is $\varphi=45^{\circ}$, in every dimension. Verified at the closure point for $N=2,\dots,6$: $Q_N=2/N$ to $10^{-8}$ and $\varphi=45.0000^{\circ}$ each time.

What changes with $N$ is the range of $\varphi$: from $0$ on the diagonal to $\arccos\left(1/\sqrt N\right)$ in the fully hierarchical limit, which is $45.0000^{\circ}$ at $N=2$, $54.7356^{\circ}$ at $N=3$, $60.0000^{\circ}$ at $N=4$, tending to $90^{\circ}$. So $45^{\circ}$ is interior for $N\ge3$ and is the endpoint at $N=2$, which is a third way to see why two rungs cannot close. The cone exists in every dimension; the leptons sit on it at $N=3$.

15. The Singlet–Octet Balance

[PUBLISHED] The $U(3)_F$ reading of the same relation is a statement about two invariants (arXiv 2608.19277). A flavon nonet $\Phi$ splits into a singlet and an octet part, and with the $Z_3$-cosine eigenvalues the two traces are

$$ I_{(0)}=\operatorname{Tr}\Phi_{(0)}^{2}=3a_0^{2}, \qquad I_{(8)}=\operatorname{Tr}\Phi_{(8)}^{2}=6a_8^{2}, $$

so that $\sum_i\lambda_i=3a_0$ and $\sum_i\lambda_i^{2}=I_{(0)}+I_{(8)}$.

[ALGEBRA] With the masses the squares of the eigenvalues, $\sqrt{m_i}\propto\lambda_i$, and

$$ Q=\frac{\sum_i\lambda_i^{2}}{\left(\sum_i\lambda_i\right)^{2}} =\frac{I_{(0)}+I_{(8)}}{3I_{(0)}} =\frac13\left(1+\frac{I_{(8)}}{I_{(0)}}\right). $$

The relation is exactly the equality of the two invariants. $Q=2/3$ is $I_{(0)}=I_{(8)}$, which is $a_0^{2}=2a_8^{2}$; and since $I_{(8)}/I_{(0)}=k^{2}$, the coefficient of §11 is the square root of the invariant ratio.

quantity value on the leptons
$Q$ $0.666\,660\,51$
$I_{(8)}/I_{(0)}=3Q-1$ $0.999\,981\,5$
distance of the ratio from $1$ $1.85\times10^{-5}$

So the lepton data say that the two invariants of the family matrix are equal to two parts in $10^5$. The factor is three larger than the six parts in a million by which $Q$ misses $2/3$, because $Q\mapsto3Q-1$ has slope three; it is one number in another coordinate, and it is the cleanest available translation of Koide into the language of a potential.

[PUBLISHED] The potential that produces the equality is

$$ V=-\mu_F^{2}\left(I_{(0)}+I_{(8)}\right)+\kappa_F\left(I_{(0)}+I_{(8)}\right)^{2}+\kappa_F'\left(I_{(0)}-I_{(8)}\right)^{2}, $$

whose stationarity conditions give $I_{(0)}=I_{(8)}=\mu_F^{2}/(2\kappa_F)$ for $\kappa_F>0$, $\kappa_F'>0$. The cross term is allowed by the symmetry, needs no tuning of its coefficient, and its minimum is exactly the relation.

What this does and does not buy. It gives the value $2/3$ a mechanism of a kind: a term that the symmetry allows, whose minimum is equipartition. It does not give the coefficient $\kappa_F'$ a derivation, and it says nothing about the phase, which the paper that states it fits and declares out of scope. The honest reading is that the relation is the statement that a matrix's singlet and octet invariants carry equal weight, and that a symmetry-allowed quartic prefers that point. Why the quartic is there is a separate question.

16. Four Candidates for $2/9$

The phase is the one free number the geometry leaves. Four things have been offered for it. One is an identity, one is a rule that fails, one is a restatement, and one is a normalisation that nobody has derived.

16.1 The circle's Green function

[PUBLISHED] On a circle sampled at three equal points, the Green's function sum is an exact lattice constant (arXiv 2605.10245):

$$ \sum_{n\ge1}\frac{1-\cos\left(2\pi n/3\right)}{n^{2}}=\frac{2\pi^{2}}{9}. $$

[ALGEBRA] The proof is elementary and worth having. The mask vanishes when $n\equiv0$ modulo three and equals $3/2$ otherwise, so

$$ \sum_{n\ge1}\frac{1-\cos\left(2\pi n/3\right)}{n^{2}} =\frac32\left(1-\frac19\right)\zeta(2) =\frac32\cdot\frac89\cdot\frac{\pi^{2}}{6} =\frac{2\pi^{2}}{9}. $$

Verified numerically: the partial sum to three million terms is $2.193\,245\,09$ against $2\pi^{2}/9=2.193\,245\,42$, which agrees to the seven digits the truncation allows.

Why it is the best candidate. The phase in this reading is $\theta=-2\lambda_B/9$ with $\lambda_B$ a matching coefficient of the compactification, so the numeral $2/9$ is not fitted: it is the value of a convergent series fixed by the spacing of the three points. A mechanism that produces $2/9$ from a compact direction needs no coincidence, only a circle with three equally spaced points on it.

What stands in the way. $\lambda_B$ is undetermined, so the derivation fixes the shape and not the value. At $\lambda_B=1$ the phase is $2/9$ and the paper's own benchmark misses the measured electron-to-muon ratio by $9.8\times10^{-6}$ relative, a $452\sigma$ offset against experimental errors that the theory does not explain. So the identity is real and the normalisation is an assumption, and the note must keep the two apart.

16.2 The hypercharge rule

[FAILED] The proposal $\delta=\left(1+\left|Y\right|\right)/9$ gives $\delta=2/9$ for the leptons, whose hypercharge is $-1$, and it was once read as a second success for the up-type quarks. It fails its own sum rule: the rule assigns the phase from a charge and then does not reproduce the masses it was fitted to when the charges are summed. It is recorded and not used.

16.3 Rosen's parametrisation

[PUBLISHED] Rosen writes the masses as $\sqrt{m_g}\propto1+\sqrt2\cos\left(2\pi g/3+2/9\right)$ with integer $g$. Written out, the three roots are $0.040\,350$, $0.580\,212$, $2.379\,438$ in units of the smallest, and the mass ratios are $0.004\,836\,28$ and $0.000\,287\,565$ against measured $0.004\,836\,33$ and $0.000\,287\,574$.

[ALGEBRA] That is the same formula as §11 at $k=1$ and $\delta=2/9$, so it is a restatement and not a fourth candidate. Its value is that it fixes the convention: it shows that the natural variable is an integer generation index and that the phase is one number added to a $Z_3$ sequence. $Q$ computed from those roots is $0.666\,666\,666\,667$, exact, as §11 requires.

16.4 What would settle it

A derivation would need a number that multiplies $2/9$ and is not chosen. The three shapes such a number could take are a holonomy, a stiffness, and an endpoint charge; the compactification paper names all three and derives none. Until one of them is computed, $2/9$ is a constant of a model and not of nature, and the note should say so in the same sentence in which it quotes the agreement.

17. What the Two Lenses Do Not Do

  1. They do not explain $2/3$. The angle is $2/3$; the phase parametrisation is a coordinate change. Both are exact and neither is a mechanism.
  2. They are not two independent facts. $\varphi$ and $\delta$ are the polar and azimuthal angles of one vector. A fit to one is a fit to the same three masses as the other.
  3. $2/9$ is not derived. The best candidate is a lattice constant with a free normalisation.
  4. $45^{\circ}$ is not fundamental because it is round. The invariant is $\lVert\vec v\rVert=r_{\parallel}$, equivalently $\sigma=\mu$.
  5. The phase is not evidence of order replacing chaos. Three numbers to three parameters is a reparametrisation. The only content is that $\delta$ is small and close to a short fraction, and small numbers of that kind are common in fitted triples.
  6. Neither coordinate fixes the other. $Q$ is blind to $\delta$ by §8, and the window of §13 shows that the light rung varies by a factor of fifty at fixed $Q$.

Summary

The closure ratio is one angle. With $\vec r$ the vector of square roots and $\vec u$ the diagonal, $Q=1/\left(3\cos^{2}\varphi\right)$, the length cancels, and $Q=2/3$ is $\varphi=45^{\circ}$. Equivalently $\sigma=\mu$: the roots are as spread as their own mean. The measured angle is $44.999\,74^{\circ}$.

The vector has a second coordinate. Every triple has an exact representation $\sqrt{\rho_i}=\mu+R\cos\left(\delta+2\pi i/3\right)$, in which $Q=\left(1+R^{2}/2\mu^{2}\right)/3$ and $\delta$ cancels. So the closure fixes the radius, $R=\sqrt2\mu$, and leaves the phase free. Measured, $\delta=0.222\,229\,6$ rad against $2/9=0.222\,222\,2$ rad.

The $120^{\circ}$ spacing is not an assumption. The projections of the three coordinate axes onto the plane orthogonal to the diagonal are equally spaced at $120^{\circ}$ because the space is three-dimensional, and at $N=4$ the same construction gives $109.47^{\circ}$.

The best candidate for the phase is a lattice constant: the Green's function of a circle sampled at three equal points sums to $2\pi^{2}/9$ exactly, verified to seven digits. It fixes the numeral and not the normalisation, and the model that states it misses the data by $452\sigma$ at unit normalisation.

In the language of a $U(3)_F$ flavon the relation is $Q=\left(1+I_{(8)}/I_{(0)}\right)/3$, so $Q=2/3$ is equipartition of the singlet and octet invariants, and the data put the two equal to two parts in $10^{5}$. A symmetry-allowed quartic with that minimum is known; the coefficient of the quartic is not derived.

For $N$ rungs, $Q_N=\left(1+k^{2}\right)/N$ with $k=\sigma/\mu$, so the closure is $k=1$ and $45^{\circ}$ in every dimension, the value $2/N$ is the normalisation, and $N=2$ cannot close because $k=1$ there forces a massless member.
