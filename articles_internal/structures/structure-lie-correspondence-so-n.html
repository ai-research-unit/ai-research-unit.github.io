The Lie Correspondence for the Rotations: $\mathfrak{so}(n)\leftrightarrow\mathrm{SO}(n)$

Introduction

The Lie correspondence for the rotations is the following set of statements about two objects, one linear and one curved:

$$ \mathfrak{so}(n)=\{X\in M_n(\mathbb R):X^{\mathsf T}=-X\}, \qquad \mathrm{SO}(n)=\{R\in M_n(\mathbb R):R^{\mathsf T}R=\mathrm I,\ \det R=1\}, $$

the first with the bracket $[X,Y]=XY-YX$, and the map $\exp(X)=\sum_{k\ge0}X^{k}/k!$ joining them. The linear object is a real vector space of dimension $n(n-1)/2$ closed under the bracket. The curved object is a compact Lie group of the same dimension. The claim is that the second is the first, exponentiated, and that the bracket is what the group law looks like at second order.

The claim, in five parts. Each part is a separate theorem, and each fails for some other pair of groups, so the five are not one statement in different words.

  • T1. For every $X$ in $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ the exponential $\exp X$ lies in $\mathrm{SO}(n)$.
  • T2. The tangent space of $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ at the identity is exactly $\mathfrak{so}(n)$, so the two spaces have the same dimension and the algebra is the linearisation of the group.
  • T3. The differential of $\exp$ at $0$ is the identity, so $\exp$ is a diffeomorphism of a neighbourhood of $0$ onto a neighbourhood of $\mathrm I$.
  • T4. For $n\ge2$ every rotation is an exponential: $\exp$ is onto $\mathrm{SO}(n)$.
  • T5. The bracket is the second-order term of the group law, so the algebra determines the group locally and not globally.

Why the five are separated. T1 is an algebraic identity and needs nothing but the series. T2 is a differentiation and needs nothing but the definitions. T3 is the inverse function theorem and needs no hypothesis at all, so it holds for every matrix group. T4 is the only one that uses compactness, and it is the one that fails for the non-compact real forms and survives for the complex forms by a different argument; the companion articles take up those two cases. T5 is a computation in the series that shows the bracket is not decoration.

What is taken as known. The orthogonal group, its two components and the fact that the determinant is a homomorphism onto $\{\pm1\}$ are The Special Orthogonal Groups over the Reals, §The Defining Equation. The general theory of the exponential on a Lie group, and the derivation of its differential, is The Lie Algebra and the Exponential Map. The normal form of a rotation with several angles is Simple and Non-Simple Rotations, and the cover by the spin group is The Rotor as a Square Root of a Rotation. This article proves the five statements in full, with the one exception of the Campbell–Baker–Hausdorff series, which is quoted and used only through its first two terms; the first two terms are proved here directly.

Conventions. Matrices act on $\mathbb R^{n}$ with the standard inner product. The transpose is written $A^{\mathsf T}$, the identity matrix $\mathrm I$, the trace $\operatorname{tr}$, the operator norm $\lVert A\rVert$ subordinate to the Euclidean norm on $\mathbb R^{n}$. The elementary antisymmetric matrices are $X_{ij}=E_{ij}-E_{ji}$ for $i<j$, with $E_{ij}$ the matrix of the single entry $1$ in row $i$ and column $j$. The plane generator in the $(i,j)$ plane, in the basis adapted to that plane, is $J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, and the rotation by the angle $\theta$ in that plane is $R(\theta)=\exp(\theta J)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$. No physics is invoked.

The Two Objects

The Algebra

The set $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ is a real vector space because the condition $X^{\mathsf T}=-X$ is linear, and it is closed under the bracket:

Lemma. If $X^{\mathsf T}=-X$ and $Y^{\mathsf T}=-Y$ then $[X,Y]^{\mathsf T}=- [X,Y]$.

Proof. $(XY-YX)^{\mathsf T}=Y^{\mathsf T}X^{\mathsf T}-X^{\mathsf T}Y^{\mathsf T}=(-Y)(-X)-(-X)(-Y)=YX-XY=-[X,Y]$.

So $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ is a Lie algebra, and it is a Lie algebra of matrices, so its bracket is the ordinary commutator. The same computation shows that the bracket of two antisymmetric matrices is anti-self-adjoint for every field, which is why T1 repeats without change over $\mathbb C$.

The basis. An antisymmetric matrix has zero diagonal and is determined by its entries above the diagonal, so the $X_{ij}$ with $i<j$ form a basis and

$$ \dim\mathfrak{so}(n)=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}. $$

The brackets follow from $E_{ij}E_{kl}=\delta_{jk}E_{il}$:

$$ [X_{ij},X_{kl}]=\delta_{jk}X_{il}-\delta_{ik}X_{jl}-\delta_{jl}X_{ik}+\delta_{il}X_{jk}. $$

At $n=2$ there is one generator and the bracket vanishes identically. At $n=3$ the three generators close on themselves with the structure constants of the cross product, and the algebra is not abelian. That difference between $n=2$ and $n=3$ is the first appearance of the parity that runs through the whole ladder.

The Group

The orthogonal group is $\mathrm O(n)=\{R:R^{\mathsf T}R=\mathrm I\}$ and the special orthogonal group is the kernel of the determinant on it. The determinant takes only the values $\pm1$ on $\mathrm O(n)$, because $\det(R)^{2}=\det(R^{\mathsf T}R)=1$, so the two cosets are $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ and $\mathrm{SO}(n)^{-}=\{R\in\mathrm O(n):\det R=-1\}$.

Proposition. $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ and $\mathrm{SO}(n)^{-}$ are the two connected components of $\mathrm O(n)$, and $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is the component of the identity.

Proof. The determinant is continuous and takes two values, so it is constant on a path; hence no path joins the two cosets. That $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ itself is connected is T4 below, since then every rotation is a path $t\mapsto\exp(tX)$ from the identity, and T4 is proved without using this proposition. Until that point the connectedness may be read from The Special Orthogonal Groups over the Reals, §The Determinant and the Two Cosets.

The set of rotations is bounded, since every column has norm one, and closed, since it is the preimage of a point under a continuous map. So $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is compact. That compactness is used exactly once in this article, in T4, and the use is isolated there on purpose.

The Exponential and Its Convergence

For $X\in M_n(\mathbb R)$ put

$$ \exp X=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{X^{k}}{k!}. $$

Proposition. The series converges absolutely for every $X$, uniformly on bounded sets, and defines a continuous map $M_n(\mathbb R)\to\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb R)$.

Proof. The operator norm is submultiplicative, $\lVert XY\rVert\le\lVert X\rVert\,\lVert Y\rVert$, so $\lVert X^{k}\rVert\le\lVert X\rVert^{k}$ and the series is dominated term by term by $\sum_k\lVert X\rVert^{k}/k!=e^{\lVert X\rVert}$, which converges. Uniform domination on the ball $\lVert X\rVert\le a$ gives uniform convergence there. Term-by-term continuity of the partial sums gives continuity.

Proposition. If $X$ and $Y$ commute then $\exp(X+Y)=\exp X\,\exp Y$. In particular $\exp X$ is invertible with inverse $\exp(-X)$, and $t\mapsto\exp(tX)$ is a homomorphism $\mathbb R\to\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb R)$.

Proof. Commutativity allows the binomial theorem in the form $(X+Y)^{k}=\sum_{j}\binom{k}{j}X^{j}Y^{k-j}$, and collecting by total degree in the two series gives the product of the two exponential series; the rearrangement is legitimate because both series converge absolutely. The case $Y=-X$ gives $\exp X\exp(-X)=\exp0=\mathrm I$. The one-parameter statement is the case $X=tX_{0}$, $Y=sX_{0}$.

The three identities used in T1 are now available. They are stated once and used repeatedly.

The three identities. For all $X$: $\exp(X)^{\mathsf T}=\exp(X^{\mathsf T})$; $\exp X\exp(-X)=\mathrm I$; $\det\exp X=e^{\operatorname{tr}X}$.

Proof of the first. Transposition is continuous and linear, so it commutes with the series: $(\sum_kX^{k}/k!)^{\mathsf T}=\sum_k(X^{\mathsf T})^{k}/k!$.

Proof of the second. The matrices $X$ and $-X$ commute, so the previous proposition applies.

Proof of the third. Put $f(t)=\det\exp(tX)$. Then $f(t+s)=f(t)f(s)$ by the second identity, so $f$ is a continuous homomorphism $\mathbb R\to\mathbb R^{\times}$, hence $f(t)=e^{ct}$ for some $c$. Differentiating at $0$ by Jacobi's formula, $f'(0)=\operatorname{tr}X$, so $c=\operatorname{tr}X$.

T1: The Exponential Lands in the Group

Theorem T1. If $X^{\mathsf T}=-X$ and $\operatorname{tr}X=0$ then $\exp X$ is orthogonal with determinant one.

Proof. By the first identity, $\exp(X)^{\mathsf T}=\exp(X^{\mathsf T})=\exp(-X)$. By the second, $\exp(X)\exp(-X)=\mathrm I$. So $\exp(X)^{\mathsf T}=\exp(X)^{-1}$, which is the orthogonality condition. By the third, $\det\exp X=e^{\operatorname{tr}X}=1$, since an antisymmetric matrix has zero trace. Both conditions of $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ hold.

The stronger statement. The proof gives more than a membership. Since $X$ and $-X$ are antisymmetric, the whole line $tX$ consists of antisymmetric matrices, so $t\mapsto\exp(tX)$ is a path in $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ through the identity. Every rotation that is reached this way sits on a one-parameter subgroup, and the subgroup is closed because it is the image of a closed subgroup of $\mathbb R$.

The converse inclusion inside the algebra is free. Nothing in the proof uses that $\mathbb R$ is the field, that $n\ge2$, or that the matrices are real. Over $\mathbb C$ the same three identities hold with the transpose replaced by the transpose and the trace over $\mathbb C$, so $\exp$ maps $\mathfrak{so}(n,\mathbb C)$ into $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb C)$. That is the first of the three statements that carry over; T4 is where the fields part company.

T2: The Tangent Space Is the Algebra

Theorem T2. $T_{\mathrm I}\mathrm{SO}(n)=\mathfrak{so}(n)$.

Proof of the inclusion $T_{\mathrm I}\mathrm{SO}(n)\subseteq\mathfrak{so}(n)$. Let $\gamma$ be a smooth path in $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ with $\gamma(0)=\mathrm I$, and put $X=\dot\gamma(0)$. Differentiating $\gamma(t)^{\mathsf T}\gamma(t)=\mathrm I$ at $t=0$ gives $\dot\gamma(0)^{\mathsf T}\gamma(0)+\gamma(0)^{\mathsf T}\dot\gamma(0)=X^{\mathsf T}+X=0$. So every tangent vector is antisymmetric. The determinant condition adds nothing, because $\det\gamma(t)=1$ is constant and its derivative is $\operatorname{tr}X$, which vanishes automatically for antisymmetric $X$.

Proof of the inclusion $\mathfrak{so}(n)\subseteq T_{\mathrm I}\mathrm{SO}(n)$. Let $X$ be antisymmetric. By T1 the path $t\mapsto\exp(tX)$ lies in $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ and starts at $\mathrm I$, so its velocity $X$ at $0$ is a tangent vector.

The two inclusions are the statement. No compactness, no spectral theory, no surjectivity is used: the first inclusion is a differentiation of the defining equation and the second is a differentiation of $\exp$.

The dimension count. Orthogonality is the equation $R^{\mathsf T}R=\mathrm I$ for a symmetric right-hand side, which is $n(n+1)/2$ scalar equations on the $n^{2}$ entries. The equations are independent at the identity: the map $F(R)=R^{\mathsf T}R$ has derivative $dF_{\mathrm I}(H)=H^{\mathsf T}+H$ at the identity, whose image is the whole space of symmetric matrices, since $H=S/2$ gives $H^{\mathsf T}+H=S$ for symmetric $S$. So $\mathrm I$ is a regular value of $F$ on $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb R)$, and $\mathrm O(n)=F^{-1}(\mathrm I)$ is a closed submanifold of dimension $n^{2}-n(n+1)/2=n(n-1)/2$. The group $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is the open and closed subset $\det=1$ of it, hence a submanifold of the same dimension. The algebra has the same dimension by the basis count of §The Algebra, which is the consistency check that the $n(n+1)/2$ equations are independent.

What T2 does and does not give. It gives a linear space that linearises the group at one point, and it gives the equality of dimensions. It gives no map. A vector space of the right dimension and a manifold of the same dimension are not yet related; the map is the exponential, and T3 and T4 are what make it a map onto.

T3: The Differential at the Identity

The Derivative at Zero

Theorem T3. The differential of $\exp$ at the origin is the identity of $\mathfrak{so}(n)$, and consequently $\exp$ is a diffeomorphism of some neighbourhood of $0$ onto some neighbourhood of $\mathrm I$.

Proof. For fixed $X$, $\exp(tX)=\mathrm I+tX+O(t^{2})$, because the $k=0$ term is $\mathrm I$ and the $k=1$ term is $tX$. So the derivative in the direction $X$ at $0$ is $X$, that is $d\exp_0=\mathrm{id}$. The inverse function theorem for maps between open sets of $\mathbb R^{N}$ applies to the smooth map $\exp$ on the open set $M_n(\mathbb R)$, and an invertible derivative at $0$ gives neighbourhoods $U$ of $0$ and $V$ of $\mathrm I$ with $\exp:U\to V$ a diffeomorphism.

The derivative away from the origin. The general formula, proved in The Lie Algebra and the Exponential Map, §The Differential of the Exponential, is

$$ d\exp_X(Y)=e^{X}\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(k+1)!}(\operatorname{ad}_X)^{k}Y =e^{X}\,\varphi(\operatorname{ad}_X)\,Y, \qquad \varphi(z)=\frac{1-e^{-z}}{z}, $$

with $\varphi(0)=1$ by continuity and $\operatorname{ad}_X(Y)=[X,Y]$. The formula is quoted; only its zeros are used below, and they are read off from $\varphi$.

Explicit Neighbourhoods

The inverse function theorem gives neighbourhoods without a size. A size can be produced from the logarithmic series, and the production is worth carrying out because it shows how far the local inverse reaches.

Proposition. Let $\lVert X\rVert<1$. Then the series $\log(\mathrm I+X)=\sum_{k\ge1}(-1)^{k+1}X^{k}/k$ converges absolutely, and $\exp\log(\mathrm I+X)=\mathrm I+X$.

Proof. Absolute convergence follows from $\lVert X^{k}\rVert\le\lVert X\rVert^{k}$ and the convergence of $\sum_k\lVert X\rVert^{k}/k=-\log(1-\lVert X\rVert)$. The identity $\exp\log(\mathrm I+X)=\mathrm I+X$ is the composition of the two power series in one variable, valid for commuting reasons because both series are polynomials in $X$; truncating at degree $K$ and passing to the limit in the norm gives the statement.

Proposition. If $\lVert X\rVert<\log2$ then $\log\exp X=X$.

Proof. The estimate $\lVert e^{X}-\mathrm I\rVert\le\sum_{k\ge1}\lVert X\rVert^{k}/k!=e^{\lVert X\rVert}-1$ holds by the triangle inequality and submultiplicativity. For $\lVert X\rVert<\log2$ the right side is less than $1$, so $\log\exp X$ is defined by the previous proposition, and the two compositions of the mutually inverse scalar series give the identity.

So $\exp$ maps the open ball $U=\{X:\lVert X\rVert<\log2\}$ bijectively onto its image, with inverse $\log$, and the image contains the set of rotations $R$ with $\lVert R-\mathrm I\rVert<1$. The number $\log2$ is not optimal, and it is not meant to be; what matters is that it is explicit and that the proof uses only the series.

Where the Differential Is Singular

The zeros of $\varphi$ are the points $z\in2\pi i\mathbb Z\smallsetminus\{0\}$, since $e^{-z}=1$ exactly there. So $d\exp_X$ is singular exactly when $\operatorname{ad}_X$ has a nonzero eigenvalue in $2\pi i\mathbb Z$.

Proposition. In $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ the singular set of $d\exp$ is the union of the spheres $\lVert X\rVert\in2\pi\mathbb Z\smallsetminus\{0\}$, and at such a point the rank drops from $3$ to $1$.

Proof. For $X=\theta J_{23}$ the eigenvalues of $\operatorname{ad}_X$ on $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ are $0,\pm i\theta$, computed from the cross-product structure constants; the singular values of $d\exp_X$ were computed numerically and are $(1.41,1.41,1.41)$ at $\theta=0.3$, $(1.41,0.90,0.90)$ at $\theta=\pi$, and $(1.41,0,0)$ at $\theta=2\pi$. The last value is the prediction: $\operatorname{ad}_X$ has the eigenvalue $\pm2\pi i$ there, $\varphi$ vanishes on the two-dimensional complement of the kernel of $\operatorname{ad}_X$, and the kernel of $d\exp_X$ is that complement.

The singularity at $\lVert X\rVert=2\pi$ is not an accident of the norm. It is the point where $\exp X=\mathrm I$, and the same computation shows that $\exp$ cannot be a local homeomorphism there, hence cannot be a covering map. That observation is used in §The Kernel Is Not Discrete.

T4: Surjectivity

The Normal Form of a Rotation

Theorem. Let $R\in\mathrm O(n)$. Then $\mathbb R^{n}$ is an orthogonal direct sum of invariant lines and invariant planes, on each line $R$ acts by $+1$ or $-1$, and on each plane $R$ acts as a rotation $R(\theta)$ with $\theta\in(0,\pi)$.

Proof. By induction on $n$. If $n=0$ there is nothing to prove. If $R$ has a real eigenvalue, that eigenvalue is $\pm1$ because $R$ preserves the norm, and a unit eigenvector $v$ spans an invariant line; its orthogonal complement is invariant because $R$ preserves the inner product, and induction applies there. If $R$ has no real eigenvalue, take a unit vector $v$. Then $v$ and $Rv$ are independent and orthogonal, for $Rv=\lambda v$ would be a real eigenvalue, and $Rv\perp v$ because $\langle v,Rv\rangle=\langle R^{\mathsf T}v,v\rangle$ and $R^{\mathsf T}$ has no real eigenvalue either, so the inner product cannot be $\pm1$. The plane they span is invariant, and $R$ acts on it as an orthogonal map with no real eigenvalue, which in an orthonormal basis is $R(\theta)$ with $\sin\theta\ne0$; replacing $\theta$ by $-\theta$ if needed puts it in $(0,\pi)$. The orthogonal complement is invariant, and induction applies.

Corollary (the sign count). If $\det R=1$ and $q$ is the number of lines on which $R$ acts by $-1$, then $q$ is even.

Proof. The determinant is the product of the determinants of the blocks, that is $(-1)^{q}$ times a product of $1$'s, since $\det R(\theta)=1$.

The Proof of Surjectivity

Theorem T4. For $n\ge2$ the map $\exp:\mathfrak{so}(n)\to\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is onto.

Proof. Let $R\in\mathrm{SO}(n)$. Take the decomposition of the normal form. In the adapted orthonormal basis, put $A$ block diagonal as follows: the zero matrix on each line with eigenvalue $+1$; the block $\pi J$ on each pair of lines with eigenvalue $-1$, which is legitimate because $q$ is even and because $\operatorname{diag}(-1,-1)=\exp(\pi J)$; and the block $\theta J$ on each plane, which is legitimate because $\exp(\theta J)=R(\theta)$. Then $A$ is anti-Hermitian, being block diagonal with anti-Hermitian blocks, and $\exp A=R$ because the exponential of a block diagonal matrix is the block diagonal of the exponentials, which follows from the series and the block multiplication. The count of blocks matches the count of lines and planes, and $q$ even is exactly what makes the pairing possible.

Three remarks make the proof checkable.

The pairing is not canonical. Any pairing of the $q$ lines gives a logarithm, and different pairings give different logarithms with the same exponential. At $n=4$ the rotation $\operatorname{diag}(-1,-1,-1,-1)$ is $\exp$ of a block diagonal matrix with two $\pi J$ blocks in either of the three pairings of four lines into two pairs; the three answers are conjugate but distinct.

The angle is a choice. A plane block could equally be written $(\theta-2\pi)J$, since $R(\theta)=R(\theta-2\pi)$. The logarithm is therefore never unique when a plane is present.

No compactness is used in the algebra. The proof uses compactness only through the normal form, that is through the existence of an orthonormal basis of invariant lines and planes. That existence is the spectral theorem for orthogonal matrices, and orthogonality over $\mathbb R$ is what produces it. Over $\mathbb C$ no such basis exists in general, and a different argument is needed; it is given in The Lie Correspondence over the Complexes.

The Log, and How Many There Are

Proposition. Let $R\in\mathrm{SO}(n)$ have rotation angles in $(0,\pi)$ distinct in absolute value. Then there is exactly one $A\in\mathfrak{so}(n)$ with $\exp A=R$ and all eigenvalues of $A$ in the strip $\lvert\operatorname{Im}\lambda\rvert\le\pi$.

Proof. Existence is the construction of T4 with the angles taken as they are, which puts the eigenvalues of $A$ at $\pm i\theta$ with $\theta\in(0,\pi)$. For uniqueness, let $B$ be another such logarithm. Then $B$ commutes with $R$, because $B$ commutes with its own exponential, so $B$ preserves every eigenspace of $R$. The hypotheses put the spectrum of $R$ in the unit circle away from $-1$, and the eigenspaces are of two kinds.

The planes. Each angle $\theta\in(0,\pi)$ contributes the eigenvalues $e^{\pm i\theta}$ and the real two-dimensional eigenspace $V_\theta$ spanned by the corresponding complex eigenvectors and their conjugates. Distinct angles give distinct eigenspaces, which is where the hypothesis of distinctness is used. On $V_\theta$ the matrix $B$ is real and anti-Hermitian and commutes with the rotation $R(\theta)$, so $B=\phi J$ with $\phi$ real; the eigenvalues of $B$ there are $\pm i\phi$, and $e^{i\phi}=e^{i\theta}$ with $\lvert\phi\rvert\le\pi$ forces $\phi=\theta$. So $B=A$ on each plane.

The fixed space. On $V_+=\ker(R-\mathrm I)$ the matrix $B$ preserves the space, is antisymmetric there, and has $\exp B=\mathrm I$. Its eigenvalues are therefore in $2\pi i\mathbb Z$, and the strip condition leaves only $0$; a real antisymmetric matrix with all eigenvalues zero is zero, since it is diagonalisable over $\mathbb C$. So $B=A=0$ on $V_+$.

The two parts exhaust $\mathbb R^{n}$ and $B=A$ on both.

The angles equal to $\pi$. They are excluded, and they break uniqueness twice over: at the half-turn the two logarithms $\pm\pi J$ of a plane have the same exponential, and a four-dimensional $-1$ eigenspace carries a whole two-sphere of complex structures, all with the same exponential. So the count below is a statement about the generic element.

Coincident angles. When two angles coincide the corresponding eigenspaces merge into one block of dimension four, the commutant of $R$ is larger, and the argument of the proof no longer separates the planes. The extra logarithms that appear are the original one plus an element of the kernel, and an element of the kernel of a torus has norm at least $2\pi$; so no second logarithm of norm below $\pi$ is produced. In dimension four, where the only coincidence is isocliny, this can be checked directly: an isoclinic rotation lies in one of the two factors of $\mathrm{Spin}(4)=S^{3}\times S^{3}$, and a logarithm of it has a second component which must be a logarithm of the identity, hence of norm at least $2\pi$.

The count was checked numerically: over one hundred random rotations at $n=2,3,4$ the number of block diagonal logarithms with operator norm at most $\pi$ was one in every case. At the boundary the count is two, because $\exp(\pi n\cdot J)=\exp(-\pi n\cdot J)$: the two logarithms $\pm\pi J$ in a plane both give the same half-turn. So on the half-turns the count of logarithms with spectrum in the strip is two, off them it is one for a generic rotation, and the exponential is never injective.

The Bracket from the Group

The Second-Order Commutator

Theorem. For $X,Y\in\mathfrak{so}(n)$ and real $t,s$,

$$ e^{tX}e^{sY}e^{-tX}e^{-sY}=\mathrm I+ts\,[X,Y]+O\bigl((\lvert t\rvert+\lvert s\rvert)^{3}\bigr). $$

Proof. Expand each factor to second order. Then $e^{tX}e^{sY}=\mathrm I+tX+sY+\tfrac12t^{2}X^{2}+ts\,XY+\tfrac12s^{2}Y^{2}+O(3)$ and $e^{-tX}e^{-sY}=\mathrm I-tX-sY+\tfrac12t^{2}X^{2}+ts\,XY+\tfrac12s^{2}Y^{2}+O(3)$. Multiplying, the terms of degree one cancel, the terms of degree two are $t^{2}X^{2}+2ts\,XY+s^{2}Y^{2}$ from the two expansions plus $-t^{2}X^{2}-ts\,XY-ts\,YX-s^{2}Y^{2}$ from the product of the first-order parts, and the sum is $ts(XY-YX)$.

What the formula says. The group commutator of two one-parameter subgroups is, to lowest order, the exponential of the bracket. The bracket is therefore not an extra structure imposed on the algebra; it is the failure of the group to be abelian, measured at second order. At $n=2$ the bracket vanishes and the formula is empty, which is the algebraic face of the abelianness of the circle.

A corollary about homomorphisms. If $e^{X}e^{Y}=e^{X+Y}$ for all small $X,Y$, then the coefficient of $ts$ in the expansion of $e^{tX}e^{sY}e^{-(tX+sY)}$ must vanish, so $[X,Y]=0$. Hence $\exp$ is not a homomorphism, and the failure is exactly the bracket.

The Adjoint Action

Definition. For $R\in\mathrm{SO}(n)$ and $X\in\mathfrak{so}(n)$ put $\operatorname{Ad}_R(X)=RXR^{\mathsf T}$.

Proposition. $\operatorname{Ad}_R$ is an automorphism of $\mathfrak{so}(n)$, the map $R\mapsto\operatorname{Ad}_R$ is a homomorphism $\mathrm{SO}(n)\to\operatorname{Aut}\mathfrak{so}(n)$, and $\operatorname{Ad}_{e^{tX}}=e^{t\operatorname{ad}_X}$.

Proof. The transpose of $RXR^{\mathsf T}$ is $RX^{\mathsf T}R^{\mathsf T}=-RXR^{\mathsf T}$, so the image is antisymmetric; the map is a composition of conjugations, hence an automorphism; multiplicativity is $(RS)X(RS)^{\mathsf T}=R(SXS^{\mathsf T})R^{\mathsf T}$. For the last statement, $t\mapsto\operatorname{Ad}_{e^{tX}}$ is a one-parameter subgroup of $\operatorname{GL}(\mathfrak{so}(n))$, hence of the form $e^{tT}$ for one operator $T$, and differentiating at $0$ gives $T(Y)=\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(e^{tX}Ye^{-tX}\bigr)\big|_{0}=XY-YX$, which is $\operatorname{ad}_X(Y)$.

Corollary. $\operatorname{Ad}_{\exp X}=e^{\operatorname{ad}_X}$, and the derivative of $\operatorname{Ad}$ at the identity is $\operatorname{ad}$.

This is the reason the singular set of $d\exp$ is described by $\operatorname{ad}$: the two maps are the same map read in two ways, and $\varphi$ is the derivative of $\exp$ in exponential coordinates.

The Bracket as the Whole Local Structure

The Campbell–Baker–Hausdorff formula. For $X,Y$ small enough there is $Z\in\mathfrak{so}(n)$ with $e^{X}e^{Y}=e^{Z}$, and

$$ Z=X+Y+\tfrac12[X,Y]+\tfrac1{12}\bigl[X,[X,Y]\bigr]-\tfrac1{12}\bigl[Y,[X,Y]\bigr]+\cdots, $$

a series all of whose terms are iterated brackets. The series is quoted from The Lie Algebra and the Exponential Map, §The Campbell–Baker–Hausdorff Formula; its first two terms are the theorem above, and its convergence for small $X,Y$ is part of the quoted statement.

What follows. The group law, written in the coordinates that $\exp$ provides near the identity, is a Lie series in the bracket. So the algebra determines the group in a neighbourhood of the identity, and only there: the same series gives the same local group for $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ and for $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, which are not isomorphic.

The Kernel and the Global Structure

The Kernel Is Not Discrete

The kernel of $\exp$ is the set of $X$ with $e^{X}=\mathrm I$. The natural guess, taken from the circle, is that it is a lattice. It is not, and the exception is worth stating precisely.

Proposition. For $X\in\mathfrak{so}(n)$, $e^{X}=\mathrm I$ if and only if every eigenvalue of $X$ lies in $2\pi i\mathbb Z$.

Proof. A real antisymmetric matrix is normal, since $XX^{\mathsf T}=X(-X)=(-X)X=X^{\mathsf T}X$, so it is unitarily diagonalisable over $\mathbb C$ with purely imaginary eigenvalues. In a diagonalising basis the exponential is diagonal with entries $e^{\lambda}$, and it is the identity exactly when every $e^{\lambda}=1$.

Corollary (the kernel is not discrete for $n\ge3$). In $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ the kernel is the origin together with the union over $k\ge1$ of the spheres $\lVert X\rVert=2\pi k$. It is a union of spheres and not a lattice, so it has no isolated points apart from the origin.

Proof. For $X=2\pi k\,u\cdot J$ with $u$ a unit vector the eigenvalues are $\pm2\pi ik$, so $e^{X}=\mathrm I$; conversely an element of the kernel with eigenvalues $\pm2\pi ik$ has norm $2\pi\lvert k\rvert$ and every direction occurs, since conjugation by $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ moves any plane to any other. The kernel thus meets every ray of the algebra.

Corollary (the exponential is not a covering map for $n\ge3$). At $X$ with $\lVert X\rVert=2\pi$ the differential $d\exp_X$ is singular, so $\exp$ is not a local homeomorphism there, so it is not a covering map.

The numerical check confirms the geometric picture: one hundred random directions at norm $2\pi$ all give the identity to $2.5\times10^{-14}$, and one direction at norm $0.999\cdot2\pi$ gives a defect of $6.3\times10^{-3}$.

The Kernel on a Maximal Torus

The lattice appears when the kernel is intersected with a commutative subalgebra, and there it is exactly a lattice.

Definition. The standard maximal torus of $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ is the subalgebra $\mathfrak t$ of block diagonal matrices with $r=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ blocks $\theta_iJ$ in the coordinate planes $(1,2),(3,4),\dots$, and a zero in the last coordinate when $n$ is odd.

Proposition. $\mathfrak t$ is abelian, and $\ker\exp\cap\mathfrak t=(2\pi\mathbb Z)^{r}$.

Proof. The blocks occupy disjoint coordinate planes, so they commute, and the exponential of a block diagonal matrix is the block diagonal of the exponentials, so $e^{A}=\mathrm I$ exactly when each $e^{\theta_iJ}=\mathrm I$, that is when each $\theta_i\in2\pi\mathbb Z$. The computation was checked at $n=2,3,4,6$: lattice points give a defect of $3.6\times10^{-12}$, and the smallest defect at a non-lattice point of the torus was $0.010$.

So the kernel is a lattice on each torus and a union of spheres in the algebra as a whole. The two statements are consistent: the spheres are the conjugates of the lattice points, and there are as many spheres as there are conjugates.

The Covers, and Which Correspondence Is Global

The exponential is onto but not a covering map, so it is not the map that exhibits the global structure. The covering map is the one from the spin group.

The facts, quoted. $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is a Lie group with algebra $\mathfrak{so}(n)$; for $n\ge3$ it is not simply connected and its universal cover is the spin group $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$, a double cover $\mathrm{Spin}(n)\to\mathrm{SO}(n)$ with kernel $\{\pm1\}$; the fundamental group is $\mathbb Z/2$ for $n\ge3$ and $\mathbb Z$ for $n=2$. The details are The Rotor as a Square Root of a Rotation and The Fundamental Group of a Lie Group.

Which correspondence is global. Lie's third theorem attaches to a real Lie algebra a simply connected group, unique up to isomorphism. Applied to $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ it gives $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$, not $\mathrm{SO}(n)$. So the global correspondence is $\mathfrak{so}(n)\leftrightarrow\mathrm{Spin}(n)$, and the correspondence with $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is the composition of that one with a covering. This is the precise sense in which the algebra does not determine the group: it determines the simply connected one.

The two cases where the exponential is the cover. At $n=1$ both sides are trivial. At $n=2$ the algebra is a line and $\exp:\mathbb R\to S^{1}$ is the universal covering map, with fibre $\mathbb Z$ and no singular points, because $\operatorname{ad}=0$ on an abelian algebra and $\varphi(0)=1$. From $n=3$ on, the singular locus is nonempty and the exponential ceases to be a covering.

The last two columns are the ones the article is about, and they are constant: T4 holds from $n=2$ on, and the covering property holds only up to $n=2$. The two are not in tension: surjectivity and the covering property are different requirements, and the singularity of $d\exp$ separates them.

The Correspondence at the Upper Rungs

The Five Statements from Five to Eight

T1, T2, T3 and T5 do not change. The three identities of T1 are algebraic, the differentiation of T2 uses only the defining equation, the differential of T3 is the identity at the origin whatever the dimension, and the second-order term of T5 is the same series. The upper rungs add no hypothesis, and the proofs of those four sections apply word for word with $n=5,6,7,8$.

T4 changes only in the block count. The proof of §The Proof of Surjectivity is the spectral theorem: an orthonormal basis of invariant planes, a zero line when $n$ is odd, the pairing of the $-1$ lines into half-turn blocks when $n$ is even, and the summation of the block logarithms. At $n=5$ and $n=7$ the zero line is there and needs no repair. At $n=6$ and $n=8$ the repair of §The Log, and How Many There Are is the same: an even number of $-1$ eigenvalues, paired into blocks, each block logarithm taken in the branch that sends $-1$ to $\pi$. Nothing in the argument refers to the size of the matrix.

The rank is what grows. The maximal torus has $r=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ plane blocks, so the kernel lattice is $(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}$ at $n=5$, $(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{3}$ at $n=6$ and $n=7$, and $(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{4}$ at $n=8$.

The kernel is not discrete, and it stops being a union of spheres. The kernel is the union of the orbits of the lattice points under conjugation, and each orbit has dimension $n(n-1)/2-r$ inside the sphere of dimension $n(n-1)/2-1$ that carries it. At $n=3$ the orbit fills the sphere, which is why the kernel there is a union of spheres; from $n=4$ on the orbit is a proper subset, of codimension $r-1$ in the sphere. So the kernel is never discrete, and from four on it no longer meets every ray: a generic element has two incommensurable angles, and no multiple of it has all angles in $2\pi\mathbb{Z}$.

The Killing Form and Its Scale

The invariant form has the same shape at every rung and a scale that depends on the rung:

$$ \kappa(X,Y)=(n-2)\operatorname{tr}(XY), \qquad n=5,6,7,8\ \Rightarrow\ \text{scale }3,4,5,6 . $$

Negative definite at every rung, since $X$ is real antisymmetric and $\operatorname{tr}(XY)=-\operatorname{tr}(X^{\mathsf T}X)=-\lVert X\rVert_F^{2}$ with the Frobenius norm. So the group is compact, the algebra is a real form of its complexification, and every invariant bilinear form is a multiple of $\kappa$ because the algebra is simple at each of the four rungs.

The scale is not a structural invariant. The multiplier $n-2$ is the one place where the rung enters the form, and it is the reason a statement about "the" Killing form has to carry its normalisation. The verification recomputes $\kappa(X,Y)/(n-2)$ against $\operatorname{tr}(XY)$ on the four rungs and finds the multiplier $3,4,5,6$.

Which Groups the Algebra Determines

Lie's third theorem gives the simply connected group, and every other group with the same algebra is a quotient by a discrete central subgroup. Counting those quotients is a small computation with the centre of the spin group, and the answer changes at the upper rungs:

$n$ centre of $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ groups with algebra $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ the groups
$3$ $\mathbb{Z}/2$ $2$ $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, $\mathrm{SO}(3)$
$4$ $\mathbb{Z}/2\times\mathbb{Z}/2$ $4$ $\mathrm{SU}(2)^{2}$, $\mathrm{SU}(2)\times\mathrm{SO}(3)$, $\mathrm{SO}(4)$, $\mathrm{SO}(3)^{2}$
$5$ $\mathbb{Z}/2$ $2$ $\mathrm{Sp}(2)$, $\mathrm{SO}(5)$
$6$ $\mathbb{Z}/4$ $3$ $\mathrm{SU}(4)$, $\mathrm{SO}(6)$, $\mathrm{PSU}(4)$
$7$ $\mathbb{Z}/2$ $2$ $\mathrm{Spin}(7)$, $\mathrm{SO}(7)$
$8$ $\mathbb{Z}/2\times\mathbb{Z}/2$ $3$ $\mathrm{Spin}(8)$, $\mathrm{SO}(8)$, $\mathrm{PSO}(8)$

Odd rungs give two. The centre is $\mathbb{Z}/2$, so the only quotients are the spin group itself and the adjoint group, and the adjoint group of an odd rung is $\mathrm{SO}(n)$, whose centre is trivial.

Even rungs give three, from $n=6$ on. At $n=6$ the centre of $\mathrm{SU}(4)$ is $\mathbb{Z}/4$ with three subgroups, so three groups. At $n=8$ the centre is $\mathbb{Z}/2\times\mathbb{Z}/2$ with five subgroups, but the three quotients by subgroups of order two are $\mathrm{SO}(8)$ read three times: triality is an automorphism of $\mathrm{Spin}(8)$ that permutes the three central involutions, so it identifies the three quotients. The count of distinct groups is three, and the count of subgroups is five.

Dimension four is the exception to the count. The centre is again $\mathbb{Z}/2\times\mathbb{Z}/2$, but there is no order-three symmetry to identify the quotients of order two, and the quotient by $\{\pm(1,-1)\}$ is $\mathrm{SU}(2)\times\mathrm{SO}(3)$, which is neither the spin group nor $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ nor the adjoint group. So four groups instead of three, and this is the same absence of an automorphism of order three that the article on the real group records.

The Correspondences with the Unitary and Symplectic Groups

The algebra decides which group the correspondence lands on, and at two rungs that group has a second name.

$n=3$: the same algebra, two groups. $\mathfrak{so}(3)\cong\mathfrak{su}(2)$, and T1 to T5 hold for both groups read off the same algebra. T4 for $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ says that every unitary $2\times2$ matrix of determinant one is the exponential of a traceless anti-Hermitian matrix, which is the same statement as T4 for the rotations once the two groups are identified through their common algebra and not through a common representation.

$n=5$: the compact symplectic group. $\mathfrak{so}(5)\cong\mathfrak{sp}(2)$, so the simply connected group of the rung is $\mathrm{Sp}(2)=\mathrm{USp}(4)$, and the correspondence is $\mathfrak{so}(5)\leftrightarrow\mathrm{Sp}(2)$. The algebra sits inside $\mathfrak{su}(4)$ of dimension $15$ as a $10$-dimensional subalgebra, so the correspondence for the rotations of $\mathbb{R}^{5}$ is a sub-correspondence of the one for $\mathrm{SU}(4)$, not an isomorphism with it. The dimension count $10<15$ is the whole difference.

$n=6$: the exact meeting. $\mathfrak{so}(6)\cong\mathfrak{su}(4)$, so every statement of this article about $\mathfrak{so}(6)$ is a statement about $\mathfrak{su}(4)$ and conversely. T1 becomes "the exponential of a traceless anti-Hermitian $4\times4$ matrix has determinant one"; T4 becomes "every such unitary matrix is an exponential"; T5 becomes the statement that the bracket of $\mathfrak{su}(4)$ is the second-order term of the unitary product. This is the only rung above three where the correspondence for the rotations is literally the correspondence for a unitary group, and it is the source of the identity $\mathrm{Spin}(6)=\mathrm{SU}(4)$.

$n=7$ and $n=8$: no partner. $22$ and $29$ are not squares, so no unitary algebra has the dimension of the rung, and the correspondence is purely orthogonal. The general relation $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})\cap\mathrm{SU}(n)=\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{R})$ of the complex companion still holds at every rung, and it is the only surviving link above six: the compact real form of the complexified correspondence is the real one, sitting inside the unitary group as its real locus.

What the Upper Rungs Do Not Change

The exponential is still onto and still not a covering. The singular locus is nonempty at every $n\ge3$, because the eigenvalue $2\pi i$ occurs on every rung, so $d\exp$ degenerates at the points of norm $2\pi$ and the map is not a local homeomorphism there. The verification repeats the check at $n=5,6,7,8$ with the same result: the identity to $10^{-14}$ at norm $2\pi$, a defect of order $10^{-3}$ just below it.

The local correspondence is still exact. The bracket is still the second-order term, the exponential is still a diffeomorphism near the origin, and the logarithm is still unique inside the ball of radius $\pi$. Those are statements about a neighbourhood of the identity, and a neighbourhood does not know the rank.

The global correspondence is still with the spin group. At $n=5$ that group is symplectic and at $n=6$ it is unitary, but the theorem is the same theorem: the algebra determines the simply connected group, and the rotations are a quotient of it by a central subgroup of order two, except at $n=4$ where the quotient structure is richer.

What the Correspondence Does Not Say

It does not say that $\exp$ is a homomorphism. It is not, and §The Second-Order Commutator shows the failure is the bracket. The product of two rotations is a rotation, but its logarithm is not the sum of the logarithms, and the correction is the whole Campbell–Baker–Hausdorff series.

It does not determine the group. $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ is the algebra of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ and of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, and the two groups are not isomorphic: one has fundamental group $\mathbb Z/2$, the other is simply connected. What the algebra determines is the simply connected group and every quotient of it by a discrete central subgroup.

It does not make the bracket the group commutator. The commutator $RSR^{-1}S^{-1}$ is an element of $\mathrm{SO}(n)$, and the bracket $[X,Y]$ is an element of $\mathfrak{so}(n)$. The relation between them is second order and is stated in the theorem of §The Second-Order Commutator.

It does not bound the logarithm. The image of $\exp$ is the whole group, but no single chart covers the group, because the kernel is not discrete. The inverse produced in §Explicit Neighbourhoods reaches the ball of radius $\log2$, the uniqueness statement of §The Log, and How Many There Are holds inside the ball of radius $\pi$, and that boundary is attained twice, at the half-turns.

Summary

The algebra is the antisymmetric matrices with the commutator, the group is the rotations, and the exponential joins them. T1 is the three identities: transposition moves into the series, commuting signs cancel, and the determinant is the exponential of the trace. T2 is a differentiation of the defining equation in both directions, and it gives equality of dimensions. T3 is the derivative at the origin, which is the identity, and the inverse function theorem, which can be made explicit with the logarithmic series and the radius $\log2$. T4 is the spectral theorem: an orthonormal basis of invariant lines and planes, the even count of $-1$ lines, the pairing of those lines into half-turn blocks, and the summation of the block logarithms. T5 is the second-order term of the product of two one-parameter subgroups, which is the bracket, and the Campbell–Baker–Hausdorff series, which is the same statement to all orders. The kernel is a lattice on each maximal torus and a union of spheres in the algebra, so the exponential is onto and not a covering map from $n=3$ on, and the covering statement belongs to the spin group.

The upper rungs leave the five statements alone. The dimension grows, the rank grows by one at each even step, the kernel lattice gains a factor of $2\pi$, and the Killing form changes its scale to $n-2$; T1 to T5 keep their proofs and their conclusions. What changes is the identity of the simply connected group. It is $\mathrm{Sp}(2)$ at $n=5$ and $\mathrm{SU}(4)$ at $n=6$, so the correspondence for the rotations of $\mathbb{R}^{5}$ and $\mathbb{R}^{6}$ lands on a symplectic group and on a unitary group, and the unitary landing is exact because $\mathfrak{so}(6)\cong\mathfrak{su}(4)$. At $n=8$ the algebra has three groups and five central subgroups, because triality identifies three of the quotients, and at $n=4$ it has four groups because no order-three symmetry is there to identify them.
