$\mathcal N=2$ Super Yang–Mills: The Prepotential and the Seiberg–Witten Solution

Introduction

The question. What is the exact low-energy content of four-dimensional $\mathcal N=2$ super Yang–Mills, and what precisely does the Seiberg–Witten solution solve? The theory is the first extended case that is genuinely distinct: $\mathcal N=3$ collapses to $\mathcal N=4$, but $\mathcal N=2$ has its own moduli space, its own dynamics, and an exact solution of its Coulomb branch. This note writes the multiplet, the Lagrangian determined by a single holomorphic function, the special Kähler geometry, the curve, the periods, and the duality action, and then says which of those statements is exact and which is a convention.

The answer in one line.

$$ \boxed{\; \mathcal L_{\mathcal N=2} =\operatorname{Im}\Bigl[ \int d^{2}\theta\;\tfrac14\,\mathcal F''(\Phi)\,W^{\alpha}W_\alpha +\int d^{4}\theta\;\bar\Phi\,\mathcal F'(\Phi)\Bigr], \qquad \tau(\Phi)=\mathcal F''(\Phi),\quad K=\operatorname{Im}\bigl(\bar\Phi\,\mathcal F'(\Phi)\bigr) \;} $$

so the whole theory is a holomorphic function $\mathcal F$ of one adjoint chiral superfield, and on the Coulomb branch the exact $\mathcal F$ is fixed by the periods of a meromorphic differential on an elliptic curve.

What is claimed, and what is not. Claimed: the field content, the superspace form, the one-loop exactness of the perturbative prepotential, the curve and differential for pure $SU(2)$ (in a stated normalization), the exact BPS mass formula $M=|n_e a+n_m a_D|$, and the $SL(2,\mathbb Z)$ action on the period vector. Not claimed: an exact solution of the full short-distance theory, of the Higgs branch, or of the non-BPS spectrum. The solution is exact in the infrared, on the Coulomb branch, for the quantities just listed.

Field Content

The on-shell $\mathcal N=2$ vector multiplet, all in the adjoint of $G$:

field description $SU(2)_R$ $U(1)_R$ d.o.f.
$A_\mu$ gauge boson $\mathbf 1$ $0$ 2
$\lambda^{i}$, $i=1,2$ two Weyl gaugini $\mathbf 2$ $+1$ 4
$\phi$ one complex scalar $\mathbf 1$ $+2$ 2

The two charges shown are the classical ones. The algebra has a $U(2)_R=SU(2)_R\times U(1)_R$ automorphism, and $SU(2)_R$ is an exact symmetry, but $U(1)_R$ is anomalous in the asymptotically free theory: the mixed gauge–$R$ anomaly with $T(\text{adj})=2N_c$ acting on two gaugini of charge $+1$ breaks it to $\mathbb Z_{4N_c}$, twice the $\mathbb Z_{2N_c}$ of $\mathcal N=1$ because there are twice as many gaugini. The $U(1)_R$ survives intact only when the matter content cancels the anomaly, as at $N_f=2N_c$, where it becomes the $R$-symmetry of the superconformal algebra $SU(2,2\vert 2)$.

Bosons $2+2=4$, fermions $4$: balanced. Off shell one needs a whole $\mathcal N=2$ supermultiplet of auxiliary fields; the practical route is the $\mathcal N=1$ decomposition

$$ \mathcal N=2\ \text{vector} =\underbrace{(A_\mu,\lambda)}_{\mathcal N=1\ \text{vector}} \;\oplus\; \underbrace{(\phi,\psi)}_{\mathcal N=1\ \text{chiral}\ \Phi}, $$

with $\psi$ the second gaugino. The two gaugini are rotated into each other by the second supersymmetry; the scalar $\phi$ is the partner of the chiral multiplet's auxiliary field.

Matter. A hypermultiplet is, in $\mathcal N=1$ language, a pair of chiral multiplets $Q,\tilde Q$ (or a pseudoreal pair), carrying four real scalars and two Weyl fermions. It must sit in a real or pseudoreal representation, or in a conjugate pair, because it is CPT self-conjugate. This is a genuine restriction relative to $\mathcal N=1$, where any complex representation is allowed.

The Lagrangian and the Prepotential

Non-Abelian caveat. The superspace formula above is exact for an Abelian vector multiplet. For non-Abelian $G$ the prepotential is a gauge-invariant function of the adjoint $\Phi$ (typically through $\operatorname{Tr}\Phi^{k}$), and the formula is understood on the Coulomb branch, where $\Phi$ lies in the Cartan subalgebra and the theory reduces to $r=\operatorname{rank}G$ Abelian multiplets. This is the setting in which the Seiberg–Witten solution lives.

Pure theory. With $\mathcal F(\Phi)=\tfrac12\tau_0\Phi^{2}$,

$$ \tau=\tau_0,\qquad K=\operatorname{Im}(\tau_0)|\Phi|^{2}, $$

canonical kinetic terms and a constant coupling at tree level.

Components. With the scalar kinetic term normalized with an overall $1/g^{2}$,

$$ \mathcal L=\frac{1}{g^{2}}\operatorname{Tr}\Bigl[ -\tfrac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} +i\bar\lambda\bar\sigma^{\mu}D_\mu\lambda +i\bar\psi\bar\sigma^{\mu}D_\mu\psi +D_\mu\bar\phi\,D^{\mu}\phi -\tfrac12[\phi,\bar\phi]^{2} -i\sqrt2\,\lambda[\psi,\phi]+\text{h.c.}\Bigr]. $$

The scalar potential is a single D-term,

$$ V=\frac{1}{2g^{2}}\operatorname{Tr}\bigl([\phi,\bar\phi]^{2}\bigr), $$

which vanishes exactly where $[\phi,\bar\phi]=0$: the Coulomb branch. In the canonical normalization $V=\frac{g^{2}}{8}\operatorname{Tr}([\phi,\bar\phi]^{2})$; the two differ by $\phi\to g\phi$ and describe the same physics. The Yukawa coupling $-i\sqrt2\,\lambda[\psi,\phi]$ is fixed by $\mathcal N=2$; its sign and the factor $\sqrt2$ are convention.

Special Kähler geometry. The Kähler potential is not arbitrary:

$$ K=\operatorname{Im}\bigl(\bar\Phi\,\mathcal F'(\Phi)\bigr), \qquad g_{\Phi\bar\Phi}=\partial_\Phi\partial_{\bar\Phi}K=\operatorname{Im}\tau(\Phi). $$

This is the rigid special Kähler condition: the metric is the imaginary part of the second derivative of a holomorphic prepotential. Equivalently, the geometry is described by a period vector of a holomorphic one-form, which is exactly what the curve supplies.

The $\beta$-Function and Perturbative Exactness

For $SU(N_c)$ with $N_f$ fundamental hypermultiplets,

$$ \beta(g)=-\frac{(2N_c-N_f)\,g^{3}}{16\pi^{2}}, $$

one-loop exact: the perturbative prepotential receives only a one-loop correction,

$$ \mathcal F_{\text{pert}}=\mathcal F_{\text{tree}}+\mathcal F_{1\text{-loop}}, \qquad \mathcal F_{1\text{-loop}} =c_1\sum_{\text{roots}}(\Phi\cdot\alpha)^{2} \log\frac{(\Phi\cdot\alpha)^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}}, $$

with $c_1$ normalization-dependent. There are no higher-loop perturbative corrections; there are non-perturbative instanton corrections, and those are what the curve encodes. The two statements must be kept apart: the $\mathcal N=2$ prepotential is not one-loop exact, only its perturbative part is.

The Seiberg–Witten Curve

Pure $SU(2)$. The Coulomb branch is parameterized by $u=\langle\operatorname{Tr}\phi^{2}\rangle$. The curve is

$$ \boxed{\;y^{2}=x^{3}-u\,x^{2}+\frac{\Lambda^{4}}{4}\,x\;} $$

with the differential

$$ \lambda_{\text{SW}}=\frac{\sqrt2}{8\pi}\,\frac{2x-u}{y}\,dx . $$

Both are normalization choices. The essential, convention-independent data are the location of the singularities and the monodromy; the overall scale of $x$ and the coefficient of the $\Lambda^{4}x$ term can be rescaled, and many papers write $y^{2}=x^{3}-ux^{2}+\Lambda^{4}x$, which shifts the singular points from $u=\pm\Lambda^{2}$ to $u=\pm2\Lambda^{2}$. Any statement about “$\pm\Lambda^{2}$” must therefore be read together with the curve it came from.

Singularities. With the curve as written, the roots of the cubic degenerate at

$$ u=\pm\Lambda^{2}. $$

At $u=+\Lambda^{2}$ a magnetic monopole becomes massless; at $u=-\Lambda^{2}$ a dyon does. These are the points where the $U(1)$ description fails and a dual description is weakly coupled.

Periods. Define

$$ a=\oint_{A}\lambda_{\text{SW}}, \qquad a_D=\oint_{B}\lambda_{\text{SW}}, \qquad \tau(a)=\frac{\partial a_D}{\partial a}. $$

At weak coupling, $a\simeq\sqrt{2u}$ (in this normalization), matching the classical relation between the scalar VEV and the modulus. The prepotential is recovered from $a_D=\partial\mathcal F/\partial a$; equivalently,

$$ \mathcal F(a)=\frac{i}{2\pi}a^{2}\log\frac{a^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}} +\sum_{k\ge1}\mathcal F_k\Bigl(\frac{\Lambda}{a}\Bigr)^{4k}a^{2}. $$

The first term is the one-loop piece; the sum is the instanton expansion, with $\mathcal F_k$ determined by the curve (or, independently, by instanton counting). The numerical coefficient $i/2\pi$ depends on the normalization of $a$; here it goes with $a=\sqrt{2u}$.

Monodromy. Continuing the periods around a singularity acts on the vector $(a_D,a)$ by an element of $SL(2,\mathbb Z)$. Around infinity the monodromy is

$$ M_\infty=-T^{2}, \qquad T=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}, $$

i.e. $a_D\to a_D+a$, $a\to-a$ with the minus sign from $M_\infty=-T^{2}$. Around a singular point where a state of charge $(n_e,n_m)$ becomes massless, the monodromy is a shear in the direction of that charge vector in the $(a_D,a)$ plane. The three monodromies multiply to the identity for a contour enclosing all singularities; the group they generate is the congruence subgroup $\Gamma(2)$ of $SL(2,\mathbb Z)$, up to the $\pm1$ that acts trivially on $\tau$.

Matter. For $SU(N_c)$ with $N_f$ fundamental hypermultiplets of masses $m_i$,

$$ y^{2}=P(x)^{2}-4\Lambda^{2N_c-N_f}\prod_{i=1}^{N_f}(x-m_i), \qquad P(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{N_c}(x-\phi_j),\quad \sum_j\phi_j=0, $$

and the differential is the natural generalization of the $SU(2)$ one. At $N_f=2N_c$ the $\Lambda$-exponent vanishes and the theory is a candidate $\mathcal N=2$ superconformal field theory with exactly marginal gauge coupling.

BPS States and the Central Charge

The $\mathcal N=2$ algebra has one complex central charge $Z$ in the vector sector. For a state of electric charge $n_e$ and magnetic charge $n_m$,

$$ Z=n_e\,a+n_m\,a_D, \qquad M\ge|Z| . $$

States saturating the bound are BPS; their masses are exact functions of the moduli. The W boson (charge $(1,0)$), the monopole $(0,1)$, and the dyons $(n_e,1)$ are BPS. Across walls of marginal stability the BPS spectrum can jump, because the central charges of two states can become aligned; this is a genuine strong-coupling phenomenon, not a calculational artifact.

Why this is the real content of the solution. The effective action is a statement about a $U(1)$ gauge theory with a complicated kinetic function. The BPS mass formula is a statement about the spectrum, and it is what makes the solution predictive: it gives exact masses in a regime where perturbation theory is useless.

Duality

With $\tau=\theta/2\pi+4\pi i/g^{2}$, the solution exhibits

$$ \tau\longrightarrow\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}, \qquad \begin{pmatrix}a_D\\a\end{pmatrix} \longrightarrow \begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}a_D\\a\end{pmatrix}, \qquad ad-bc=1 . $$

The generator $\tau\to-1/\tau$ exchanges electric and magnetic descriptions. Near $u=\Lambda^{2}$ the monopole is light and the dual $U(1)$ is weakly coupled; near $u=-\Lambda^{2}$ the dyon is. The $SL(2,\mathbb Z)$ here is an exact statement about the low-energy theory on the Coulomb branch; it is the prototype for the Montonen–Olive duality of $\mathcal N=4$, where it is conjectured to hold for the full theory rather than only in the infrared.

$\mathcal N=4$ and $\mathcal N=2^{*}$ as Corners

$\mathcal N=4$ inside $\mathcal N=2$. Decompose

$$ \mathcal N=4\ \text{vector} =\mathcal N=2\ \text{vector}\;\oplus\; \mathcal N=2\ \text{hypermultiplet in the adjoint}, $$

set the hypermultiplet mass to zero, and take $\mathcal F=\tfrac12\tau_0\Phi^{2}$ with no corrections. Then $\beta=0$ identically and the curve degenerates to a constant-$\tau$ family. In this sense $\mathcal N=4$ is the conformally invariant corner of the $\mathcal N=2$ landscape.

Interpolation. Giving the adjoint hypermultiplet a mass $m$ gives the $\mathcal N=2^{*}$ theory: $m=0$ is $\mathcal N=4$, $m\to\infty$ decouples the adjoint and gives pure $\mathcal N=2$. The $\mathcal N=2^{*}$ curve is the standard testing ground for instanton counting and for the interpolation between the two limits.

Argyres–Douglas points. At special points of the Coulomb branch, mutually non-local BPS states become massless simultaneously and the low-energy theory is an interacting $\mathcal N=2$ SCFT with no vector-multiplet Lagrangian description. This is the sharpest reminder that “the Seiberg–Witten solution” describes a family of theories whose singular loci are themselves new theories.
