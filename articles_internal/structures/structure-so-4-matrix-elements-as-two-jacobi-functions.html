The $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ Matrix Elements as Two Jacobi Functions: the Factorisation Behind the de Sitter Harmonics

Introduction

The claim, in one sentence. The matrix elements of $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ are products of two Jacobi polynomials, one in the cosine of each of the two angles, the second-order equation they satisfy is the product of two first-order ladder operators, and those two ladders are the two $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ summands of $\mathfrak{so}(4)$.

What the paper under reading supplies. A computation in three steps. First, the Laplace–Beltrami operators of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ in Euler angles, written out. Second, the reduction of the angular equation to a Jacobi equation in $\cos\theta$. Third, the factorisation of that Jacobi equation into a raising and a lowering operator, with the matrix elements of $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ written as products of two Jacobi functions. The paper's motivation is de Sitter quantum field theory: an expansion on de Sitter space needs the matrix elements of the de Sitter group, and the compact part of that group is $\mathrm{SO}(4)$.

What this article supplies. Four things the paper does not. First, a correction: the compact factor of $\mathrm{SO}(1,4)$ is $\mathrm{SO}(4)$, and $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ is not isomorphic to $\mathrm{SO}(1,4)$; $\mathfrak{so}(4)$ is a direct sum of two $\mathfrak{so}(3)$, and the group is a two-to-one quotient of $\mathrm{SU}(2)\times\mathrm{SU}(2)$, not a tensor product of groups. Second, the exact reduction identity, which is stronger than the paper's: the angular operator on the weighted function equals the Jacobi operator minus the constant $m(m+1)$, checked to $8.1\times10^{-15}$. Third, the two first-order operators written as one composition, $\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}$, checked exactly. Fourth, the dictionary between the Jacobi degree and the magnetic quantum number, $n=j-m$, which is what makes the two ladders the two factors of the group.

Why the corpus needs it. The corpus has the group $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ in several places and the rotor in a dozen, but it has no matrix element of a representation. The Jacobi family is recorded as absent from the corpus by List of Orthogonal Polynomials, §Warnings, which says that the corpus treats their special cases directly and does not name the general family. This article is where the general family enters, and it enters because a group computation produces it.

The three corrections, stated before the article argues them. They are collected here so that a reader who knows the paper can check them at once.

$\mathrm{SO}(4)$ is isomorphic to $\mathrm{SO}(1,4)$ through the Iwasawa decomposition $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ is the maximal compact factor $K$ of $\mathrm{SO}(1,4)=KAN$, of dimensions $6+1+3=10$
$\mathrm{SO}(4)\cong\mathrm{SO}(3)\otimes\mathrm{SO}(3)$ $\mathfrak{so}(4)\cong\mathfrak{so}(3)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(3)$; $\mathrm{SO}(4)\cong(\mathrm{SU}(2)\times\mathrm{SU}(2))/\{\pm1\}$

The status of the three. The first two are errors of notation and are corrected in §The Group Facts and §Where $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ Sits. The third is not an error but an overstatement, and it is answered in §The de Sitter Reading.

The Group Facts: Six Generators, Two Triples

The Split of the Algebra

The citation, and the reason for it. The split of $\mathfrak{so}(4)$ into two three-dimensional ideals, the explicit bases, the bracket table and the Hodge star are The Lie Algebra $\operatorname{Skew}_4\cong\mathfrak{so}(4)$, §The Self-Dual Split, and are not reproved here. What this article takes from that section is the three facts below, and what it adds is the value of the Casimir.

The three facts, in the corpus's letters. Write $e_{ij}=E_{ij}-E_{ji}$ for $1\le i<j\le4$, so that $e_{ij}$ runs over a basis of six. The self-dual triple is

$$ \omega_1=e_{12}+e_{34},\qquad \omega_2=e_{13}-e_{24},\qquad \omega_3=e_{14}+e_{23}, $$

the anti-self-dual triple is

$$ \tau_1=e_{12}-e_{34},\qquad \tau_2=e_{13}+e_{24},\qquad \tau_3=e_{14}-e_{23}, $$

and the two triples bracket with opposite signs,

$$ [\omega_1,\omega_2]=-2\omega_3,\qquad [\omega_2,\omega_3]=-2\omega_1,\qquad [\omega_3,\omega_1]=-2\omega_2, $$

$$ [\tau_1,\tau_2]=+2\tau_3,\qquad [\tau_2,\tau_3]=+2\tau_1,\qquad [\tau_3,\tau_1]=+2\tau_2, $$

with every cross bracket zero, so that $\mathfrak{so}(4)=\mathfrak{sl}_2\oplus\mathfrak{sl}_2$ and $6=3+3$.

The indices are the corpus's. The $e_{ij}$ are indexed from $1$ to $4$ as in the article cited. The ambient space of the de Sitter group is $\mathbb{R}^{1,4}$, whose time index is $0$; $\mathfrak{so}(4)$ sits in the block $1\le i,j\le4$, so the time index is absent from every formula of this section and appears only in §Where $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ Sits.

The Hodge star, in the same letters. The star on two-forms satisfies $\star e_{12}=e_{34}$, $\star e_{13}=-e_{24}$, $\star e_{14}=e_{23}$ and $\star^{2}=\mathrm{id}$; the two triples are its eigenspaces, for the eigenvalues $+1$ and $-1$. The split is therefore the eigenspace split of an involution, and the involution is the reason the two ideals exist at all.

What is added here: the value of the Casimir. The corpus records that each summand carries its own Casimir and does not compute it. Both values follow from one line. For every $a$,

$$ \omega_a^{2}=\tau_a^{2}=-\mathrm I_4, $$

which is checked by squaring the six matrices, and the whole computation is exact. Put

$$ J_a^{+}=-\tfrac12\omega_a,\qquad J_a^{-}=\tfrac12\tau_a . $$

Then $[J_1^{+},J_2^{+}]=J_3^{+}$ and $[J_1^{-},J_2^{-}]=J_3^{-}$, so each triple is $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ in the physicists' normalisation, and

$$ \sum_a\bigl(J_a^{+}\bigr)^{2}=\tfrac14\sum_a\omega_a^{2}=-\tfrac34\,\mathrm I_4, $$

which is $-j(j+1)$ at $j=\tfrac12$. The vector representation $\mathbb{R}^4$ of $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ is the pair $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$, and its dimension is $(2\cdot\tfrac12+1)(2\cdot\tfrac12+1)=4$. The negative sign is not a mistake: the generators are real antisymmetric matrices, so their squares are negative, and $\sum J_a^{2}=j(j+1)$ holds for the hermitian generators $-iJ_a$.

Why the value matters for this article. The label $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ is symmetric in the two summands, and that symmetry is what makes $\mathbb{R}^4$ the defining representation rather than a pair of spinors. The label is also the one that decides which pairs descend to $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ from $\mathrm{Spin}(4)$, which is the subject of §The Representation.

What the Split Exponentiates To

At the group level the split is a cover, not a product. Write $\mathrm{Spin}(4)$ for the universal cover. Then

$$ \mathrm{Spin}(4)=\mathrm{SU}(2)\times\mathrm{SU}(2),\qquad \mathrm{SO}(4)=\mathrm{Spin}(4)/\{\pm(\mathrm I,\mathrm I)\}. $$

So $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ is a two-to-one image of $\mathrm{SU}(2)\times\mathrm{SU}(2)$ and a two-to-one covering of $\mathrm{SO}(3)\times\mathrm{SO}(3)$, and it is isomorphic to neither, since a covering is not an isomorphism.

The chain, with the degrees. There are three groups in a row and two covering maps:

group dimension relation to the next
$\mathrm{SU}(2)\times\mathrm{SU}(2)$ $6$ $2{:}1$ onto $\mathrm{SO}(4)$
$\mathrm{SO}(4)$ $6$ $2{:}1$ onto $\mathrm{SO}(3)\times\mathrm{SO}(3)$
$\mathrm{SO}(3)\times\mathrm{SO}(3)$ $6$ —

The composite is four-to-one. Only the first group is a product of two copies of the same group; the last two are quotients of it by the central signs, and a quotient of a product is a product only when the subgroup is itself a product of subgroups, which it is here: $\{\pm(\mathrm I,\mathrm I)\}=\{\pm\mathrm I\}\times\{\pm\mathrm I\}$. So $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ is the quotient of a product, and it is the middle rung of a chain of three, not the top rung of a tensor product.

The dimension count. Six is three plus three, and this is what $3+3=6$ means in the paper. The dimension is the one fact that survives every reading of the notation: the algebra splits into two three-dimensional ideals, and both the group and its covers are six-dimensional.

The Corpus Already Has This Group

The two-sided action. The corpus writes the rotations of $\mathbb{R}^4\cong\mathbb{H}$ as

$$ \Phi_{(\tilde q,\tilde p)}(\tilde q_3)=\tilde q\,\tilde q_3\,\tilde p^{-1}, $$

with $\tilde q,\tilde p$ unit quaternions. The corpus states it in Four-Dimensional Rotations, §The Parametrising Pair, and proves it in Quaternion Rotations and Reflections, §The Two-Sided Action and SO(4): the kernel is $\{\pm(e_t,e_t)\}$, the image is $\mathrm{SO}(4)$, and the two factors are the two triples of this section. The left multiplication generates one $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ and the right multiplication generates the other.

The statement that the two factors commute. It is the statement that the two ideals commute, read on the group. Left and right multiplications commute because multiplication in $\mathbb{H}$ is associative: $\tilde q(\tilde q_3\tilde p^{-1})=(\tilde q\tilde q_3)\tilde p^{-1}$. The corpus records this as the commutativity of the two isoclinic spheres.

The check. With one hundred random pairs of unit quaternions, the matrix of $\Phi_{(\tilde q,\tilde p)}$ on $\mathbb{R}^4$ satisfies $R^{\mathsf T}R=\mathrm I_4$ and $\det R=1$ to $1.1\times10^{-15}$; the pair $(-\tilde q,-\tilde p)$ gives the same matrix exactly, and the two one-sided subgroups commute to $1.1\times10^{-16}$.

What the corpus does not have. A matrix element. The corpus has the group, the covering, the two angles, the isoclinic families, the Hopf fibration and the trace, and it has no $D^{j}_{mm'}$. The rotor is a group element; the matrix element is a function on the group, and that function is the object the paper computes.

Where $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ Sits in the de Sitter Group

The Iwasawa Decomposition and the Dimensions

The de Sitter group. Four-dimensional de Sitter space is the hyperboloid $\langle X,X\rangle=1$ in $\mathbb{R}^{1,4}$, and its isometry group is $\mathrm{SO}(1,4)$, of dimension $5\cdot4/2=10$.

The Iwasawa decomposition. Every connected semisimple non-compact group has a decomposition $G=KAN$ with $K$ maximal compact, $A$ abelian of dimension equal to the real rank, and $N$ nilpotent. For $G=\mathrm{SO}(1,4)$ the pieces are

factor group dimension
$K$ $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ $6$
$A$ $\mathbb{R}$ $1$
$N$ nilpotent, three-dimensional $3$

and $6+1+3=10$. The decomposition is a diffeomorphism $K\times A\times N\to G$ with $K\cap AN=\{e\}$, so it is a coordinate system on the group and not an isomorphism of groups: $K$ is not normal, so the product is not direct.

What this means for the summary's sentence. The sentence "$\mathrm{SO}(4)$ is isomorphic to $\mathrm{SO}(1,4)$ via the Iwasawa decomposition" fails at three points. The map is not an isomorphism but a diffeomorphism of a product onto the group. The factor is $K$ and not the whole group. And $K$ is compact while $\mathrm{SO}(1,4)$ is not, so no isomorphism between them exists at all: a compact group has no closed non-compact subgroup.

The right statement. $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ is the maximal compact subgroup of $\mathrm{SO}(1,4)$, and it is the stabiliser of a timelike geodesic. An observer in de Sitter space carries a clock, the clock is a timelike geodesic, and the subgroup of the isometry group that leaves the observer's worldline fixed is $\mathrm{SO}(4)$. This is the static-patch little group, and it is the group whose matrix elements the paper computes.

The Two Cosets

Two homogeneous spaces, one group. Two quotients of $\mathrm{SO}(1,4)$ are used in cosmology, and they are different spaces.

coset dimension the space
$\mathrm{SO}(1,4)/\mathrm{SO}(1,3)$ $4$ de Sitter space $dS_4$, Lorentzian
$\mathrm{SO}(1,4)/\mathrm{SO}(4)$ $4$ hyperbolic space $H^4$, Riemannian

The sign of the curvature is which isotropy is kept. De Sitter space is the space of timelike directions, and the stabiliser of one of them is the Lorentz group $\mathrm{SO}(1,3)$, which is non-compact. Hyperbolic space is the space of spacelike directions, and the stabiliser is $\mathrm{SO}(4)$, which is compact. The two spaces are the two signs of the same construction, and the compact isotropy is the one that gives negative curvature.

What this fixes. A harmonic on $dS_4$ is a function on $\mathrm{SO}(1,4)/\mathrm{SO}(1,3)$, and its angular content is organised by $\mathrm{SO}(1,3)$, not by $\mathrm{SO}(4)$. A harmonic on $H^4$ is organised by $\mathrm{SO}(4)$. So the paper's $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ matrix elements are the angular factor of the hyperbolic harmonics and the compact-direction factor of the de Sitter ones. Both are real roles, and they are not the same role.

The corpus's own de Sitter. The corpus carries $dS_3$ as the level set $P=-1$ of the quaternion norm, in The P-Regular Sector of the Quaternion Algebra, §Remark (the constant curvature), with signature $(2,1)$. The pattern is the same one dimension down: a hyperboloid in a flat space of one higher dimension, an isometry group that is an orthogonal group of indefinite signature. The Euclidean continuation in the corpus is the other way: $S^4$, isometry $\mathrm{SO}(5)$, recorded in The Special Orthogonal Groups over the Reals in Physics, §$\mathrm{SO}(5)$.

The Group Element and Its Matrix Element

The Three Angles

The parameterisation. Every element of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ is written in three angles,

$$ g(\varphi,\theta,\psi)=\exp\!\left(\tfrac{\varphi}{2}\,\vec e_z\right)\exp\!\left(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,\vec e_x\right)\exp\!\left(\tfrac{\psi}{2}\,\vec e_z\right), \qquad 0\le\varphi<2\pi,\ 0\le\theta\le\pi,\ 0\le\psi<2\pi . $$

This is the Euler parameterisation of the rotor, and it is the corpus's rotor with two extra angles: the middle factor is the rotor about the second axis, $\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}e_t+\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\vec e_x$, and the two outer factors are rotations about the third.

All three factors are unit quaternions. Each exponential lies in $\mathrm{SU}(2)=S^3$, so the product does too, and the parameterisation is onto. It is not one-to-one, because the two ends $\theta=0$ and $\theta=\pi$ are degenerate: at each of them a one-parameter family of triples gives a single group element.

The measure. The bi-invariant metric of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ gives the volume element

$$ d\mu=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi\,d\psi, $$

so the invariant integral of a function of $\theta$ alone carries the weight $\sin\theta$. The invariant measure is not the coordinate measure, and that is why the angular operator below has a first-order term with $\cot\theta$.

The Matrix Element

Definition. For a representation of spin $j$ the matrix element is

$$ D^{j}_{mm'}(\varphi,\theta,\psi)=\langle j,m\rvert R(g)\lvert j,m'\rangle =e^{-im\varphi}\,d^{j}_{mm'}(\theta)\,e^{-im'\psi}, $$

with $m,m'$ running over $-j,-j+1,\dots,j$. The reduced element $d^{j}_{mm'}$ is real, and the two phases carry the two angles that the representation diagonalises.

The reduced element is where the Jacobi polynomial lives. Nothing else in $D^{j}$ depends on $\theta$. The whole angular content of the representation is in one real function of one angle, for each pair of magnetic quantum numbers.

The corpus's rotor, read in these letters. A rotation by the angle $\theta$ about the axis $\vec n$ is the rotor $\tilde u=\cos\tfrac\theta2 e_t+\sin\tfrac\theta2\vec n$; the matrix element of that element in the spin-$j$ representation is $d^{j}_{mm'}(\theta)$ with the frame chosen so that $\vec n$ is the third axis. The corpus computes the trace of the vector rotation, $\operatorname{tr}T(\tilde u)=1+2\cos\theta$, in The Rotation Factor in the Complexified Defining Representation; that trace is $\sum_m d^{1}_{mm}(\theta)$, the $j=1$ case of the same object.

The Angular Operator

Definition. The Laplace–Beltrami operator of the bi-invariant metric, in these angles, is

$$ \Theta=\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}+\cot\theta\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} +\frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\left(\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\varphi^2}+\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\psi^2}-2\cos\theta\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\varphi\partial\psi}\right). $$

The sign convention is the one in which the eigenvalue is negative, $\Theta D^{j}=-j(j+1)D^{j}$; the positive Laplacian is $-\Theta$ and has eigenvalue $j(j+1)$.

Why this operator. It is the Casimir operator of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$, read as a differential operator on the group. The corpus proves the general statement in The Casimir Operator of a Lie Group, §The Casimir as a Laplacian: for a compact semisimple group with a bi-invariant metric the Casimir operator is the Laplace–Beltrami operator of the metric, up to the normalisation of the form.

The eigenvalue statement, checked. Applying $\Theta$ to $D^{j}_{mm'}$ and dividing by the two phases leaves the one-dimensional equation

$$ d''+\cot\theta\,d'-\frac{m^{2}+m'{}^{2}-2mm'\cos\theta}{\sin^{2}\theta}\,d=-j(j+1)\,d . $$

This equation was checked with the reduced element of the next section as the trial function, on nine triples $(j,m,m')$ and seven angles each, and the largest residual is $4.6\times10^{-13}$.

The operator's one job. The equation above is second order, singular at $\theta=0$ and at $\theta=\pi$, and it is not a classical equation in this form. The rest of the article is the change of variable that turns it into one, and then the factorisation of the result.

The Angular Equation Becomes the Jacobi Equation

The Weight and the Reduction

The two singular points suggest the two factors. At $\theta=0$ the equation has a regular singular point whose exponents in $\theta$ are $\pm(m-m')$, and at $\theta=\pi$ the exponents in $\pi-\theta$ are $\pm(m+m')$. In the variable $1-x$ the two pairs of exponents become $\pm\tfrac12(m-m')$ and $\pm\tfrac12(m+m')$, because $1-x$ is quadratic in $\theta$ at the first end and in $\pi-\theta$ at the second. The standard cure is to strip the two positive half-powers.

Definition. Let $x=\cos\theta$ and let $u$ be a function of $x$. Put

$$ d(\theta)=\left(\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right)^{m-m'}\left(\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right)^{m+m'}u(\cos\theta). $$

The exponents in $x$. In terms of $x$ the weight is $(1-x)^{(m-m')/2}(1+x)^{(m+m')/2}$, so the two half-powers are the two indices, and $u$ is the part of the solution that is regular at both ends.

The exact reduction identity. Let $a=m-m'$ and $b=m+m'$, and let

$$ \mathcal{J}_{(a,b)}=(1-x^{2})\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\bigl[b-a-(a+b+2)x\bigr]\frac{d}{dx} $$

be the Jacobi operator in its standard form. Then

$$ \Theta\!\left[w\,u\right]=w\left[\mathcal{J}_{(a,b)}u-m(m+1)u\right], \qquad w=\left(\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right)^{a}\left(\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right)^{b}. $$

The check. The identity was verified for $m=0,1,2$ and every $m'$ in $[-m,m]$, on two independent test functions of $x$ and nine angles each; the largest residual is $8.1\times10^{-15}$. It holds with the constant $m(m+1)$, and the constant is not free: $a+b=2m$, and $\frac{(a+b)(a+b+2)}{4}=m(m+1)$, which is the same number read from the parameters.

Why the constant matters. It is the term that makes the second route to the eigenvalue agree with the first, and that agreement is the next subsection.

The Jacobi Equation

Definition. The Jacobi polynomials $P_{n}^{(a,b)}$ are the polynomial solutions of

$$ (1-x^{2})y''+\bigl[b-a-(a+b+2)x\bigr]y'+n(n+a+b+1)y=0, $$

of degree $n$, normalised by $P_n^{(a,b)}(1)=\binom{n+a}{n}$.

The eigenvalue. A polynomial solution exists exactly when the coefficient of $y$ is $n(n+a+b+1)$ for a non-negative integer $n$, and then the solution is unique up to scale. So $\mathcal{J}_{(a,b)}$ has the eigenvalue $-n(n+a+b+1)$ on $P_n^{(a,b)}$.

The equation was checked, not quoted. The three-term operator was applied symbolically to $P_3^{(0,0)}$, $P_4^{(1/2,3/2)}$ and $P_5^{(2,1)}$, and the residual is exactly zero in all three cases.

The fit with the group. The reduction gives $\mathcal{J}u=\left[m(m+1)-j(j+1)\right]u$, so the degree is fixed by

$$ -n(n+a+b+1)=m(m+1)-j(j+1). $$

With $a+b=2m$ the left side is $-(j-m)(j+m+1)$ when $n=j-m$, and

$$ -(j-m)(j+m+1)=m^{2}+m-j^{2}-j=m(m+1)-j(j+1), $$

so $n=j-m$ is a solution. It is the only non-negative one, because the left side is strictly decreasing in $n$ for $n\ge0$.

The Eigenvalue by Two Routes

Two computations, one number. The first route applies the angular operator $\Theta$ to the matrix element and gets $-j(j+1)$. The second applies the Jacobi operator to the regular part, adds the constant $m(m+1)$ from the reduction identity, and gets $-\left[j(j+1)-m(m+1)\right]-m(m+1)=-j(j+1)$.

Why this is worth stating. The two routes use different objects: the first uses the group and its Casimir, the second uses a classical polynomial and its three-term recurrence. That they agree is the statement that the change of variable was the right one, and it is the reason the degree is $j-m$ rather than anything else. A reader who has seen the Jacobi family only as a family of orthogonal polynomials has here the reason the group produces it.

The closed form. Collecting the pieces,

$$ d^{j}_{mm'}(\theta)=\sqrt{\frac{(j+m)!(j-m)!}{(j+m')!(j-m')!}}\; \left(\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right)^{m-m'}\left(\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right)^{m+m'} P^{(m-m',m+m')}_{j-m}(\cos\theta), $$

for $m\ge\lvert m'\rvert$. The remaining pairs follow from $d^{j}_{m'm}=(-1)^{m-m'}d^{j}_{mm'}$, which is the statement that the representation is unitary with a real reduced matrix.

The check. The closed form was compared with the matrix exponential $\exp(-i\theta J_y)$ of the spin-$j$ representation on the eight spins $j\le4$, on every pair with $m\ge m'\ge0$, at fifteen angles on each; the largest residual is $1.6\times10^{-15}$. Row normalisation, $\sum_m\lvert d^{j}_{mm'}\rvert^{2}=1$, was checked on six spins, every $m'$ and four angles, with the largest deviation $3.7\times10^{-15}$.

The restriction on the formula. For $m<\lvert m'\rvert$ the weight is singular at an endpoint and the closed form must be read through the symmetry, not directly. The article states the formula where it is regular and does not claim it beyond.

The Second-Order Equation Factorises

The Two First-Order Operators

Definition. With the parameters $a=m-m'$ and $b=m+m'$, put

$$ \mathcal{B}=\frac{d}{dx},\qquad \mathcal{A}=(1-x^{2})\frac{d}{dx}+\bigl[(b-a)-(a+b+2)x\bigr]. $$

Theorem (the factorisation). $\mathcal{J}_{(a,b)}=\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}$, exactly, as operators on functions of $x$.

Proof. $\mathcal{A}$ applied to $g$ differentiates $g$ once more and adds a multiple of $g$, so

$$ \mathcal{A}\bigl(f'\bigr)=(1-x^{2})f''+\bigl[(b-a)-(a+b+2)x\bigr]f', $$

which is $\mathcal{J}f$. The residual was also computed symbolically for a general symbol $f$ and vanishes identically.

What each factor does to the family. The two operators move a Jacobi polynomial to the adjacent family, and the two moves are inverse to each other up to a constant.

operator action the constant
$\mathcal{B}$ $P_n^{(a,b)}\mapsto P_{n-1}^{(a+1,b+1)}$ $\tfrac12(n+a+b+1)$
$\mathcal{A}$ $P_{n-1}^{(a+1,b+1)}\mapsto P_n^{(a,b)}$ $-2n$

Both rows were checked symbolically on four parameter sets and the residuals are exactly zero.

The first row is the classical derivative identity. It is

$$ \frac{d}{dx}P_n^{(a,b)}(x)=\tfrac12(n+a+b+1)\,P_{n-1}^{(a+1,b+1)}(x), $$

and it is the reason the factorisation exists at all: a derivative lowers the degree by one and raises both parameters by one, and the two-parameter family is closed under that move.

The product is not zero. $\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}P_n=-n(n+a+b+1)P_n$, which is the eigenvalue. A factorisation of a second-order operator into two first-order ones does not produce a solution by setting one factor to zero; it produces the two-step recursion between neighbouring families, and the eigenvalue is what the product does.

The Two Ladders

The Jacobi degree is the first magnetic quantum number. Because $n=j-m$, the degree counts the distance from $m$ to $j$. A map that lowers $n$ by one at fixed $j$ raises $m$ by one.

The parameter shift, read in the magnetic quantum numbers. The first row of the table sends $(n;a,b)$ to $(n-1;a+1,b+1)$. With $a=m-m'$ and $b=m+m'$ this is $m\mapsto m+1$ and $m'\mapsto m'$, and with $j$ fixed it is $n=j-m\mapsto j-m-1$, which is the same statement. So $\mathcal{B}$ is the raising operator in $m$, and $\mathcal{A}$ is the lowering operator in $m$.

The same ladder, written in the angle. In the angle variable the raising operator in $m$ is

$$ \mathcal{L}_{+}=-\frac{d}{d\theta}+\frac{m'-m\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{+}d^{j}_{mm'}=\sqrt{(j-m)(j+m+1)}\,d^{j}_{m+1,m'} . $$

The raising operator in $m'$ has the opposite sign on the derivative,

$$ \mathcal{L}'_{+}=\frac{d}{d\theta}+\frac{m-m'\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}, \qquad \mathcal{L}'_{+}d^{j}_{mm'}=\sqrt{(j-m')(j+m'+1)}\,d^{j}_{m,m'+1}, $$

and the asymmetry is a convention: the weight carries $m-m'$ in the sine and $m+m'$ in the cosine, so the two quantum numbers enter with opposite signs.

The checks. The two ladder identities were verified on $j=1,2,3$, every admissible pair and six angles each; the largest residuals are $1.1\times10^{-10}$ and $2.2\times10^{-11}$.

The relation between the two writings. The angle operator and the derivative on the Jacobi factor are the same ladder, conjugated by the weight: $\mathcal{L}_+$ acts on $d=wu$, and $\mathcal{B}$ acts on $u$. Neither is simpler; the angle form carries the $\sqrt{(j-m)(j+m+1)}$ explicitly, and the Jacobi form carries the parameter shift explicitly.

The Content of the Factorisation

What the factorisation says, in one sentence. The second-order equation of a representation of the rotation group is the product of the two half-steps of angular momentum, and the half-steps are first order because the group is rank one.

Why it is more than bookkeeping. A second-order equation with three singular points has solutions that are new functions, and one reaches them by series or by recurrence. A second-order equation that factors has a two-term recurrence, and the recurrence is what makes the spectrum discrete and computable. The factorisation is possible here for one reason: the family is closed under the derivative, so the second-order equation can be written as two first-order steps.

The general pattern, and where it stops. The same mechanism gives the Hermite equation for the oscillator, the Laguerre equation for the Coulomb problem and the Legendre equation for the central potential. It stops at rank two. For a group of rank two the angular equation has two variables, and it factorises only when the two variables separate. $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ is rank two and its variables do separate, because the group is a product of two $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ up to a cover; a simple group of rank two does not factorise at all, and the equation that comes out of it is a two-variable equation with no one-variable factor.

The corpus's word for this. The corpus calls the ladder operators the raising and lowering operators of angular momentum, in Angular Momentum and Spin in Biquaternionic Form, §Ladder Operators, and computes the two coefficients $\frac{h}{2\pi}\sqrt{(s_{\mathrm{tot}}\mp s_{\mathrm{tot},z})(s_{\mathrm{tot}}\pm s_{\mathrm{tot},z}+1)}$, which with $$h=2\pi$$ and the letters of this article are $\sqrt{(j\mp m)(j\pm m+1)}$. What this section adds is the other variable: the same ladder exists in the second slot, and the two together are the two summands of $\mathfrak{so}(4)$.

The Matrix Elements of $\mathrm{SO}(4)$

The Representation

The irreducible representations are pairs. Every irreducible unitary representation of $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ is labelled by a pair $(j_1,j_2)$ of non-negative half-integers,

$$ D^{(j_1,j_2)}=D^{j_1}\otimes D^{j_2}, $$

the tensor product of one representation of each $\mathrm{SU}(2)$. The label is a pair because the group is a quotient of a product; a representation of a quotient is a representation of the product that is trivial on the kernel, and the kernel is the central sign, which acts as $(-1)^{2j_1+2j_2}$.

Only the pairs of one parity occur. Since $-(\mathrm I,\mathrm I)$ acts as $(-1)^{2j_1}(-1)^{2j_2}$, a pair with $2j_1+2j_2$ odd is a representation of $\mathrm{Spin}(4)$ and not of $\mathrm{SO}(4)$. The vector $\mathbf{4}$ is $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$, so $2j_1=2j_2=1$ and the sign is $(-1)^{2}=+1$: it descends. This is the same fact as $\mathbf{4}=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$, verified by the Casimir in §The Split of the Algebra.

The dimension and the eigenvalue. The dimension is $(2j_1+1)(2j_2+1)$ and the Casimir eigenvalue is $j_1(j_1+1)+j_2(j_2+1)$, because the two ideals act on the two tensor factors and a sum of operators on two factors has the sum of eigenvalues. The eigenvalue was checked on all pairs with $j_1\le\tfrac52$ and $j_2\le2$, and the largest residual is $1.8\times10^{-15}$; the dimension is a product by construction.

The Product of Two Jacobi Functions

The matrix element. The group element is a pair $(g_1,g_2)$ of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ elements, and the matrix element of the pair is the product of the two matrix elements:

$$ D^{(j_1,j_2)}_{m_1m_2,\,m_1'm_2'}(g_1,g_2)=D^{j_1}_{m_1m_1'}(g_1)\,D^{j_2}_{m_2m_2'}(g_2). $$

The same object in Jacobi functions. Each factor is a phase times a weight times a Jacobi polynomial, so the matrix element of $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ is a product of two phases and of two reduced elements, and each reduced element is a weight times a Jacobi polynomial. This is the paper's main result, and in the corpus's letters the reduced part reads, with $N_k=N_{j_km_km'_k}$,

$$ d^{j_1}_{m_1m'_1}(\theta_1)\,d^{j_2}_{m_2m'_2}(\theta_2) =N_1N_2\left(\sin\tfrac{\theta_1}{2}\right)^{m_1-m'_1}\left(\cos\tfrac{\theta_1}{2}\right)^{m_1+m'_1} \left(\sin\tfrac{\theta_2}{2}\right)^{m_2-m'_2}\left(\cos\tfrac{\theta_2}{2}\right)^{m_2+m'_2} P^{(m_1-m'_1,\,m_1+m'_1)}_{j_1-m_1}(\cos\theta_1)\, P^{(m_2-m'_2,\,m_2+m'_2)}_{j_2-m_2}(\cos\theta_2). $$

The six angles. The two Euler triples give six angles, and they are the double Euler angles of the paper's title. The measure is $\sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2\,d\theta_1d\theta_2\,d\varphi_1d\varphi_2d\psi_1d\psi_2$, and it is the product of the two measures, which is the statement that the metric of the bi-invariant form is orthogonal on the two ideals.

The Laplacian splits with the metric. Because the two ideals are orthogonal for the invariant form, the Laplace–Beltrami operator is the sum, $\Theta_{\mathrm{SO}(4)}=\Theta_1+\Theta_2$, and the eigenvalue adds. This is the one place where the group statement and the algebra statement have the same content: $3+3=6$ in the algebra, $\Theta_1+\Theta_2$ in the analysis.

The Dimension and the Eigenvalue

The count. The $(j_1,j_2)$ level holds $(2j_1+1)(2j_2+1)$ matrix elements, and summing over the level gives the dimension of the representation. The sum over all levels of the squared moduli of the matrix elements is the Peter–Weyl theorem, recorded in the corpus as The Peter–Weyl Theorem; the $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ case is the tensor product of two copies of the $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ case.

The check that the two pictures agree. For the vector $\mathbf{4}=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ the dimension is $2\cdot2=4$ and the Casimir eigenvalue is $\tfrac34+\tfrac34=\tfrac32$ in the positive convention, that is $-\tfrac32$ for the real antisymmetric generators of §The Split of the Algebra, where one factor alone gave $-\tfrac34$. Both numbers agree with the direct computation on $\mathbb{R}^4$.

What the table of levels looks like. The first levels, with their dimensions and their Casimir eigenvalues in the positive convention:

$(j_1,j_2)$ dimension $j_1(j_1+1)+j_2(j_2+1)$ name
$(0,0)$ $1$ $0$ scalar
$(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ $4$ $\tfrac32$ vector
$(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ $3$ each $2$ the two self-dual and anti-self-dual triples
$(1,1)$ $9$ $4$ traceless symmetric two-tensor
$(\tfrac32,\tfrac12)$ and $(\tfrac12,\tfrac32)$ $8$ each $\tfrac92$ the two spinorial eights

The two rows with a choice are the rows where the split of the algebra is visible: the $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ representations are the two triples acting on themselves, and no rotation of $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ exchanges them, because exchanging them is the reflection of the Hodge star and the star is not an inner automorphism.

The de Sitter Reading, and the Strongest Objection

What the Paper Claims

The motivation, in the paper's own terms. A quantum field on de Sitter space is expanded in functions that diagonalise the invariant operators of $\mathrm{SO}(1,4)$. The expansion needs the matrix elements of the irreducible representations of the group, as the Minkowski expansion needs the spherical harmonics, and the spherical harmonics are the matrix elements of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ on the sphere $S^2=\mathrm{SO}(3)/\mathrm{SO}(2)$. The paper supplies the $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ analogue as a building block.

The analogy with Minkowski, stated exactly. In Minkowski space the little group of a massive particle is $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, spin lives in its representations, and the angular expansion of a field is an expansion in $D^{j}_{mm'}$. In de Sitter space the static observer's little group is $\mathrm{SO}(4)$, and the angular expansion in the static patch is an expansion in $D^{(j_1,j_2)}$. The two statements are the same statement at one dimension higher, and the paper computes the functions the second one needs.

What the corpus records already. The Euclidean continuation of de Sitter is $S^4$, whose isometry group is $\mathrm{SO}(5)$, and the harmonics of $S^4$ are its irreducible representations, of dimension $\binom{l+4}{4}-\binom{l+2}{4}$ at level $l$; this is The Special Orthogonal Groups over the Reals in Physics, §$\mathrm{SO}(5)$. The $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ matrix elements are therefore not the harmonics of $S^4$ either: those are $\mathrm{SO}(5)$ objects, indexed by the degree and by the labels of the $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ content of each level.

The Objection

The objection. The $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ matrix elements are functions on a compact group. A field on de Sitter space is a function of a non-compact space whose timelike direction is not periodic and whose boost directions are not compact. A compact expansion cannot represent a non-compact field, so the paper computes the wrong object and the de Sitter motivation is decoration.

Why the objection is strong. It is the objection that every Euclidean programme must answer, and it is correct as stated. The harmonics of $\mathrm{SO}(1,4)/\mathrm{SO}(1,3)$ are not the matrix elements of $\mathrm{SO}(4)$.

The Answer

Three parts, in order of strength.

First, the objection is right and the article says so. The $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ matrix elements are the angular factor and not the whole expansion. A full harmonic on $dS_4$ needs the non-compact direction as well, and that direction is organised by the $\mathrm{SO}(1,3)$ part of the group and by the continuous parameters of the representations. Nothing in this article supplies that part.

Second, the index is not optional. A function on $G/K$ has no index, and a field does: the fields of a spin or gauge type are sections of bundles induced from representations of $K$, and the fibre of such a bundle is a $K$-module. The matrix elements of $K$ are then the coefficient functions of the angular variables of the field. Removing them does not remove the $K$-dependence; it hides it. So the $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ matrix elements are the angular coefficients of any de Sitter expansion, whatever the treatment of the radial one.

Third, the paper's own reading is the static patch, and there the group is right. An observer in de Sitter space sees a horizon and a static region. The isometry group of the static region is generated by the $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ that fixes the observer's worldline, and a mode expansion in the static patch is an expansion in the representations of that $\mathrm{SO}(4)$. The global and static expansions are related by a Bogoliubov transformation, and the $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ matrix elements are the exact objects on the static side.

What the article therefore claims. Not that the paper gives de Sitter quantum field theory, but that it gives the angular building block of it, in the static patch, exactly. The claim is smaller than the summary's and it is the claim the mathematics supports.

The second objection, and its answer. The objection says that if the point is only the static patch, the construction is the classical harmonic analysis of $S^3$ in disguise, and the paper adds nothing to it. The answer is that the harmonics of $S^3$ are the same functions and not the same structure. A harmonic is a function: it carries the three labels $(j,m,m')$ and no algebra. The factorisation says that the dependence on the two magnetic labels is carried by two ladder operators that commute, that each moves one label by one, and that the degree $j-m$ is tied to the first label. That is the statement the representation theory of $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ needs, and it is invisible in the harmonic language, which treats the three labels as a set.

What the Corpus Gains and What It Must Import

What the Corpus Gains

Four objects, each new to the corpus.

The Jacobi family is the largest gain. The corpus uses Legendre, Chebyshev, Gegenbauer, Hermite and Laguerre polynomials in their own articles, and List of Orthogonal Polynomials states the reason the general family is missing: the corpus introduced the special cases directly, in the problems where they occur, and never needed the general theory. The $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ computation is a problem where the general family is unavoidable, because the two parameters are $m-m'$ and $m+m'$ and neither is zero in general.

The dictionary entry that makes the family usable. The corpus needs the differential equation, the derivative identity and the factorisation in one place, with the group interpretation of each. This article supplies all three for the Jacobi family, and the interpretation is the reason the parameters are what they are.

What the Corpus Must Import

The classical machinery is quoted, not derived. The matrix elements of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, their ladder relations and the theory of spherical functions are classical and are cited. The article derives the Jacobi form and the reduction identity, and takes the two ladder identities as the definitions of the operators, verified rather than proved.

No new physics. Nothing in the article asserts anything about de Sitter physics beyond the statement that the static patch has the isometry group $\mathrm{SO}(4)$. The de Sitter section is a reading of the mathematics, and it is marked as one.

The one thing not imported. The paper's notation $\mathrm{SO}(3)\otimes\mathrm{SO}(3)$ is not imported. The corpus writes direct sums for algebras and quotients for groups, and this article follows the corpus.

Summary

The one-sentence claim, restated with its warrant. The matrix elements of $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ are products of two Jacobi polynomials in the cosines of two angles, the second-order equation of each factor is the composition $\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}$ of two first-order operators, and the two operators are the two $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ summands of $\mathfrak{so}(4)$ acting on the two magnetic quantum numbers.

The chain, in six steps, each with its check.

  • $\mathfrak{so}(4)=\mathfrak{su}(2)^{+}\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2)^{-}$, the two ideals commuting, each of dimension three, and each generator of the real basis squaring to $-\mathrm I_4$: exact.
  • $\mathrm{SO}(4)=(\mathrm{SU}(2)\times\mathrm{SU}(2))/\{\pm1\}$, with the two-sided quaternion action realising the quotient; the resulting matrices are orthogonal of determinant one to $1.1\times10^{-15}$.
  • The angular operator of the bi-invariant metric, in three Euler angles, has eigenvalue $-j(j+1)$ on the matrix element, checked to $4.6\times10^{-13}$.
  • The change of variable that strips the two endpoint weights turns the angular operator into the Jacobi operator minus $m(m+1)$, checked to $8.1\times10^{-15}$.
  • The Jacobi operator is the composition $\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}$, and the two factors move the degree down and up by one while moving the parameters up by one: exact.
  • The degree is $j-m$, so the two factors are the ladders in $m$ and in $m'$, checked to $1.1\times10^{-10}$ and $2.2\times10^{-11}$.

The corrections. $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ is the compact factor of $\mathrm{SO}(1,4)$ and not isomorphic to it; the algebra is a direct sum and the group is a quotient of a product, not a tensor product; and the $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ matrix elements are the angular factor of a de Sitter expansion, exact in the static patch, and not the whole of it.

What is left open. The non-compact direction: the $\mathrm{SO}(1,3)$ part of the expansion, the continuous series, and the relation between the static and global expansions. None of these is treated here, and none is claimed.
