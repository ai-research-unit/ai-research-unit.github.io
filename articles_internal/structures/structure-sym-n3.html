$\mathcal N=3$ Super Yang–Mills: The Degenerate Case

Introduction

The question. Is there a four-dimensional $\mathcal N=3$ super Yang–Mills theory distinct from $\mathcal N=4$? The answer is no for the Lagrangian theory, and yes for a class of non-Lagrangian superconformal field theories. The two answers are often conflated. This note separates them, states exactly what forces the enhancement, and lists what a genuine $\mathcal N=3$ theory must look like.

The answer in one line.

$$ \boxed{\; \text{any }\mathcal N=3\text{ invariant Lagrangian gauge theory in }4D \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathcal N=4\ \text{SYM}, \qquad \text{but genuine }\mathcal N=3\ \text{SCFTs exist and are non-Lagrangian} \;} $$

What is claimed, and what is not. Claimed: the $\mathcal N=3$ vector multiplet has the same components as the $\mathcal N=4$ one; the fourth supersymmetry closes on shell on that multiplet; the $\mathcal N=3$ constraint forces the $\mathcal N=2$ prepotential to be free; the $\beta$-function vanishes; genuine $\mathcal N=3$ SCFTs retain $U(3)_R$ and satisfy $a=c$. Not claimed: that every theory with an $SU(3)_R$ symmetry is $\mathcal N=4$; that $\mathcal N=3$ SCFTs have a Yang–Mills description; that the enhancement is a statement about arbitrary matter content.

The $\mathcal N=3$ Algebra and Its $R$-Symmetry

The algebra has three Weyl supercharges $Q_\alpha^{i}$, $i=1,2,3$:

$$ \{Q_\alpha^{i},\bar Q_{\dot\beta j}\}=2\sigma^{\mu}_{\alpha\dot\beta}P_\mu\,\delta^{i}{}_{j}, \qquad \{Q_\alpha^{i},Q_\beta^{j}\}=\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}Z^{ij}, \qquad Z^{ij}=-Z^{ji}. $$

The central-charge matrix is antisymmetric in the $\mathbf 3$ of $SU(3)_R$: three independent complex entries. The automorphism group is

$$ U(3)_R=SU(3)_R\times U(1)_R, \qquad Q_\alpha^{i}\in\mathbf 3 . $$

The $U(1)_R$ charge of $Q$ is conventionally $+1$. For a Lagrangian theory the exact symmetry is larger, $SU(4)_R$, precisely because the theory is $\mathcal N=4$; for a genuine $\mathcal N=3$ SCFT it is $U(3)_R$ and not more.

Group-theoretic seed of the enhancement.

$$ U(3)_R\subset SU(4)_R, \qquad \mathbf 4_{SU(4)}=\mathbf 3_{SU(3)}\oplus\mathbf 1 . $$

The singlet in the decomposition is the state that the fourth supercharge needs; its presence in the $\mathcal N=3$ multiplet is what makes the enhancement possible.

Field Content

The vector multiplet. On shell,

$$ V_{\mathcal N=3}=\bigl(A_\mu,\;\lambda^{A}\ (A=1,\dots,4),\; z^{A}\ (A=1,2,3)\bigr), $$

all in the adjoint. Under $SU(3)_R$,

$$ \mathbf 4_{\text{gaugini}}=\mathbf 3\oplus\mathbf 1, \qquad \mathbf 6_{\text{real scalars}}=\mathbf 3\oplus\bar{\mathbf 3}, $$

i.e. three complex scalars $z^{A}$ and their conjugates. Degrees of freedom:

sector count
$A_\mu$ $2$
four Weyl gaugini $2\times4=8$
three complex scalars $2\times3=6$
total bosonic $2+6=8$

Balanced. The content is identical to the $\mathcal N=4$ vector multiplet; only the organizing symmetry and the number of supersymmetries are stated differently.

Helicity table. Building the multiplet from $|h=1\rangle$ by acting with $k$ supercharges of the $\mathcal N=3$ algebra and CPT-completing gives

$h$ $+1$ $+\tfrac12$ $0$ $-\tfrac12$ $-1$
multiplicity 1 4 6 4 1

which is the $\mathcal N=4$ table. The reason is that CPT completion restores the missing states: three supercharges alone would give a smaller tower, but the conjugate tower supplies the rest, and the two together are the $\mathcal N=4$ tower.

Decomposition into $\mathcal N=2$ multiplets.

$$ \mathcal N=3\ \text{vector} =\mathcal N=2\ \text{vector}\;\oplus\; \mathcal N=2\ \text{hypermultiplet in the adjoint}. $$

The $\mathcal N=2$ vector gives $(A_\mu,\lambda^{i},\phi)$; the adjoint hypermultiplet gives the remaining two Weyl fermions and two complex scalars. This is the decomposition that makes the enhancement argument concrete, because the $\mathcal N=2$ technology of the prepotential is available.

Why the Fourth Supersymmetry Is Not Optional

There are three independent arguments; they agree.

1. On-shell closure. The $\mathcal N=3$ algebra closes on the vector multiplet only with the equations of motion. Commuting three supercharges on the fields produces the field strength and the fermion bilinears that constitute a fourth transformation. Hence any action invariant under the three is invariant under the fourth. The fourth generator is composite on the physical states; there is no independent fourth charge that could be switched off while keeping the three.

2. Field-content comparison. The multiplets coincide (previous section), and the $\mathcal N=3$ transformation rules are the restriction of the $\mathcal N=4$ ones to a subalgebra. Since the field content and the algebra action agree, the most general renormalizable invariant Lagrangian agrees.

3. Prepotential rigidity. In $\mathcal N=2$ language the $\mathcal N=3$ constraint forbids any prepotential correction:

$$ \mathcal F(\Phi)=\tfrac12\tau_0\Phi^{2}, $$

because a correction would require a second $U(1)$ phase acting on $\Phi$ that the $U(1)_R\subset U(3)_R$ does not provide. The free prepotential is exactly the $\mathcal N=4$ one, the adjoint hypermultiplet must be massless, and the $SU(2)_R\times U(1)_R$ of $\mathcal N=2$ combines with the extra generators into $U(3)_R$.

In components. The would-be $\mathcal N=3$ Lagrangian is

$$ \mathcal L_{\mathcal N=3} =\mathcal L_{\mathcal N=4}\big|_{\text{fields reorganized under }U(3)_R}, $$

with only $g$ and $\theta$ as parameters, and with $\beta(g)=0$.

The $\mathcal N=4$ Rewriting, Explicitly

In $\mathcal N=1$ language the $\mathcal N=4$ vector multiplet is one vector multiplet plus three adjoint chiral multiplets $\Phi_i$, $i=1,2,3$, with

$$ W=\frac{g}{3!}\epsilon^{ijk}f^{abc}\Phi_i^{a}\Phi_j^{b}\Phi_k^{c} \qquad(\text{up to an }i), $$

and the three $\Phi_i$ are the $\mathbf 3$ of the $SU(3)$ inside $SU(4)_R$. The $\mathcal N=3$ subalgebra rotates the three chiral multiplets among themselves and mixes them with the $\mathcal N=1$ gaugino; the extra singlet gaugino that completes the $\mathbf 4$ is precisely the state the fourth supercharge needs.

The one-loop check is immediate: three adjoint chirals contribute $3N_c$ to $b=3N_c-N_f$, so $b=0$. In $\mathcal N=2$ language the same statement is $N_f=2N_c$ and $b=2N_c-N_f=0$. The two computations agree, as they must.

Genuine $\mathcal N=3$ Superconformal Field Theories

What they are not. They are not weakly coupled Yang–Mills theories. The enhancement argument applies to Lagrangians built from the standard vector multiplet; a non-Lagrangian SCFT is outside its hypothesis.

What they are. Four-dimensional SCFTs with

  • R-symmetry exactly $U(3)_R=SU(3)_R\times U(1)_R$;
  • superconformal algebra $SU(2,2|3)$;
  • $a=c$ (a hallmark of $\mathcal N\ge3$);
  • no exactly marginal coupling in the usual sense;
  • typically a construction by discrete twisting of an $\mathcal N=4$ theory.

The $a=c$ statement. For $\mathcal N=3$ the superconformal algebra forces the two trace-anomaly coefficients to coincide. In $\mathcal N=2$ they generally differ; in $\mathcal N=4$ they coincide and, for $SU(N)$, $a=c=(N^{2}-1)/4$. A genuine $\mathcal N=3$ theory is therefore distinguishable from a generic $\mathcal N=2$ SCFT by its anomaly coefficients and its R-symmetry, even before any spectrum is computed.

S-folds. The standard construction starts from an $\mathcal N=4$ theory and gauges a discrete symmetry that combines a rotation of the internal space with an S-duality transformation. For the appropriate discrete choice the result preserves only $\mathcal N=3$. These theories are strongly coupled and have no Yang–Mills description; they are the reason the $\mathcal N=3$ label is not vacuous.

What the existence of these theories does not do. It does not reopen the Lagrangian question. The two statements coexist:

  • $\mathcal N=3$ Lagrangian gauge theory $\Rightarrow$ $\mathcal N=4$;
  • $\mathcal N=3$ SCFT $\not\Rightarrow$ $\mathcal N=4$, but also $\not\Rightarrow$ Lagrangian.

Other Dimensions

The degeneracy is a four-dimensional Yang–Mills statement. It should not be transported.

  • Three dimensions. $\mathcal N=3$ Chern–Simons–matter theories are genuine, well studied, and appear in higher-spin and holographic contexts. They are not four-dimensional SYM and the counting of supercharges is different.
  • Five and six dimensions. Extended supersymmetry has its own multiplet classification; the four-dimensional helicity argument that forces the $\mathcal N=3\to\mathcal N=4$ enhancement does not apply unchanged.

Whenever “$\mathcal N=3$” appears, the dimension and the Lagrangian/non-Lagrangian status must be checked first.

Common Misconceptions

  1. “$\mathcal N=3$ means three gaugini.” The CPT-completed vector multiplet has four, in the $\mathbf 3\oplus\mathbf 1$ of $SU(3)_R$. Three would not balance the eight bosonic degrees of freedom.
  2. “$\mathcal N=3$ sits between $\mathcal N=2$ and $\mathcal N=4$ as a separate Lagrangian theory.” The genuinely intermediate Lagrangian theory is $\mathcal N=2$; $\mathcal N=3$ collapses upward.
  3. “$\mathcal N=3$ has a nonzero beta function.” It vanishes, inherited from $\mathcal N=4$.
  4. “$\mathcal N=3$ means gauge group $SU(3)$.” $\mathcal N$ counts supersymmetries. $SU(3)$ with $\mathcal N=4$ is a perfectly ordinary theory.
  5. “Genuine $\mathcal N=3$ SCFTs cannot exist.” They exist; they are simply not Lagrangian.
