$n$ Super Yang–Mills: What the Letter Counts

Introduction

The question. The phrase “$n$ super Yang–Mills,” and its variant “$\mathcal N=n$ SYM,” carries two independent labels that look like one letter. This note separates them, gives the multiplet catalogue for $\mathcal N=1,2,3,4$, states the bound $\mathcal N\le4$, and explains why the four-dimensional series is exactly four entries long.

The answer in one line.

$$ \boxed{\; \mathcal N\ \text{(or }n\text{)}\ \text{counts supersymmetries}:\;4\mathcal N\ \text{real supercharges}, \qquad n\ \text{in }SU(n)\ \text{counts colour}:\;\text{independent input} \;} $$

What is claimed, and what is not. Claimed: the algebra and its central charges, the R-symmetry of each $\mathcal N$, the multiplet content, the bound $\mathcal N\le4$ from helicity counting, $a=c$ for $\mathcal N\ge3$, and the dimensional-reduction relations. Not claimed: that any particular gauge group is preferred, or that “maximal supersymmetry” implies a unique theory — the gauge group and the choice of global form remain free.

Two Letters, Two Labels

Label one: $\mathcal N$. The number of independent Weyl supercharges $Q_\alpha^{I}$, $I=1,\dots,\mathcal N$. Equivalently, $4\mathcal N$ real supercharges. This is the label that appears in “$\mathcal N=4$ SYM” and it is what the rest of this note counts.

Label two: $n$. The size parameter of the gauge group: $SU(n)$, $SO(n)$, $USp(2n)$. This is a free input at every $\mathcal N$.

Three ways the notation is abused.

  1. “$SU(4)$ super Yang–Mills” normally means gauge group $SU(4)$ with unspecified supersymmetry, not $\mathcal N=4$.
  2. “$\mathcal N=4$ $SU(4)$ SYM” has two $SU(4)$'s: the gauge group and the R-symmetry $SU(4)_R$. They are unrelated groups that happen to be isomorphic.
  3. “$n$ super Yang–Mills” may mean $\mathcal N=n$ or $SU(n)$; context decides, and the phrase should be rewritten when it is ambiguous.

The test. Replace “$SU(4)$” by “$SU(5)$” in the sentence. If the sentence still makes sense, the original referred to the gauge group; if it collapses, it referred to the R-symmetry.

The Extended Algebra

Without central charges,

$$ \{Q_\alpha^{I},\bar Q_{\dot\beta J}\} =2\sigma^{\mu}_{\alpha\dot\beta}P_\mu\,\delta^{I}{}_{J}, \qquad \{Q_\alpha^{I},Q_\beta^{J}\}=0, \qquad \{\bar Q_{\dot\alpha I},\bar Q_{\dot\beta J}\}=0 . $$

With central charges,

$$ \{Q_\alpha^{I},Q_\beta^{J}\}=\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}Z^{IJ}, \qquad Z^{IJ}=-Z^{JI}, \qquad [\bar Z_{IJ}]=\bar Z_{IJ}. $$

The central-charge matrix is antisymmetric, so its number of independent complex entries grows as $\mathcal N(\mathcal N-1)/2$:

$\mathcal N$ $Z^{IJ}$ entries rep under $SU(\mathcal N)_R$ BPS bound
1 0 — none in the gauge sector
2 1 singlet $M\ge\vert Z\vert$
3 3 $\bar{\mathbf 3}$ $M\ge$ largest eigenvalue
4 6 $\mathbf 6$ fractions $\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18$

For $\mathcal N=4$ the matrix $Z^{IJ}$ satisfies a reality condition that reduces the six complex entries to six real central charges in the standard massive representations; this is the statement that in $SO(6)_R$ language the central charge sits in the $\mathbf 6$ vector.

$R$-Symmetry: Algebraic versus Exact

The short answer to “does $\mathcal N=4$ mean an $SU(4)$ acts on the Lagrangian?” Yes — but it is a global internal symmetry, not the gauge group, and the group that acts on the quantum theory is $SU(4)_R$, not $U(4)_R$. In components the action is

$$ \lambda^{A}\longrightarrow U^{A}{}_{B}\,\lambda^{B}, \qquad X^{I}\longrightarrow O^{I}{}_{J}\,X^{J}, \qquad U\in SU(4),\quad O=\text{the }SO(6)\text{ image of }U . $$

What “acts on the field” has to mean, exactly. A field is a map from spacetime to a vector space of components, $\phi:\ x\mapsto\phi(x)\in V$. To say that a group “acts on the field” is to say that it acts on $V$ — on the component labels — and not on the base point $x$. This is a statement about slots, and the $\mathcal N=4$ gaugino has four of them:

$$ \lambda_{\alpha}{}^{aA}(x): \qquad \underbrace{\alpha}_{\text{spinor (Lorentz)}} \quad \underbrace{a}_{\text{colour (adjoint of }G)} \quad \underbrace{A}_{\text{R-symmetry }(\mathbf 4)} \quad \underbrace{x}_{\text{spacetime base}} . $$

The colour slot is acted on by $G$, the $R$ slot by $SU(4)_R$, both leaving $\alpha$ and $x$ alone. Neither of these is the same kind of statement as supersymmetry, which carries an index $\alpha$ and an index $A$ on the supercharge $Q_\alpha^{A}$ and therefore maps a field of spin $s$ to a different field of spin $s\pm\tfrac12$: supersymmetry relates distinct fields, it does not rotate the components of one field. So “$\mathcal N=4$ has an $SU(4)$ acting” is a statement about the $A$ slot only.

The diagnostic that settles whether it is gauged: look at the covariant derivative.

$$ D_\mu\lambda^{aA} =\partial_\mu\lambda^{aA} +g\,f^{abc}A_\mu^{b}\lambda^{cA} +\underbrace{0}_{\text{no }SU(4)_R\text{ term}} . $$

The first term is the plain gradient; the second is the colour connection, which mixes the $a$ slot; and there is no third term mixing the $A$ slot. Had $SU(4)_R$ been gauged there would be an $SU(4)_R$ gauge field $\mathcal A_\mu$ and a term $g_R f^{ABC}\mathcal A_\mu^{B}\lambda^{aC}$. There is none, in any normalization. Equivalently: a gauge transformation has an $x$-dependent parameter, so $\partial_\mu(U(x)\lambda)\neq U(x)\partial_\mu\lambda$ and the derivative must be covariantized; an $SU(4)_R$ transformation has a constant parameter, so $\partial_\mu(U\lambda)=U\,\partial_\mu\lambda$ and the derivative needs nothing. Global symmetry: no connection, no gauge field, no entry in the gradient. This is the whole content of the distinction, and it is why the $R$-symmetry never appears as a covariant-derivative term.

Two warnings, because the phrase invites both errors.

  1. The $R$-symmetry is not the gauge group. $\mathcal N=4$ SYM with gauge group $SU(4)$ has two $SU(4)$'s — the colour group, gauged, with a connection, acting on the $a$ slot; and $SU(4)_R$, global, with no connection, acting on the $A$ slot. They commute and are unrelated. Changing one leaves the other untouched.
  2. A gauged $SU(4)$ elsewhere is not evidence for this one. A theory whose action contains $D_\mu=\partial_\mu-igA_\mu$ with $A_\mu$ in $\mathfrak{su}(4)$, and a scalar transforming as $\Xi\mapsto U\Xi U^{\dagger}$ with $U$ local, has a gauged $SU(4)$ — a genuine connection, and Gauss laws. That object and $SU(4)_R$ are the same group in two entirely different roles. In $\mathcal N=4$ SYM the $R$-symmetry is not gauged; gauging it produces $\mathcal N=4$ supergravity, a different theory.

Invariance of the three terms of $\mathcal L_{\mathcal N=4}$ holds for a structural reason in each case:

  • Kinetic terms. $U$ and $O$ are constant and orthogonal/unitary, so $\delta_{AB}$ and $\delta_{IJ}$ are invariant tensors.
  • Yukawa. $\Gamma^{I}_{AB}\lambda^{A}[X^{I},\lambda^{B}]$ is invariant because $\Gamma^{I}_{AB}$ is the unique invariant tensor in $\mathbf 4\otimes\mathbf 4\otimes\mathbf 6$ (up to normalization). This uniqueness is why $\mathcal N=4$ admits exactly one Yukawa coupling.
  • Potential. $[X^{I},X^{J}]^{2}$ is contracted with $\delta_{IJ}$, hence $SO(6)$-invariant.

What the symmetry buys is rigidity, but not by itself: a scalar mass $m^{2}X^{I}X^{I}$ is $SO(6)$-invariant and is excluded only by supersymmetry; the gaugino mass term would need an antisymmetric $\epsilon_{AB}$, and $\lambda^{A}\lambda^{B}$ is symmetric in $A,B$, so it vanishes identically. The correct statement is that $SU(4)_R$ together with supersymmetry leaves one coupling.

Algebraic versus exact. The automorphism group of the $\mathcal N$-extended Poincaré algebra is $U(\mathcal N)_R$, with $Q$ in the $\mathbf N$. That is a statement about the algebra; it does not by itself produce a symmetry of any Lagrangian. What survives in the quantum theory is smaller, and the two columns must not be conflated. For each $\mathcal N$ the classical group, the exact group, and the mechanism that shrinks the abelian part are:

$\mathcal N$ algebraic: $\operatorname{Aut}$ of the Poincaré algebra exact in the quantum theory why the abelian part shrinks
1 $U(1)_R$ $\mathbb Z_{2N_c}$ in pure $SU(N_c)$ (then $\to\mathbb Z_2$ after condensation); $U(1)_R$ itself only in an SCFT mixed gauge–$R$ anomaly, $\partial_\mu j_R^{\mu}\propto F\tilde F$
2 $U(2)_R=SU(2)_R\times U(1)_R$ $SU(2)_R$ always; $U(1)_R$ only when $N_f=2N_c$ $U(1)_R$ anomalous otherwise, broken to $\mathbb Z_{4N_c}$
3 $U(3)_R$ $U(3)_R$ for genuine SCFTs; a Lagrangian theory is $\mathcal N=4$ —
4 $U(4)_R$ $SU(4)_R$ the extra $U(1)$ is anomalous and is not in $SU(2,2\vert 4)$

The organizing principle. In a superconformal theory the exact $R$-symmetry is the one that sits inside the superconformal algebra:

$$ SU(2,2|1)\supset U(1)_R,\quad SU(2,2|2)\supset U(1)_R\times SU(2)_R,\quad SU(2,2|3)\supset U(3)_R,\quad SU(2,2|4)\supset SU(4)_R . $$

For $\mathcal N=4$ the Poincaré algebra has a $U(4)_R$ automorphism, but $SU(2,2\vert 4)$ contains only $SU(4)_R$: the missing $U(1)$ is exactly the factor that does not survive. The same logic explains the $\mathcal N=1$ row: $U(1)_R$ is exact when the theory is conformal (it is the $R$-symmetry of $SU(2,2|1)$), and in an asymptotically free theory it is broken to a discrete subgroup by instantons.

What “anomalous” means here — and what it does not. The anomaly at issue is the mixed gauge–abelian one, $\partial_\mu j_R^{\mu}\propto F^{a}\tilde F^{a}$, which makes a would-be $U(1)_R$ rotation equivalent to a shift of $\theta$; it is this that breaks the abelian factor to $\mathbb Z_{2N_c}$ ($\mathcal N=1$) or $\mathbb Z_{4N_c}$ ($\mathcal N=2$). It is not a 't Hooft anomaly: a non-abelian $SU(4)_R$ has no such mixed gauge anomaly (the $\mathcal N=4$ gauge theory is vector-like, so the gauge anomaly vanishes), and a 't Hooft anomaly in a global symmetry would not break the symmetry anyway — it would only obstruct gauging it. So $SU(4)_R$ is exact, the current is conserved, and the symmetry organizes the spectrum; the $U(1)$ is not exact, and the discrete survivor then acts and is spontaneously broken by condensation.

Two further consequences deserve emphasis.

  • In $\mathcal N=4$ the exact R-symmetry is $SU(4)_R\cong SO(6)_R$, which is why the ten-dimensional origin is manifest: the six scalars are the $SO(6)$ vector.
  • In $\mathcal N=3$ a genuine SCFT keeps the full $U(3)_R$; the Lagrangian theory does not, because it is $\mathcal N=4$ and its R-symmetry is $SU(4)_R\supset U(3)_R$.

The Multiplet Catalogue

Vector multiplet (adjoint, universal gauge multiplet):

$\mathcal N$ gaugini real scalars scalars as $SU(\mathcal N)_R$ rep
1 1 0 —
2 2 2 $\mathbf 1$ (one complex)
3 4 6 $\mathbf 3\oplus\bar{\mathbf 3}$
4 4 6 $\mathbf 6$

The $\mathcal N=3$ and $\mathcal N=4$ rows coincide as field content; the difference is the R-symmetry and the number of supercharges.

Matter multiplets:

  • $\mathcal N=1$: chiral $(\phi,\psi)$, one complex scalar plus one Weyl fermion, any representation.
  • $\mathcal N=2$: hypermultiplet, four real scalars plus two Weyl fermions, real or pseudoreal representation (or a conjugate pair); in $\mathcal N=1$ language a pair $Q,\tilde Q$.
  • $\mathcal N=3,4$: strongly restricted; the pure $\mathcal N=4$ theory has only the vector multiplet, and adding matter typically spoils conformality unless the content is chosen to cancel the beta function.

The pattern. As $\mathcal N$ grows the multiplets grow and the allowed interactions shrink. This is the trade-off behind the standard division of labour: $\mathcal N=1$ for model building, $\mathcal N=2$ for exact low-energy solutions, $\mathcal N=4$ for exact conformal and holographic statements.

Why the Series Stops at $\mathcal N=4$

Start from $|h=1\rangle$ and act with $k$ supercharges: the state at helicity $1-k/2$ has multiplicity $\binom{\mathcal N}{k}$. CPT requires the conjugate tower built on $|h=-1\rangle$, whose highest state is

$$ h=-1+\frac{\mathcal N}{2}. $$

For $\mathcal N=5$ this is $+\tfrac32$: a massless spin-$\tfrac32$ field. A consistent interacting theory containing one requires local supersymmetry, hence supergravity. Therefore rigid SYM in four dimensions has

$$ \mathcal N\le4, \qquad \mathcal N=4\ \text{being maximal}. $$

For $\mathcal N=4$ the top of the conjugate tower is $h=+1$, the gauge boson, so the tower closes on itself with no gravitino. $\mathcal N=8$ supergravity is the gravitational analogue of $\mathcal N=4$ SYM and is not a gauge theory.

The same bound as a supercharge count. Sixteen supercharges is the maximum for a non-gravitational theory in four dimensions; sixteen is also the count in $D=10$ $\mathcal N=1$, $D=6$ $\mathcal N=(1,1)$, $D=5$ $\mathcal N=2$, and $D=3$ $\mathcal N=8$.

Central Charges, BPS, and Exact Masses

The bound $M\ge\vert Z\vert$ holds in every massive representation of an extended algebra. Saturating it defines BPS states, whose masses are protected.

  • $\mathcal N=2$: one central charge, $Z=n_e a+n_m a_D$, with $a,a_D$ the Seiberg–Witten periods. The exact BPS masses are the main predictive content of the solution.
  • $\mathcal N=4$: six central charges, BPS fractions $\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18$, and S-duality acting on the charge lattice.
  • $\mathcal N=1$: no central charges in the pure gauge sector. BPS-like objects (domain walls) exist but are protected by a different mechanism, namely the holomorphic superpotential and the discrete chiral symmetry.

Superconformal Symmetry and the Anomalies

For a superconformal theory the algebra is $SU(2,2|\mathcal N)$. The trace anomalies behave as follows.

$\mathcal N$ $a$ versus $c$ comment
1 generally $a\neq c$ fixed by R-charges via the anomaly formula
2 generally $a\neq c$ fixed by matter content
3 $a=c$ hallmark of $\mathcal N\ge3$
4 $a=c=(N^{2}-1)/4$ for $SU(N)$ maximal case

The equality for $\mathcal N\ge3$ is one of the sharpest distinctions between the $\mathcal N=2$ and $\mathcal N=4$ worlds, and it is the quickest way to tell a genuine $\mathcal N=3$ SCFT from an $\mathcal N=2$ one.

Dualities Across $\mathcal N$

$\mathcal N$ duality what it is
1 Seiberg duality infrared duality between two different $SU$ theories
2 Seiberg–Witten $SL(2,\mathbb Z)$ exact action on the period vector, Coulomb branch
3 none of its own the Lagrangian theory is $\mathcal N=4$
4 Montonen–Olive $S$-duality conjectured exact strong–weak duality of the full theory

The pattern is monotone: more supersymmetry means more exact control, and the dualities become stronger statements — from an IR equivalence ($\mathcal N=1$) to a low-energy symmetry ($\mathcal N=2$) to a conjectured symmetry of the full theory ($\mathcal N=4$).

$\mathcal N=4$ as a parent. Deformations produce much of the landscape:

  • $\mathcal N=2^{*}$: give the adjoint hypermultiplet a mass; $m=0$ is $\mathcal N=4$, $m\to\infty$ is pure $\mathcal N=2$.
  • $\mathcal N=1$ deformations: add a superpotential for the three adjoint chirals.
  • Orbifolds and quivers: quotient by a discrete symmetry.
  • S-folds: twist by an S-duality to obtain genuine $\mathcal N=3$ SCFTs.

Gauge-Group Independence

For every Lie group $G$ one can write $\mathcal N=1,2,4$ SYM with gauge group $G$; $\mathcal N=3$ follows only as $\mathcal N=4$. The statements that depend on $G$ are the detailed dynamics, the curves (for $\mathcal N=2$), and the Langlands dual in S-duality; the statements that do not are the multiplet content, the algebra, and the R-symmetry.

The two $SU(4)$'s, stated once. $\mathcal N=4$ SYM with gauge group $SU(4)$ is a theory whose gauge group is $SU(4)$ and whose R-symmetry is $SU(4)_R$. Both are $SU(4)$; neither is the other. Changing the gauge group to $SU(2)$ leaves the R-symmetry untouched; changing $\mathcal N$ from 4 to 1 changes the R-symmetry to $U(1)_R$ and leaves a gauge $SU(4)$ untouched.
