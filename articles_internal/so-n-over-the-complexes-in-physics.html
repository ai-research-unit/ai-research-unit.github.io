The Special Orthogonal Groups over the Complexes in Physics: Analytic Continuation, Chirality and the Nilpotent

The Plan

One complex group, two real groups. The companion article so-n-over-the-complexes.md walks $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})$ up the dimensions. This article asks what the complex rung is for in physics, and the answer is sharper than for the real ladder: the complex group is the object that contains both the Euclidean and the Lorentzian theory, and every analytic continuation in physics is a path inside it.

The single sentence. Physics computes in Euclidean signature and interprets in Lorentzian signature, and the passage between them is the choice of a real form inside one complex group. The heat kernel, the instanton, the lattice, and the path integral's convergence all live on the Euclidean side; the S-matrix, the light cone, and the causal structure all live on the Lorentzian side; and $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})$ is the group that has both, so every formula that survives the passage is a holomorphic formula on the complex group.

The three methods the complex group serves. First, Wick rotation: the substitution $t\mapsto-it$ turns the Lorentz group into the orthogonal group and the oscillatory path integral into a convergent one. The verification shows the mechanism at the bottom rung: a rotation through an imaginary angle is a boost, $A(i\zeta)=D\,B(\zeta)\,D^{-1}$ with $D=\operatorname{diag}(1,i)$, exactly, to $0$. Second, analytic continuation in a parameter: the angular momentum, the energy, the coupling and the dimension are all continued into the complex plane, and the singularities of the continuation are the physics — Regge poles, resonances, exceptional points, critical exponents. Third, holomorphic extension of the field: the Dirac spinor, the gauge connection and the metric are extended to complex values, and the reality conditions are imposed at the end. Twistors, the spinor-helicity formalism, the self-dual connection of canonical gravity, and the complexified instanton are all of this kind.

The upper rungs serve the same three methods. Dimension five gives the complexified de Sitter coset and a holomorphic Higgs potential. Dimension six gives the complexified conformal group $\mathrm{SL}(4,\mathbb{C})$ and the twistor space $\mathbb{C}^{4}$. Dimension seven gives the complex $G_{2}$ and the complex structure moduli of an internal manifold. Dimension eight gives three complex eights with triality acting on them, which is what the complexified spinor-helicity formalism uses. In every case the complexification removes a reality condition and the physics is what remains when the condition is put back.

What is gained and what is lost. The complex group gains the nilpotent elements, and therefore the massless case, the degenerate limit, the resonance and the exceptional point. It loses compactness, and therefore the discreteness of the spectrum; the representation theory becomes infinite-dimensional and the unitary structures must be reimposed by hand as real forms and reality conditions. The verification makes the first point concrete: the stabiliser of a null momentum inside $\mathfrak{so}(3,1)$ is three-dimensional with a two-dimensional abelian derived algebra whose elements are nilpotent of index three, and their exponentials are unipotent and fix the momentum. There is no such element in the compact real form.

Why Complexify

The path integral. The weight is $e^{iS/\hbar}$ in Lorentzian signature and $e^{-S_{E}/\hbar}$ in Euclidean signature, and only the second is a probability measure. Every nonperturbative computation — instantons, lattice QCD, the heat kernel, the index theorem — is done in the second. The rotation that connects them is a rotation of the time coordinate through $-\pi/2$, and the group that contains both the Euclidean and the Lorentzian rotation is the complex orthogonal group. The corpus records the interpretation in Interpretation of the Wick Rotation in the Biquaternion Universe.

The analytic S-matrix. An amplitude is an analytic function of the Mandelstam variables, with branch points at the thresholds and poles at the bound states and the resonances. The analyticity is not an assumption added to the theory; it follows from the causal structure and the spectrum condition, and it is the content of the dispersion relations. So the physically meaningful object is a holomorphic function, and the complex group is the symmetry of the holomorphic object.

The coupling. The gauge coupling and the $\theta$ angle combine into one complex number,

$$ \tau=\frac{\theta}{2\pi}+\frac{4\pi i}{g^{2}}, $$

which lives in the upper half plane, and the duality of the theory acts on it by a subgroup of $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$. The complexification of the coupling is the statement that the $\theta$ term and the $1/g^{2}$ term are the real and imaginary parts of one holomorphic object. The same structure appears in the complexified metric of the self-dual sector of gravity, and in the complexified moduli of a string compactification.

$\mathrm{SO}(1,\mathbb{C})$: The Trivial Complex Rung

The group is trivial and the complexification adds nothing. $\mathrm{SO}(1,\mathbb{C})=\{1\}$, so the first rung is the same over $\mathbb{C}$ as over $\mathbb{R}$, and it carries the same physics: a scalar, with no internal rotation. The complexification of a scalar field theory is the statement that the field takes values in $\mathbb{C}$ rather than $\mathbb{R}$, which is a $\mathrm U(1)$ of phase and not an orthogonal group.

Where the complex scalar matters. A complex scalar carries a conserved charge, and its quanta and its antiparticles are distinct. The Goldstone mode of a broken global symmetry is a real scalar; the Higgs field before breaking is a complex scalar doublet; the axion is a real scalar whose complexification is the Peccei–Quinn field. The rung is trivial; what the field does with the complex numbers is not.

$\mathrm{SO}(2,\mathbb{C})$: The Complexified Phase

Rotation and Boost in One Group

The group is $\mathbb{C}^{\times}$. $\mathrm{SO}(2,\mathbb{C})$ consists of the matrices

$$ A(z)=\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\ b&a\end{pmatrix}, \qquad a=\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2},\quad b=\frac{z-z^{-1}}{2i},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}, $$

and the map $A(z)\leftrightarrow z$ is an isomorphism. So

$$ \mathrm{SO}(2,\mathbb{C})\cong\mathbb{C}^{\times}\cong S^{1}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}, $$

the last factor by the polar decomposition $z=e^{i\theta}\rho$.

The two real forms are the two physical readings. The real points $|z|=1$ are the rotations, which is $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ and the phase of quantum mechanics. The positive real points $z=e^{\zeta}$ are a different real form, the group of hyperbolic rotations, which is the Lorentz group of $1+1$ dimensions,

$$ \mathrm{SO}(1,1)=\Bigl\{\begin{pmatrix}\cosh\zeta&\sinh\zeta\\ \sinh\zeta&\cosh\zeta\end{pmatrix}\Bigr\}, $$

with $\zeta$ the rapidity. The two are not subgroups of one another in the real matrix representation; they are related by conjugation with $D=\operatorname{diag}(1,i)$, which is the Wick rotation:

$$ A(i\zeta)=D\,B(\zeta)\,D^{-1}. $$

The verification confirms this identity exactly, to $0$, for three rapidities, and confirms that $A(i\zeta)$ is complex orthogonal to $3\times10^{-15}$.

The physics of the identity. An angle is a rapidity times $i$. A rotation and a boost are the same operation continued to different values of one complex parameter, and the analytic structure of that parameter is the structure of the Lorentz group in two dimensions. The boost parameter is unbounded, so the non-compactness of the Lorentz group is the non-compactness of $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ inside $\mathbb{C}^{\times}$; the compact rotation angle is periodic, so the compactness of $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ is the compactness of $S^{1}$. The two sit inside one complex group, and the difference in their physics is the difference in which real slice is taken. The corpus records the relativistic reading in The Two-Sheeted Cover and the Topology of Boosts in Biquaternionic Form.

Why it matters beyond two dimensions. The same continuation, applied to the four-dimensional group, turns $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ into $\mathrm{SO}(3,1)$ and the instanton into the Lorentzian solution; applied to the momentum, it turns the Euclidean propagator into the Feynman propagator with the $i\epsilon$ prescription; applied to the time coordinate of a lattice, it turns a statistical mechanics problem into a quantum one. The bottom rung is where the mechanism is visible without indices.

The Complexified Phase in Circuits and Media

Phasors. An AC circuit is analysed by writing every voltage and current as a complex amplitude and every impedance as a complex number. The rotation of the phase by $\pi/2$ between the voltage across an inductor and the current through it is the multiplication by $i$, and the power factor is the cosine of the $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ angle between them. The method is exactly the isomorphism $\mathrm{SO}(2,\mathbb{C})\cong\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ applied to a linear response.

Complex permittivity. A dielectric with loss has a complex permittivity $\epsilon=\epsilon'+i\epsilon''$, and the imaginary part is the absorption. The complex refractive index $n+i\kappa$ gives both the phase velocity and the attenuation, and the Kramers–Kronig relations tie the real and imaginary parts because the response function is holomorphic in the upper half plane and vanishes at infinity. The whole of dielectric spectroscopy, of ellipsometry, and of the design of a metamaterial is the use of a holomorphic response function, which is a complexification and not a convenience.

Complex modulus. A viscoelastic material is described by $G^{*}=G'+iG''$, the storage and loss moduli, and the measurement is a phase lag of a driven oscillation. The mathematics is the same $\mathrm{SO}(2,\mathbb{C})$ as the circuit, applied to strain instead of current.

$\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{C})$: Complex Angular Momentum

The Analytic Continuation in $l$

The partial wave expansion is a sum over this rung. A two-body scattering amplitude is expanded in partial waves,

$$ f(k,\theta)=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)\,a_{l}(k)\,P_{l}(\cos\theta), $$

and each term is a representation of the rotation group. The sum is over the integer $l$, because the physical angular momentum is quantised.

The continuation. Regge's observation is that $a_{l}(k)$ is the restriction to integer $l$ of a function $a(l,k)$ that is analytic in $l$ except for poles, and the poles move with the energy: $l=\alpha(t)$. The Sommerfeld–Watson transform replaces the sum by a contour integral in the complex $l$ plane, and the high-energy behaviour of the amplitude is governed by the rightmost pole,

$$ f(s,t)\ \sim\ s^{\alpha(t)}, $$

so the power of $s$ is the position of a singularity in the complex angular momentum plane. The group that makes the continuation natural is $\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{C})$, the complexification of the rotation group: the representations of the real form are labelled by integers or half-integers, and the representations of the complex form are labelled by a continuous complex parameter.

The physics built on it. The Regge trajectories are the spectroscopy of the hadrons: the mesons lie on nearly linear trajectories $\alpha(t)=\alpha_{0}+\alpha't$ with the same slope for all trajectories, and the slope is the string tension in the dual string picture. The Pomeron is the trajectory with the quantum numbers of the vacuum that gives the nearly constant total cross-section. The Veneziano amplitude is the Euler beta function whose poles in $s$ and $t$ are exactly the Regge poles, and it is the ancestor of string theory. The corpus records the hadronic side in SU(3) Representations and the Gell-Mann–Okubo Mass Formula in Biquaternionic Form.

The mathematical statement. The characters of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ are Chebyshev polynomials of $\cos(\theta/2)$; the continuation in $j$ replaces $\cos\theta$ by a complex variable and the character becomes a function with branch points where the two eigenvalues collide. The branch point is the degeneracy of the two weights, and it is the same degeneracy that produces the square-root behaviour of the exceptional points of the last section. Complex angular momentum is the statement that a representation label can be continued until two weights meet.

$\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{C})$ in Gravity

The self-dual connection. In the Ashtekar formulation the phase space of general relativity is the space of connections for the complex group $\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{C})$, and the metric is recovered from the soldering form. The real theory is a real section of the complex one, and the reality conditions are imposed after the canonical analysis. The reason for the complexification is the same as at $\mathrm{SO}(4)$: the self-dual part of the curvature is the part that solves the constraints, and the self-dual projection is complex.

The Lorentzian and Euclidean sections. The complex connection has two real sections, one for each signature, and a solution of the complex constraints gives a Euclidean solution in one section and a Lorentzian solution in the other. The instanton and the Lorentzian metric with the same self-dual curvature are one holomorphic object, and the corpus records the Euclidean side in Linearized Gravity in Biquaternionic Form.

$\mathrm{SO}(4,\mathbb{C})$: Chirality

Two Real Forms and One Complex Group

The real forms. $\mathrm{SO}(4,\mathbb{C})$ has three real forms that physics uses: the compact $\mathrm{SO}(4)$, the split $\mathrm{SO}(3,1)$, and the split $\mathrm{SO}(2,2)$. The first is the Euclidean rotation group of the lattice and the instanton. The second is the Lorentz group. The third is the conformal group of the plane, the isometry group of three-dimensional anti-de Sitter space, and the symmetry of the two-dimensional conformal field theory whose algebra is two copies of the Virasoro algebra.

The Hodge star decides which one is which. On two-forms the Hodge star squares to $+1$ in Euclidean signature and to $-1$ in Lorentzian signature. The verification computes both and finds $\star^{2}=+1$ with eigenvalues $\pm1$ three times each in the first case, and $\star^{2}=-1$ with eigenvalues $\pm i$ three times each in the second. So:

$$ \text{Euclidean: } \mathfrak{so}(4)=\mathfrak{so}(3)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(3)\ \text{(real ideals)}, \qquad \text{Lorentzian: } \mathfrak{so}(3,1)_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\oplus\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\ \text{(complex ideals)}. $$

The consequence. In Euclidean signature the self-dual projection is real, so the split of the algebra is a split of the group and the two chiralities are two independent real subgroups. In Lorentzian signature the self-dual projection is complex, so the split exists only after complexification and the two chiralities are complex conjugate. That single sign is the reason the Euclidean theory has self-dual and anti-self-dual fields that can be treated independently, while the Lorentzian theory has one field whose two chiral projections are tied by the reality condition. The verification confirms the complex statement numerically: the projectors $P_{\pm}=\frac12(1\mp i\star)$ onto the $\pm i$ eigenspaces are self-adjoint idempotents of rank three, the bracket of two elements of the $+i$ eigenspace stays in it to $4.4\times10^{-16}$, and the bracket of a $+i$ with a $-i$ element lands in the $-i$ eigenspace to $2.3\times10^{-16}$.

Chirality and the Weyl Spinor

The decomposition. $\mathrm{SO}(4,\mathbb{C})\cong(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\times\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))/\mathbb{Z}_{2}$. The Dirac spinor is the direct sum of the two defining representations,

$$ \text{Dirac}=\Bigl(\tfrac12,0\Bigr)\oplus\Bigl(0,\tfrac12\Bigr), \qquad 2+2=4, $$

and the two summands are the two chiralities. Each is acted on by one of the two $\mathrm{SL}_{2}$ factors and is annihilated by the other, and the verification confirms the stronger structural statement: for a generator in the self-dual part of the algebra, the spinor representative satisfies $P_{L}\,S\,P_{R}=0$ exactly, so it acts on one chiral half only.

The verification of the Lorentz action. The spinor representative is $S(\omega)=\exp(\frac{i}{4}\omega_{\mu\nu}\sigma^{\mu\nu})$, and the verification confirms on forty random $\omega$ that $S\gamma^{\mu}S^{-1}=\Lambda^{\mu}{}_{\nu}\gamma^{\nu}$ at $9\times10^{-15}$. The same check confirms $\gamma_{5}^{2}=1$, $\{\gamma_{5},\gamma^{\mu}\}=0$, $\operatorname{tr}\gamma_{5}=0$ and that $P_{L}$ has rank two. So the chirality is a genuine decomposition of the four-dimensional spinor space, and the two halves have complex dimension two.

The mass term is what couples them. A Dirac mass is $m\bar\psi\psi=m(\bar\psi_{L}\psi_{R}+\bar\psi_{R}\psi_{L})$, and it is the only renormalisable term that mixes the two chiralities. So a massless fermion has two independent chiral fields and a massive one has one Dirac field, and the statement is a statement about which real form of $\mathrm{SO}(4,\mathbb{C})$ is being used: the massless theory is at the boundary of the complexified moduli where the two chiralities decouple. The corpus records the decomposition in Exercise: Chirality and the Weyl Spinors, Chiral Fermions in the Biquaternion Framework, and The Neutrino and Majorana Fermions in Biquaternionic Form.

Majorana is a reality condition. A Majorana spinor is a Weyl spinor together with an antilinear map $J$ that commutes with the Lorentz action and satisfies $J^{2}=1$; the existence of such a map is a property of the real form and of the dimension, and it is the statement that the representation is quaternionic or real rather than complex. The classification is periodic in $n$ with period eight, and the corpus records the physics in The Majorana Equation in Biquaternionic Form and the discrete symmetries in Parra's Four Options of the Dirac Equation and the Discrete Symmetries.

The Massless Momentum Is a Spinor

The factorisation. For a momentum $p$, the matrix $p_{\mu}\sigma^{\mu}$ (with $\sigma^{0}=1$) has determinant $p_{0}^{2}-|\mathbf p|^{2}$. The verification confirms on fifty random null momenta that the determinant vanishes to $1.5\times10^{-15}$, on fifty massive momenta that it equals $m^{2}$ to $8.9\times10^{-16}$, and that at a null momentum the matrix has rank one and factors exactly as

$$ p_{\mu}\sigma^{\mu}=\lambda\lambda^{\dagger}, \qquad \lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{2}, $$

with the factorisation exact to $0$ on the example $p=(1,0,0,1)$.

Why this is the heart of the complex rung. A null momentum is a rank-one Hermitian matrix, so it is a spinor times its conjugate, and the spinor $\lambda$ is the fundamental object. The little-group phase $\lambda\mapsto e^{i\varphi}\lambda$ is the $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ helicity phase of the real article, so the two rungs meet here: the complexified spinor carries the helicity phase of the massless particle. The formalism built on the factorisation — spinor helicity, the BCFW recursion, the scattering amplitudes of the modern era — is a formalism in which the external data of a massless particle are two spinors and not a vector. The corpus records the structure in Helicity in Biquaternionic Form and The Celestial Sphere of the Null Cone: Null Directions, Chirality and the Phase.

The geometric reading. A null complex momentum is a point of the complexified light cone, and the complexified light cone is a quadric in $\mathbb{C}^{4}$ whose two rulings are the two spinor spaces. The twistor correspondence is the statement that a point of complexified Minkowski space corresponds to a projective line in the twistor space $\mathbb{CP}^{3}$, and that a solution of the self-dual Yang–Mills equation corresponds to a holomorphic vector bundle on that space. The instanton is the case of the correspondence that survives the reality conditions. The corpus records the physics in Twistor Theory and Biquaternions.

The Nilpotent and the Massless Little Group

The statement. The little group of a massless particle of momentum $p$ is the subgroup of the Lorentz group fixing $p$, and it is the semidirect product $\mathrm{SO}(2)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{2}$. The translations are not rotations: they are null rotations, and their generators are nilpotent.

The verification. Taking $p=(1,0,0,1)$, the algebra of generators annihilating $p$ is three-dimensional. Its derived algebra is two-dimensional and abelian, with the bracket of the two generators equal to $5.6\times10^{-17}$. A generic element $X$ of the derived algebra satisfies $X^{2}\ne0$ and $X^{3}=0$, so it is nilpotent of index three; its exponential is the polynomial $\mathrm I+X+\frac12X^{2}$, exact to $0$; that exponential lies in $\mathrm{SO}(3,1)$ to $2.2\times10^{-16}$, fixes the momentum to $2.2\times10^{-16}$, and has all eigenvalues equal to $1$ to $6\times10^{-6}$, so it is unipotent. The bracket of the helicity generator with a translation lands back in the derived algebra to $1.3\times10^{-16}$.

What this says. The massless case is the degenerate case, and the degeneracy is the nilpotent. In the compact rung there is no nilpotent element: a compact Lie algebra is a direct sum of simple algebras and a centre, and every element is semisimple. So a description that insists on compactness cannot describe a massless particle's little group, and the massless particle is precisely where the complexification is unavoidable. This is the deepest reason the complex rung belongs in physics: the lightlike direction is not a limit of the timelike direction that can be taken inside the compact group.

The same nilpotent in another costume. The exceptional point of a non-Hermitian system is a coalescence of two eigenvalues at which the matrix becomes non-diagonalisable, i.e. acquires a Jordan block, i.e. acquires a nilpotent part. The verification confirms the defining behaviour: the two-level matrix $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\ \epsilon&0\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $\pm\sqrt{\epsilon}$, so the splitting grows as the square root of the perturbation and not linearly. The square root is the branch point of the complex square root, which is the same degeneracy of two eigenvalues that the Regge continuation meets and that the massless little group exhibits. Exceptional points are now engineered on purpose in photonic, acoustic and microwave systems, where the $\sqrt{\epsilon}$ response is used to make a sensor more sensitive than a linear one.

$\mathrm{SO}(5,\mathbb{C})$: The Analytic Continuation of the Coset

Complexified Momenta and the Holomorphic Potential

Why the complexification is used. Scattering amplitudes are analytic functions of the momenta, and the analytic continuation takes the Lorentz group to its complexification. A complexified rotation group is what acts on complexified momenta, and this rung is the complexification of the Euclidean de Sitter group of the real companion. The coset

$$ \mathrm{SO}(5,\mathbb{C})/\mathrm{SO}(4,\mathbb{C})=S^{4}_{\mathbb{C}}, \qquad \dim_{\mathbb{C}}=10-6=4, $$

is the complexified four-sphere, and it is the space on which a holomorphic Higgs potential is a holomorphic function. The four Goldstone fields of the real coset become four complex fields, and the potential that is a function on the real sphere becomes a holomorphic function on the complex one.

What survives and what does not. The dimension survives, the rank survives, and the quotient survives. Unitarity does not: a representation of a complex group is not unitary unless it is trivial, so the "states" of a complexified theory are not Hilbert-space states. The complexified rung is a tool for continuing a computation, not a symmetry of a physical spectrum.

The Symplectic Name over $\mathbb{C}$

$\mathrm{SO}(5,\mathbb{C})\cong\mathrm{Sp}_{4}(\mathbb{C})$ up to the centre, so the complexified composite-Higgs coset is also a coset of a complex symplectic group. A model builder who continues the $\mathrm{SO}(5)$ construction to complex coupling does not need a new group theory: the two names are one algebra, and the identity holds over $\mathbb{C}$ for the same reason as over $\mathbb{R}$, namely that in rank two the two orbits of roots have the same size.

$\mathrm{SO}(6,\mathbb{C})$: Twistors

The Complexified Conformal Group

The statement. The complexified conformal group of four-dimensional Minkowski space is $\mathrm{SL}(4,\mathbb{C})$, and this rung is that group up to its centre:

$$ \mathrm{SO}(6,\mathbb{C})\cong\mathrm{SL}(4,\mathbb{C}), \qquad \mathrm{SO}(6,\mathbb{C})=\mathrm{PSL}(4,\mathbb{C}). $$

Twistor space is $\mathbb{C}^{4}$ with the group acting on it, and the incidence between a twistor and a complexified point of Minkowski space is the statement that a point is a two-plane. The six-dimensional rotation group acts on the four-dimensional twistor space, and the isomorphism is the reason the conformal group of four-dimensional space is a group of $4\times4$ matrices of determinant one.

Why the six, and not the four. The six is $\Lambda^{2}\mathbb{C}^{4}$, the bivectors of the twistor space, and the conformal metric of Minkowski space is the symmetric form on those bivectors. So the "six-dimensional rotation" of the mathematics article is a rotation of the bivectors of a four-dimensional complex vector space, and the physics name for that rotation is the conformal group.

The Complexified R-Symmetry

The $\mathcal N=4$ R-symmetry of the real companion is $\mathrm{SU}(4)$, and its complexification is this rung. So the holomorphic continuation of the $\mathcal N=4$ theory has R-symmetry $\mathrm{SL}(4,\mathbb{C})$, and the six scalars are the six of the complexified group. The statement is exact at the level of algebras and fails at the level of real forms: the compact R-symmetry of the physical theory is a real form of the complex one, and the two differ by the reality condition that makes the representation unitary.

$\mathrm{SO}(7,\mathbb{C})$: The Complex $G_2$

Complexified Exceptional Holonomy

The group. $G_{2}(\mathbb{C})$ is the stabiliser of the complex three-form, of complex dimension $14$ against $21$, and it sits inside $\mathrm{SO}(7,\mathbb{C})$ exactly as the compact $G_{2}$ sits inside $\mathrm{SO}(7)$. A complex seven-manifold with a holomorphic three-form whose stabiliser is $G_{2}(\mathbb{C})$ is the complexification of a $G_{2}$ manifold, and its moduli are the complex deformations of that form.

What the physics does with it. Compactification is a real procedure: it needs a real metric of the right signature and a real holonomy group, so the complexified rung is not the symmetry of a compactification. What it is useful for is the algebraic geometry of the internal manifold: the periods, the complex structure moduli, and the counting of the massless fields are computed on the complexified object and then restricted to the real slice.

No Second Name over $\mathbb{C}$ Either

The algebra is simple of type $B_3$, and it is not $\mathfrak{sp}_{6}(\mathbb{C})$ although both have dimension $21$, rank $3$ and eighteen roots. The test of the mathematics article applies without change: the number of long roots is twelve on one side and six on the other, and an isometry of root systems preserves that number. So the complexification does not create a second name where the real form had none.

$\mathrm{SO}(8,\mathbb{C})$: Triality and Complexified Spinors

The Complexified Transverse Group

The eight. The transverse little group of the ten-dimensional superstring is $\mathrm{SO}(8)$, and the complexified version is this rung. In the spinor-helicity formalism the momenta are taken complex, the little group becomes complex, and the three eights of triality become three complex vector spaces of dimension eight with no reality condition relating them. The consequence is a simplification: over $\mathbb{R}$ the relation between the two half-spinors is a reality statement that has to be checked, and over $\mathbb{C}$ the three representations are simply permuted.

What triality does to the spectrum. The massless spectrum is a vector and one spinor, and triality is the statement that the labels can be exchanged. Over $\mathbb{C}$ the exchange is an automorphism of the complex group, so a complexified amplitude written with the vector external state and one written with the spinor external state are the same amplitude relabelled. Over $\mathbb{R}$ only the $\mathbb{Z}/2$ part of the exchange survives on the quotient $\mathrm{SO}(8)$, which is why the physical statement is about the two chiralities and not about all three eights.

The Five Real Forms and the Physical Slice

The complex rung contains the five signatures $\mathrm{SO}(8)$, $\mathrm{SO}(7,1)$, $\mathrm{SO}(6,2)$, $\mathrm{SO}(5,3)$ and $\mathrm{SO}(4,4)$. Only the first acts on the positive-definite transverse space of the light-cone quantisation; the others appear after Wick rotation or in the isometry algebras of the higher-dimensional backgrounds. Choosing a real form is choosing which of these is the physical one, and the complex group is the statement that the choice is data and not structure.

$\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})$: The Complexified Ladder

Complex Dimension

Dimensional regularisation. The dimension $d$ is continued to $d=4-2\epsilon$ with $\epsilon$ complex, and the loop integrals are computed as functions of $d$ and then continued back. The group theory of the continued theory is the group theory of $\mathrm{SO}(d,\mathbb{C})$, and the tensors — the metric, the gamma matrices, the antisymmetric symbols — are continued algebraically. The anomalous dimensions are the poles at $\epsilon=0$, so the physics of the renormalisation group is the pole structure of a holomorphic function of the dimension. The corpus records the method in The Renormalization Group in Biquaternionic Form.

Why the continuation is legitimate. The rotation group of $d$ dimensions is defined by the same equations for every $d$, and the invariants are polynomial in $d$, so the continuation is the continuation of a rational function. The one place where it is subtle is the antisymmetric symbol, which exists only in integer dimension; the resolution is to continue the products of two symbols, which is where the evanescent operators and the 't Hooft–Veltman versus dimensional-reduction schemes come from.

The Complexified Spinor

The spinor dimension is $2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$ and the verification constructs the gamma matrices to $n=11$. The complexified spinor is the same object with the reality condition dropped, so the spinor of $\mathrm{SO}(n,\mathbb{C})$ is the Dirac spinor and the Weyl and Majorana conditions are the two ways of imposing a real structure on it. The taxonomy of real structures is the taxonomy of the real forms of the spin representation, and its periodicity of eight is the Bott periodicity of the real Clifford algebras. The corpus records the ladder of spinor carriers in The Carriers, the Spin and the Closure and Higher Spin from Tensor Products: The Three Carriers and the Absence of a Fourth.

The spinor as a square root. The reason the spinor is a complex object with a power-of-two dimension is that the Clifford algebra is the square root of the exterior algebra, and the square root exists over $\mathbb{C}$ without a choice of sign. The Dirac operator is the square root of the Laplacian for the same reason, and the corpus records that identity in Klein–Gordon from the Dirac Square in Biquaternionic Form. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the square root exists only in the dimensions where a real structure exists, and that is the content of the period-eight table.

Non-Compactness and the Continuum

The physics of the lost compactness. A compact group has a discrete spectrum of representations and a discrete spectrum of Casimirs, which is why the angular momentum is quantised. The complex group has neither, which is why the analytically continued angular momentum is continuous, why the continued dimension gives a continuous $\epsilon$, and why the continued energy gives a resonance with a complex position. The physical reading is that the discrete quantum numbers are the compact directions of the real form and the continuum is the non-compact direction of the complexification.

The resonance. An unstable particle is a pole of the S-matrix at a complex value of the energy, $E=M-\frac{i}{2}\Gamma$, below the real axis on the second sheet. The pole is not an element of the physical Hilbert space, and its interpretation requires the analytic continuation in which the energy is allowed to leave the real axis. The complexification of the group is the same move at the level of the symmetry: the physical group is a real form and the continued group is the complex one. The corpus records the physics in The Unstable State and the Complex Energy Pole in Biquaternionic Form.

The continuum and its cost. The price of the complexification is that the Hilbert space becomes a Krein space with an indefinite inner product, and unitarity becomes a statement about a real form and a positivity condition. Every complexified formalism pays this price: the self-dual connection of gravity needs reality conditions, the twistor space needs a reality structure, the optical potential needs a physical sheet. The bookkeeping of these conditions is the technical content of the subject.

The Complexified Gauge Group and Its Instanton

The instanton over $\mathbb{C}$. The BPST instanton of the real article is a solution of the self-dual equation, and the self-dual equation makes sense over $\mathbb{C}$ with the complex group. The ADHM construction is the statement that the moduli of the complexified instanton are the data of a linear algebra problem — two complex vector spaces, two maps, and a reality condition — and it is the prototype of the modern moduli-space constructions. The corpus records the construction in The ADHM Construction and Biquaternion Instanton Data and the solitons in Instantons and Solitons in Biquaternionic Form.

Anomalies over $\mathbb{C}$. The chiral anomaly is the failure of a classical symmetry at the quantum level, and its computation is the divergence of a triangle diagram whose three external legs are the gauge currents. The coefficient is the trace of the generators over the chiral fermions, and it is an integer because the fermions form a representation of the compact group. Over $\mathbb{C}$ the same computation is a holomorphic function of the complexified coupling, and its imaginary part is the $\theta$ term. The corpus records the physics in Anomalies and Anomaly Cancellation in Biquaternionic Form, The Fujikawa Method and the Path-Integral Anomaly in Biquaternionic Form, and The Trace Anomaly in Biquaternionic Form.

Spinors over the Complexes

What the complexification does to a spinor. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the spinor of $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is a real or quaternionic object of dimension $2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$ (real) and the choice depends on $n$ modulo eight. Over $\mathbb{C}$ there is only one spinor, of complex dimension $2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$, and the real forms are the extra structures one may or may not be able to impose.

The three structures. A Dirac spinor is a complex spinor with no extra structure, and a mass term couples its two chiral halves. A Weyl spinor is one chiral half, and it exists whenever the dimension is even because then the chirality operator exists. A Majorana spinor is a spinor together with an antilinear map $J$ with $J^{2}=\pm1$ commuting with the action, and its existence depends on the signature and the dimension. A Majorana–Weyl spinor has both, and it exists in Lorentzian signature only when $n\equiv2\pmod 8$ — the ten-dimensional case, which is why the ten-dimensional supergravity and the heterotic string have a sixteen-component Majorana–Weyl spinor, the $16$ of $\mathrm{SO}(10)$ that is one generation of fermions.

The table and its physics. In four dimensions the Dirac spinor has four complex components, the Weyl two, and the Majorana four real; the massless electron is a Weyl spinor and the massive electron is a Dirac spinor. In six dimensions the Dirac spinor has eight complex components and the Weyl four, and the four is the defining representation of $\mathrm{SU}(4)$, which is the group of the next article. In ten dimensions the Dirac spinor has thirty-two complex components and the Majorana–Weyl sixteen, which is the spinor of the grand unified group. The dimensions are the same numbers as in the real article, because the complex dimension of the Clifford module does not depend on the real structure.

Why physics needs both. The real structure is what makes a mass term possible, because a mass term needs a Lorentz-invariant bilinear and the bilinear requires a conjugation. So the choice of real structure is the choice of the discrete symmetries: charge conjugation, parity, time reversal and their combinations. The corpus records the classification in Parra's Four Options of the Dirac Equation and the Discrete Symmetries and the theorem in The CPT Theorem in Biquaternionic Form.

The Technology Column

Phasors and impedance. Every AC network is solved by complex algebra, and the reason is the isomorphism of the bottom rung. Power engineering, filter design, and the stability analysis of a feedback loop are the same computation.

Control theory. The poles of the resolvent are the eigenvalues of the generator, and stability is the statement that they lie in the left half plane. The root locus, the Nyquist criterion, and the phase margin are statements about the complex plane and the analytic structure of a transfer function. A gain margin is a distance to a pole; a phase margin is an angle.

Signal processing. The analytic signal $x+i\mathcal{H}x$ has a one-sided spectrum, and its complex envelope is the I/Q pair of a radio. The Hilbert transform is the multiplication by $-i\,\mathrm{sgn}\,\omega$, so the whole of quadrature modulation is the complexification of a real signal.

Dielectric and optical metrology. A complex refractive index is measured by ellipsometry, and the Kramers–Kronig relation reconstructs one part from the other because the response is holomorphic. The design of an anti-reflection coating, of a metamaterial with a negative real part, and of a plasmonic sensor is the design of a complex function on the upper half plane.

Exceptional-point sensors. A non-Hermitian system tuned to an exceptional point has a response that scales as the square root of the perturbation, and the square root is steeper than a linear response for small perturbations. The technology is real: exceptional points have been used in gyroscopes, in nanoparticle detectors, and in mode-selection in lasers, and the price is the noise amplification that comes with the same square root.

Numerical analytic continuation. Two workhorse techniques of computational physics are complexifications. The complex-step derivative evaluates $f'(x)$ as $\operatorname{Im}f(x+ih)/h$ with $h$ of order $10^{-200}$, and it is exact to machine precision because there is no subtractive cancellation; it is used in automatic differentiation and in the sensitivity analysis of large codes. The contour deformation of a lattice or a Monte Carlo integral into the complex plane is used to evade a sign problem. Both are the use of holomorphy as a computational resource.

Amplitudes. The modern computation of scattering amplitudes is done in the spinor-helicity formalism, where the external data of a massless particle are two spinors and the amplitudes are rational functions with poles at the factorisation channels. The BCFW recursion builds an amplitude from its poles, and it is a statement about the analytic structure of a holomorphic function of the complexified momenta. The method computes processes that would be inaccessible to Feynman diagrams at high multiplicity.
