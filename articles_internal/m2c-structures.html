The Structures of $M_2(\mathbb{C})$

Introduction

$M_2(\mathbb{C})$ is four complex dimensions wide and carries eight layers of structure, and the layers have different dimensions, different operations and different vocabularies. The algebra has dimension four, its centre one, its maximal commutative subalgebras two, its Lie algebra four, its group four. A sentence about $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ is exact only when the layer is named.

One confusion is systematic, and this article settles it first: the set of matrices with a zero row is a right ideal, not a left one. The set with a zero column is the left ideal. The two sets differ, the transpose exchanges them, and $E_{21}E_{11}=E_{21}$ is the one-line witness.

The article is an inventory, one section per layer. Each layer is defined, its dimension is stated, and the result is carried by a proof, a computation, or a link to the article that proves it. The companion articles hold the long arguments; this one holds the map.

The eight layers are not redundant descriptions of one object. A subspace can be a subalgebra and not an ideal, an ideal and not a unital subalgebra, a Lie subalgebra and not an associative subalgebra. The dimensions in the table differ, and every difference is a place where a statement can be true on one layer and false on the next.

The Associative Algebra

The algebra is the set of arrays with the entrywise sum and the matrix product, and four matrices span it.

$$ E_{11}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad E_{12}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad E_{21}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad E_{22}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}. $$

The product rule is the whole multiplication table,

$$ E_{ij}E_{kl}=\delta_{jk}\,E_{il}, $$

so the algebra is associative, unital, of complex dimension $4$ and real dimension $8$, and non-commutative, because $E_{12}E_{21}=E_{11}$ while $E_{21}E_{12}=E_{22}$.

The algebra is not a domain. $E_{11}E_{22}=0$ and $E_{12}^{2}=0$, so there are zero-divisor pairs and nilpotents at the first opportunity. Invertibility is decided by the determinant, and the determinant is the algebraic norm of the biquaternion carrier.

The Centre

The centre is the line of scalars. Let $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\ c&d\end{pmatrix}$. From $AE_{11}=E_{11}A$ one reads $b=0$ and $c=0$, so $A$ is diagonal. From $AE_{12}=E_{12}A$ one reads $a=d$. So $A=aI$, and the centre is $\mathbb{C}I$, of dimension $1$.

Rank, Trace and Determinant

The rank takes three values, and each value has a stratum.

rank set complex dimension equation
$0$ $\{0\}$ $0$ $A=0$
$1$ the rank-one cone minus the origin $3$ $\det A=0$, $A\neq0$
$2$ the invertible set $4$ $\det A\neq0$

The trace and the determinant are the two coefficients of the characteristic polynomial,

$$ \chi_A(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}-(\operatorname{tr}A)\lambda+\det A, $$

and they are the two invariant functions under conjugation that generate the rest. Their difference from the biquaternion side is only the carrier: $\operatorname{tr}\mathsf{M}_2(\tilde Q)=2\,\mathrm{Sc}\,\tilde Q$ and $\det\mathsf{M}_2(\tilde Q)=\langle\tilde Q\rangle_\natural$.

Central Simplicity

The algebra is central simple of degree two. It is simple as a ring, its centre is the scalars, and its dimension over the centre is $4=2^{2}$. Equivalently $M_2(\mathbb{C})\cong\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}^{2})$, and the transpose is an anti-isomorphism onto the opposite algebra.

The Two-Sided Ideals

There are exactly two: $0$ and the algebra.

Proof. Let $I\neq0$ be a two-sided ideal and let $A\in I$ with $A_{ij}\neq0$. For any $p,q$,

$$ E_{pq}=\frac{1}{A_{ij}}\,E_{pi}\,A\,E_{jq}\in I, $$

because the product keeps the $(p,q)$-entry $A_{ij}$ and kills the others. The matrix units span, so $I=M_2(\mathbb{C})$.

The consequence is that the algebra has no proper quotient. Every nonzero two-sided ideal is the whole algebra, so the only quotients are $0$ and $M_2(\mathbb{C})$, and every nonzero element generates the unit ideal. This is what the word simple means, and it is decided by the identity $E_{ij}=E_{ik}E_{kj}$: a two-sided ideal that contains one entry of one matrix contains every matrix unit.

The Left Ideals, and Why a Zero Row Is Not One

A left ideal is a subspace stable under multiplication on the left, $M_2(\mathbb{C})L\subseteq L$. The right ideals are defined by $RM_2(\mathbb{C})\subseteq R$, and the two-sided ideals are the subspaces that are both.

The left ideals are the sets that kill a fixed subspace,

$$ L_U=\{A:U\subseteq\ker A\},\qquad U\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{2}, $$

of dimension $2(2-\dim U)$, because such an $A$ is a map $\mathbb{C}^{2}/U\to\mathbb{C}^{2}$.

The right ideals are the sets with the image inside a fixed subspace,

$$ R_W=\{A:\operatorname{im}A\subseteq W\},\qquad W\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{2}, $$

of dimension $2\dim W$.

The minimal left ideals are parametrised by the lines of $\mathbb{C}^{2}$, so they form a projective line. A left ideal of dimension $2$ contains no smaller nonzero left ideal, because the dimensions are $0$, $2$ and $4$; the lines of a two-dimensional space form $\mathbb{P}^1$.

The Two Shapes, Side by Side

The shape of the set decides the side, and the criterion is one product.

set matrix form left ideal right ideal
zero second column $\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\ c&0\end{pmatrix}=M_2(\mathbb{C})E_{11}$ yes no
zero bottom row $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\ 0&0\end{pmatrix}$ no yes

The zero-second-column set is a left ideal because $(AB)e_{2}=A(Be_{2})=0$ whenever $Be_{2}=0$. It is $L_{\mathbb{C}e_{2}}$, and it is also $M_2(\mathbb{C})E_{11}$.

The zero-bottom-row set is a right ideal and not a left ideal. It is $R_{\mathbb{C}e_{1}}$, because its columns lie in $\mathbb{C}e_{1}$, and right multiplication keeps them there: the columns of $BA$ are combinations of the columns of $A$. The witness is exact:

$$ E_{21}\,E_{11}=E_{21}, $$

where $E_{11}$ has a zero bottom row and the product does not. So left multiplication by $E_{21}$ carries the set outside itself.

The transpose exchanges the two families. $\operatorname{im}A^{\mathsf T}=(\ker A)^{\perp}$, so $A\in L_U$ exactly when $A^{\mathsf T}\in R_{U^{\perp}}$. The two families are therefore the same list read through an anti-isomorphism, and that is why a statement about a left ideal does not transfer to the right side until the transpose is named.

The Socle and the Modules

The socle is the set of elements of rank at most one, of complex dimension $3$. Every minimal left ideal consists of elements of rank at most one; conversely a rank-one element $uv^{\mathsf T}$ kills the hyperplane $v^{\perp}$ and lies in $L_{v^{\perp}}$. The socle is the union of the pencil of minimal left ideals, and any two of them meet at $0$.

Each minimal left ideal is a copy of $\mathbb{C}^{2}$ as a left module, and the regular module splits,

$$ M_2(\mathbb{C})\cong\mathbb{C}^{2}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}, $$

the two summands being $M_2(\mathbb{C})E_{11}$ and $M_2(\mathbb{C})E_{22}$. The algebra is semisimple, the module has length $2$, and the endomorphism ring of either summand is $\mathbb{C}$.

The Maximal Commutative Subalgebras

The maximal dimension of a commutative subalgebra is $2$, and every such subalgebra is generated by one element.

Proof of the bound. Let $C$ be commutative. If every element of $C$ is scalar then $\dim C\le1$. Otherwise $C$ contains a non-scalar $A$, and $C$ lies in the centraliser of $A$. A non-scalar $2\times2$ matrix has a minimal polynomial of degree $2$, so its centraliser is $\mathbb{C}[A]$, of dimension $2$. Hence $\dim C\le2$.

Proof of the generation. A commutative subalgebra of dimension $2$ contains a non-scalar $A$, is contained in $\mathbb{C}[A]$, and has the same dimension, so it is $\mathbb{C}[A]$. Its isomorphism type is $\mathbb{C}[x]/(m_A(x))$, and $m_A$ has degree $2$.

The Two Classes

class algebra nontrivial idempotents nilpotents normaliser in $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{C})$
split $\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}$ two, $E_{11}$ and $E_{22}$ none the monomial group, dimension $2$
local $\mathbb{C}[x]/(x^{2})$ none one line, $\mathbb{C}N$ the Borel, dimension $3$

The split case is the diagonal. The discriminant $(\operatorname{tr}A)^{2}-4\det A$ is nonzero, the minimal polynomial has two distinct roots, and the algebra is $\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}$.

The local case is the dual numbers. Take $N=E_{12}$, so $N^{2}=0$ and $\mathbb{C}[N]=\{aI+bN\}$, the matrices $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\ 0&a\end{pmatrix}$. This is $\mathbb{C}[x]/(x^{2})$, and it contains exactly one nonzero nilpotent line.

A normaliser computation separates the two. If $g$ is upper triangular and invertible then $gNg^{-1}=(g_{11}/g_{22})N$, so the Borel normalises $\mathbb{C}[N]$, and the Borel has dimension $3$. If $g$ has a nonzero entry in position $(2,1)$ then $gNg^{-1}$ leaves the space $\mathbb{C}\{I,N\}$, as one checks on the entries. So the normaliser of the local class is the Borel. The normaliser of the diagonal class is the monomial group, of dimension $2$.

Maximal Commutative Is Not Cartan

Only the split class is a Cartan subalgebra of $\mathfrak{gl}_2(\mathbb{C})$. A Cartan subalgebra is abelian, self-normalising and reductive in the ambient algebra. The diagonal algebra satisfies all three. The dual-number algebra is abelian and maximal commutative, but its normaliser is the Borel, so it is not self-normalising, and it is not a Cartan subalgebra. The distinction matters in the Lie theory: the Cartan subalgebras of $\mathfrak{gl}_2$ form one conjugacy class, the diagonal one, while the maximal commutative subalgebras form two.

The Lie Algebra

With the commutator, the algebra becomes $\mathfrak{gl}_2(\mathbb{C})$, of dimension $4$.

The Lie algebra splits as a centre and a simple part,

$$ \mathfrak{gl}_2(\mathbb{C})=\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})\oplus\mathbb{C}I, $$

with $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ the traceless matrices, of dimension $3$. The split is a direct sum of Lie algebras because $I$ is central, and $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ is simple of type $A_1$: its only ideals are $0$ and itself, and $[\mathfrak{sl}_2,\mathfrak{sl}_2]=\mathfrak{sl}_2$.

The Killing form has rank $3$ and radical the centre. On the basis $(E_{11},E_{12},E_{21},E_{22})$ the Gram matrix of $\kappa(X,Y)=\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{ad}_X\operatorname{ad}_Y)$ is

$$ \begin{pmatrix}2&0&0&-2\\ 0&0&4&0\\ 0&4&0&0\\ -2&0&0&2\end{pmatrix}, $$

of rank $3$, with kernel spanned by $E_{11}+E_{22}=I$. So $\kappa(I,\cdot)=0$, which is also immediate because $\operatorname{ad}_I=0$, and the radical is exactly the centre. The restriction to $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$ is non-degenerate, which is Cartan's criterion for the semisimplicity of the simple part.

The Root Decomposition

The diagonal Cartan algebra diagonalises the two off-diagonal lines,

$$ \mathfrak{gl}_2(\mathbb{C})=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{n}_+\oplus\mathfrak{n}_-,\qquad \mathfrak{h}=\mathbb{C}\{E_{11},E_{22}\},\quad \mathfrak{n}_+=\mathbb{C}E_{12},\quad \mathfrak{n}_-=\mathbb{C}E_{21}, $$

with dimensions $2+1+1$. The brackets are

$$ [\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{n}_\pm]\subseteq\mathfrak{n}_\pm,\qquad [\mathfrak{n}_+,\mathfrak{n}_-]\subseteq\mathfrak{h},\qquad [\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{h}]=0 . $$

The two roots are opposite, and the system is $A_1$. With $H=\operatorname{diag}(a,-a)$ one has $[H,E_{12}]=2aE_{12}$ and $[H,E_{21}]=-2aE_{21}$, so the roots are $\pm\alpha$ with $\alpha(H)=2a$, and the coroot is $E_{11}-E_{22}$.

The Borel subalgebra is the upper-triangular one, and it is maximal solvable. Its derived algebra is $\mathfrak{n}_+$, of dimension $1$, so the derived series reaches $0$ in two steps; no larger subalgebra is solvable, because any element with a nonzero entry in position $(2,1)$ generates $\mathfrak{n}_-$ by bracketing and then $\mathfrak{h}$.

The 2-dimensional row is not a single conjugacy class. The Cartan algebra is abelian and contains no nonzero nilpotent; the column algebra $\mathbb{C}\{E_{11},E_{21}\}$ is solvable and not abelian, because $[E_{11},E_{21}]=-E_{21}$; the dual-number algebra is abelian and contains the nilpotent line $\mathbb{C}N$, so it is not conjugate to $\mathfrak{h}$.

The Group and Its Subgroups

Taking the invertible elements gives $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{C})$, of complex dimension $4$ and real dimension $8$. It is connected, and the determinant is a surjection onto $\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ with kernel the special linear group:

$$ 1\longrightarrow \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})\longrightarrow \mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{C})\xrightarrow{\ \det\ }\mathbb{C}^{\times}\longrightarrow 1 . $$

The Lie algebra of the group is the Lie algebra of the previous section. The exponential of a matrix lies in the group, and near the identity it is a local isomorphism. Over $\mathbb{C}$ the map is surjective: every invertible complex matrix has a logarithm. The failure belongs to the real form, where a matrix with a negative real eigenvalue and an odd number of Jordan blocks for it has no real logarithm, and the failure of injectivity belongs to both, because an eigenvalue is fixed only modulo $2\pi i$.

The torus is the maximal torus, and the Borel subgroup is the stabiliser of a line. The quotient $B/T$ is the projective line, of dimension $1=3-2$, and $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{C})/B$ is the same projective line, the flag variety of the flag $0\subset\mathbb{C}e_{1}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}$. The Bruhat decomposition has two cells, the closed one $B$ and the open one.

The special linear group is simply connected and semisimple. It is the double cover of $\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb{C})$, and the kernel of the cover is $\{\pm I\}$. The projective group is the quotient,

$$ \mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb{C})=\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{C})/\mathbb{C}^{\times}I\cong\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})/\{\pm I\}\cong\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb{C}), $$

of dimension $3$. It is also the automorphism group of the algebra: every automorphism of $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ is inner, by the Skolem–Noether theorem, and two inner automorphisms agree exactly when their matrices differ by a scalar.

The compact real form is $\mathrm{U}(2)$, and its determinant-one part is $\mathrm{SU}(2)\cong S^{3}$. These are real subgroups and not complex ones. Inside $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{C})$ they form the fixed set of the anti-automorphism $A\mapsto(A^{*})^{-1}$, whose fixed-set equation is $AA^{*}=I$.

The derivations are the traceless matrices. Every derivation of $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ is inner, $\delta_X(A)=[X,A]$, and the inner derivations $\operatorname{ad}_X$ depend only on the traceless part of $X$, so the space of derivations is $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$, of dimension $3$. The coincidence of the dimensions of $\mathrm{PGL}_2$ and $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ is the tangent and the algebra of the same automorphism group.

The Finite Subgroups

The finite subgroups of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})$ form five families, and they are the binary polyhedral groups.

family order image in $\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb{C})$ affine Dynkin diagram
cyclic $C_n$ $n$ cyclic $\tilde A_{n-1}$
binary dihedral $\mathrm{Dic}_n$ $4n$ dihedral $D_n$ $\tilde D_{n+2}$
binary tetrahedral $2T$ $24$ tetrahedral $T$ $\tilde E_6$
binary octahedral $2O$ $48$ octahedral $O$ $\tilde E_7$
binary icosahedral $2I$ $120$ icosahedral $I$ $\tilde E_8$

The classification is Klein's, and the correspondence with the diagrams is McKay's. Klein classified the finite rotation groups of the sphere as cyclic, dihedral, and the three symmetry groups of the Platonic solids. McKay read the same list as the affine Dynkin diagrams by building a graph from the representation theory of the binary group. The spelling is McKay, not McKey.

Every finite subgroup of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})$ is conjugate to a subgroup of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$. Averaging the standard Hermitian form over the finite group gives an invariant positive-definite form, and the group becomes unitary for it. So the finite subgroups of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})$ are the finite subgroups of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, and the table is complete.

A finite subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{C})$ need not lie in $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})$. Its image in $\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb{C})\cong\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathbb{C})$ is one of the five Klein groups, and its intersection with the scalars $\mathbb{C}^{\times}I$ is a finite cyclic group. So the general finite subgroup is a finite central extension of a Klein group by a cyclic group of scalars. Multiplying a binary polyhedral group by scalar roots of unity gives examples outside $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})$.

The biquaternion carrier gives the same list. The unit quaternions $\{\pm1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k\}$ together with the sixteen elements $\tfrac12(\pm1\pm i\pm j\pm k)$ form a group of order $24$, the binary tetrahedral group; the twenty-four elements are exactly the units of the Hurwitz quaternions. Under $\mathsf{M}_2$ they become matrices of determinant one, because the determinant is the quaternion norm. The same construction with the other two Platonic solids gives orders $48$ and $120$.

The Involution Layer

The algebra carries three complex-linear anti-automorphisms and two conjugate-linear ones, and each has a fixed set that is a structure in its own right.

involution fixed set dimension
$A\mapsto A^{\mathsf T}$ $\operatorname{Sym}_2(\mathbb{C})$ $3$ complex
$A\mapsto JA^{\mathsf T}J^{-1}$ $\mathbb{C}I$ $1$ complex
$A\mapsto A^{*}$ $\operatorname{Herm}_2$ $4$ real
$A\mapsto\bar A$ $M_2(\mathbb{R})$ $4$ real
$A\mapsto J\bar AJ^{-1}$ a copy of $\mathbb{H}$ $4$ real

The first two are complex-linear and the last three are conjugate-linear. For $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\ c&d\end{pmatrix}$ one has $JA^{\mathsf T}J^{-1}=\operatorname{adj}A$, so the symplectic involution fixes exactly the scalars and acts as $-1$ on $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$. The two conjugate-linear automorphisms have the two real forms of the algebra as fixed sets, $M_2(\mathbb{R})$ and the quaternion algebra $\mathbb{H}$, and those are the only two real forms.

The Strongest Objection

The objection is that the list is redundant: one four-dimensional object is being described eight times.

The answer is that the layers differ in dimension and in the operation preserved. The centre has dimension $1$ and the algebra $4$; the Cartan algebra has dimension $2$ and the Borel $3$; the projective group has dimension $3$ and the group $4$. An intersection of two layers is not a layer: the diagonal algebra is an associative subalgebra and a Lie subalgebra at once, but it is not an ideal; the left ideal $L_{\mathbb{C}e_{2}}$ is an associative subalgebra without identity and a Lie subalgebra; and a line $\mathbb{C}X$ is always a Lie subalgebra, while it is an associative subalgebra only when $X^{2}$ is a multiple of $X$.

The second answer is that the layers are related by named maps. The exponential carries the Lie algebra to the group; the adjoint carries the group to the automorphisms of the algebra; the commutator is the bracket read on the associative product; the trace is the linear functional that vanishes on commutators. A statement is transferred along those maps or not at all, and the maps are the content of the companion articles.

Summary

$M_2(\mathbb{C})$ has complex dimension $4$, and each layer of structure on it has its own dimension: the centre $1$, the maximal commutative subalgebras $2$, the Cartan algebra $2$, the Borel $3$, $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ $3$, the group $4$, the torus $2$, the Borel subgroup $3$, the socle $3$, the projective group $3$.

The two-sided ideals are only $0$ and the algebra; the left ideals are the sets killing a fixed subspace and form three levels with a projective line in the middle; the right ideals are the sets with image inside a fixed subspace and are the transpose of the left ones. The set with a zero row is a right ideal, not a left one, and $E_{21}E_{11}=E_{21}$ proves it.

The Lie algebra is $\mathfrak{sl}_2\oplus\mathbb{C}I$, with a Cartan algebra of dimension $2$, a Borel of dimension $3$ and roots $\pm\alpha$. The group is $\mathrm{GL}_2$, with maximal torus of dimension $2$, Borel subgroup of dimension $3$, special linear group of dimension $3$ and projective group of dimension $3$. The finite subgroups of the special linear group are the five binary polyhedral groups, of orders $n$, $4n$, $24$, $48$ and $120$.
