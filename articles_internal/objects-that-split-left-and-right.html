Objects That Split Left and Right

Introduction

The question. The Dirac equation splits into a left-handed member and a right-handed member. Which other quantities of the corpus split the same way?

The answer in one line. Four objects carry a split that is not the Dirac one: the field strength, the Euclidean two-form, the two-dimensional light-cone mode, and the operator algebra itself. Every other entry is either the Dirac split read on an object built from the Dirac field, such as a current, a bilinear, a vertex or a lattice count, or a place where the split is missing.

Why the question is confusing, and the cure. The corpus writes left and right for three different things. One is the slot of an operator. One is the label of an element. One is the helicity of a field. They are separated once, in §The Three Things Called Left and Right, and every entry of the list names the one it uses.

The checklist. A split counts as a left–right split here only when four things are present: a splitting operator, its two halves, a statement of whether the halves are copies or conjugates, and a coupling term that maps one half into the other. §The Checklist states the four and fills them for the Dirac case.

The list. §The List numbers fourteen objects. Each carries a paragraph, one line on how it stands to the Dirac case, and the corpus articles that treat it, with direct links.

The near misses. §What Is Not a Left–Right Split collects five structures that look like splits and are not.

Warrant. The algebraic statements of §The Checklist are recomputed here on the sixteen cells of the algebra, and the residuals are tabulated. Every claim about an article is read from that article and carries its link.

The Three Things Called Left and Right

First, the slot of an operator. An operator of $\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{B})$ is a cell $L_aR_b$: the left slot holds $a$ and the right slot holds $b$. The pure left multipliers form a four-dimensional space $L(\mathbb{B})$, the pure right multipliers another, $R(\mathbb{B})$, they meet in the scalars, and their sum is seven-dimensional inside the sixteen. The label is read from the cell and from nothing else. This is operator chirality, and it splits operators, not fields.

Second, the label of an element. The volume cell $\mathsf{V}^{\mathrm{vol}}=-iR_{\vec e_z}$ is an involution of spectrum $+1,+1,-1,-1$, so every element $\tilde Q$ splits into $\tilde Q_+$ and $\tilde Q_-$, written $\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}_R$ and $\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}_L$ in the letters of the Dirac literature. This is the value or subscript of an element, and it splits the algebra, two complex dimensions on each side.

Third, the helicity of a field. The projection $\lambda=\vec u_p\cdot\vec S$ of the spin on a declared axis has the spectrum $\hbar h$ on a carrier of spin $s$. This is a label of a state, and it needs an axis that the algebra does not supply.

Name Where it lives The two sides What fixes the side

The Dirac equation uses the second for its field and the first for its operators. The gradient acts by left multiplication, so it preserves the value; the chirality cell acts on the right, so it grades. The two commute because they sit on opposite sides. That is the mechanism of the whole subject, and the mass is the term that bridges the two halves because it is central, hence the one operator with no side at all.

The consequence, stated plainly. No sum of left multiplications carries one value into the other. The Dirac equation therefore cannot be one equation on one half; it is a pair, and the pair is the definition.

The Checklist

Four questions decide whether a candidate object carries a left–right split.

  1. The splitting operator. Which operator separates the two sides, and what is its square? A square of $+1$ gives two eigenspaces and a $\mathbb{Z}_2$ grading. A square of $-1$ gives a complex structure and two conjugate halves.
  2. The halves. Are they two copies of one object, or two inequivalent objects? A copy is vector-like; a conjugate is not.
  3. The coupling. Which term maps one half into the other? A term that stays inside one half is not a coupling.
  4. The status. Is the split stated in the corpus, is it a reading of a corpus statement, or is it outside the corpus?

The Dirac row, filled and recomputed. The splitting operator is $\mathsf{V}^{\mathrm{vol}}=-iR_{\vec e_z}$, of square $+1$. The halves are two complex dimensions each, both stable under left multiplication, so both are copies of the simple left module $S$. The coupling is the mass, an isomorphism of the two halves. The status is stated in the corpus.

What the table says in words. Left multiplication never leaves a half, so the Dirac operator, which is a left multiplier, cannot exchange the two halves: that is the second line, and it is why the equation is a pair. A right multiplication by the time unit or by the axis unit never leaves a half either, and a right multiplication by either other unit exchanges them: those are the third and fourth lines, and they are why the chirality cell is a right multiplier and why it grades. The mass is central, so it belongs to both families, has no side, and is the only term that can pair the halves.

2. The Operator Algebra Itself

The split. $\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{B})$ splits into the left multipliers and the right multipliers, of dimensions four and four, meeting in the scalars. The exchange is the congruence

$$ \mathsf C\,L(a^{\natural})\,\mathsf C=R(a), $$

verified at residual $0$ on random $a$. This is a left–right split of the operators, not of a field.

Relation to the Dirac case. It is the parent structure: the Dirac equation puts its derivatives in the left family and its chirality cell in the right family, and the two commute because a left multiplier and a right multiplier always do.

The one member of both families is the mass. $L\cap R$ is the scalar line, so the mass is the only term of the Dirac equation that has no side. That is where the coupling of object 1 comes from.

4. The Field Strength

The split. The Hodge dual of the field strength is the scalar imaginary,

$$ \star\tilde F=-i\tilde F,\qquad \star^2=-1\ \text{on the real two-forms}, $$

so on the complexified two-form space $\star$ has eigenvalues $\pm i$, and the projectors $\tfrac12(1\pm i\star)$ have rank three each. The two halves are $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$: the self-dual and the anti-self-dual part of the field.

The three differences from the Dirac case, and they matter.

First, the splitting operator is a complex structure, not an involution. Its two eigenspaces are a complex space and its conjugate, not two copies.

Second, the halves are inequivalent. $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ are conjugate representations of the Lorentz group. This is the one place in the algebra where the two sides differ in kind, and §What Is Not a Left–Right Split records what the Dirac halves cannot do for that reason.

Third, the mass does not couple the halves. The Proca equation is $\tilde\nabla\tilde F=\mu^2\tilde A$; its divergence gives the Lorenz condition, then $(\Box-\mu^2)\tilde A=0$, then $(\Box-\mu^2)\tilde F=0$. The dual is constant, so it commutes with $\Box$, and each half obeys the massive equation on its own. The reason is structural: a one-form has no self-dual split in four dimensions, so the mass term lives in a space where the split is not defined, and the split and the mass never meet.

Relation to the Dirac case. Same shape, since an operator's eigenspaces carry the two physical labels, and the corpus's cleanest case of a split whose halves are inequivalent. It is not a copy of the Dirac case, and it is the strongest entry of the list.

5. The Euclidean Two-Form

The split. In Euclidean signature the dual squares to $+1$,

$$ \star^2=+1,\qquad \tfrac12(1\pm\star)\ \text{real projectors}, $$

and the six real components split into two real three-dimensional halves. The conditions are $\vec B=\pm\vec E$, and they select independent real solutions, not the two projections of one field.

Relation to the Dirac case. The closest in shape of all the entries. The splitting operator is an involution over the reals, as in the Dirac case, and the two halves are separate solutions. The difference is that the halves are real and the field is real, so there is no conjugate half to pair with.

6. The Rarita–Schwinger Field

The split. The carrier is a family of four algebra elements, one per spacetime index, and the dynamics is the chiral pair written once for each,

$$ \tilde\nabla\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}_{\mu,R}=m\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}_{\mu,L},\qquad \tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}_{\mu,L}=m\tilde{Ξ}^{\mathrm{sp}}_{\mu,R}, $$

with the trace constraint and the divergence constraint on top. The chirality cell is the right multiplication by $-i\vec e_z$, which that article writes $\omega=-\vec e_z$, the factor $i$ being carried separately.

Relation to the Dirac case. Four copies of it, with two constraints. Nothing new in the split itself; what is new is that four copies must be tied together.

7. The Graviton

The split. 10. Two Dimensions: Left- and Right-Movers

The split. In two dimensions the light-cone derivatives

$$ \partial_t-\partial_x,\qquad \partial_t+\partial_x $$

Relation to the Dirac case. A split of the modes, not of the algebra. The coupling is again the mass: written on the right, it exchanges the two factors, and its bosonized image is the cosine of the sine-Gordon model.

11. The Weak Vertex

13. The Weyl Spinor and the Twistor

The split. $S=(\tfrac12,0)$ and $\bar S=(0,\tfrac12)$ are the two inequivalent conjugate representations of the Lorentz group, and a twistor is a pair, one spinor of each chirality. The article separates the two reasons a pair appears: parity, which makes a single Weyl spinor a complete but parity-violating theory, and incidence, which makes the second spinor the dual of the first rather than a second field.

Relation to the Dirac case. The split is the Dirac one, and the entry is here for its limit: the spinor halves are inequivalent as well, and it is the reason $SU(2)_L\times SU(2)_R$ cannot be read off the Dirac field. The algebra's two chiral halves are copies of $S$; the inequivalent partner is the conjugate module, a different object.

What Is Not a Left–Right Split

The material and informational sectors. $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ and $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ are the $\pm1$ eigenspaces of the anti-Hermitian conjugation $\flat=-{}^{*}$, and the central imaginary exchanges them. The shape is that of an involution, as a chirality is, and the resemblance stops there. The two sides are real four-dimensional spaces that carry different roles, four-vectors on one side and observables and states on the other, not two copies of one role. Nothing pairs them, and no first-order operator of the algebra carries one into the other.

The two copies of $\mathbb{C}^2$. The split of $\mathbb{B}$ into two copies of $\mathbb{C}^2$ replaces the algebra's $1+3$ with a $2+2$ that the algebra does not carry, and Conceptual Conventions lists it as an unpreferred concept with that defect named.

$\mathrm{SU}(2)_L\times\mathrm{SU}(2)_R$. A left–right symmetric gauge structure needs the two halves to carry inequivalent representations. The algebra's two halves are copies, so every action by left multiplication is vector-like on them. The inequivalence lives in the conjugate module, which is a different object from the chirality halves.

Helicity at non-zero mass. Helicity and the value coincide only at $m=0$. At $m\neq0$ the helicity operator still exists and still has its spectrum, and it no longer labels the halves; the two readings of one field are genuinely different.

The Peirce lines. A Peirce decomposition needs two idempotents and returns four one-dimensional corners. The chiral split is one operator and returns two halves of two complex dimensions. Four lines and two halves are not the same partition, and the difference is measurable.

Summary

A left–right split needs four things: a splitting operator, its two halves, a statement of whether the halves are copies or conjugates, and a coupling that maps one half into the other.

The Dirac field has all four, and it is the only object of the corpus that has them natively. Its splitting operator is the right multiplier $-iR_{\vec e_z}$, of square $+1$; its halves are two copies of the simple module; its coupling is the mass, the one central term, which has no side.

Four objects carry a split that is not the Dirac one. The field strength splits by the Hodge dual, whose square is $-1$, and its halves are inequivalent, which is the reason the Standard Model's chiral gauge group is not read off the Dirac field. The Euclidean two-form splits by the same dual, whose square is then $+1$, and its halves are independent real solutions. The two-dimensional light-cone mode splits into left-movers and right-movers, the two factors of the d'Alembertian. The operator algebra splits into its left multipliers and its right multipliers, and the mass is the one operator that belongs to both.

The remaining entries are the Dirac split read on a derived object: a current, a bilinear, a vertex, a count. They are worth listing because the split travels, and because two of them sharpen it: the anomaly turns it into a matching of two sums, and the lattice theorem turns it into a balance that no regularization escapes.

The one hole is the graviton. The corpus carries the helicity $\pm2$ and no involution whose eigenspaces are the two helicities, and the object that would fill the hole, the self-dual split of the linearized Weyl tensor, is outside the corpus.
