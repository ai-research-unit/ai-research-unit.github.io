The D'Alembertian and the Laplacian: A Census of Two Unmarked Glyphs

Introduction

Two glyphs, and after this pass one meaning each. The d'Alembertian:

$$ \Box=\sum_{\mu}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial Q_\mu^2}, $$

and the Laplacian:

$$ \Delta=\sum_{\eta}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial Q_\eta^2}. $$

The first stands 1510 times in the corpus, the second 606; both totals count a formula once, and every one of the second is the Laplacian.

A glyph that carries two meanings is not a notation, it is a hazard. Until this pass $\Delta$ was doing five jobs at once: the Laplacian; a difference of two values, $\Delta u$ for a spin fraction and $\Delta S$ for an action; a spinor module, $\Delta=S\oplus\bar S$; a conformal weight; and a detuning, $\Delta=\omega_0-\omega$. A reader who meets $\Delta r'_t$ cannot tell from the glyph whether the Laplacian is being applied or a coordinate is being incremented, and the sign of the entire spatial part turns on the answer.

This pass removes the collision, and the removal is counted. 261 $\Delta$ glyphs were rewritten. 34 became $\delta$, because they were differences. 227 became a calligraphic letter with a subscript that spells the object: $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{rep}}$ for the spinor module, $\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{dim}}$ for a weight, $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{gap}}$ for a gap or a detuning, and fourteen more. $\Delta$ is now the Laplacian and nothing else.

What this article establishes. What each glyph names. The rule that decides the subscript, and the second rule that decides between $\Delta$, $\delta$ and a letter. The census, by class, with every article named, linked and quoted: 235 rows, no class summarised and left unlisted, and a second article, The D'Alembertian and the Laplacian: The Patched Articles, which lists every article whose glyph carries a $\Phi$, with the count on each glyph. The renaming, symbol by symbol. And the sites that stay bare, each with its reason.

The answer, stated first. The subscript follows the coordinates. A formula that prints $r'_t$ or $r_\eta$ is in the material chart, and its Laplacian is $\Delta_\Phi$. A formula that prints $h_t$ or $h'_\eta$ is in the informational chart, and its Laplacian is $\Delta_\Psi$. A formula that prints $R_\mu$, or prints no coordinate, names the operator in the algebra: $\Delta_Q$, or the bare $\Delta$. A formula whose space coordinates are $\tau$ or $w$ takes $\Delta_E$ or $\Delta_{w\bar w}$. The subscript states which chart the derivatives were taken in. It never states a different operator: $\Delta_\Phi$ and $\Delta_\Psi$ are the same operator up to a sign.

The rule is written into the conventions, not only into this article. Operator and Calculus Conventions, §The Laplacian Is the Only Delta, states it for the series: a symbol that is not a Laplacian does not wear the letter, a difference wears $\delta$, and a named object wears a letter of its own. This article is the audit of that sentence: it counts every place the letter stands.

The Two Glyphs and Their One Definition

One definition each, in the conventions. Operator and Calculus Conventions fixes both, and fixes the identity between them:

$$ \Box=\sum_{\mu}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial Q_\mu^2} =\frac{\partial^2}{\partial R_t^2}+\Delta_Q,\qquad \Delta_Q=\sum_\eta\frac{\partial^2}{\partial R_\eta^2}. $$

The identity holds in the algebra, before any coordinate is named: the four partial derivatives are taken in the complex coefficients $Q_\mu$, and $\Delta_Q$ is the three of them that carry a space unit.

On a chart the same operator reads differently. The substitution is the chain rule, and the corpus writes it once:

chart free parameters $\Box$ $\Delta$
$\mathbb{B}$ eight real $\partial^2/\partial R_t^2+\Delta_Q$ $\Delta_Q$
material $\mathbb{B}_{\Phi}$ $r'_t$, $r_\eta$ $-\partial^2/\partial {r'_t}^2+\Delta_\Phi$ $\Delta_\Phi=\sum_\eta\partial^2/\partial r_\eta^2$
informational $\mathbb{B}_{\Psi}$ $h_t$, $h'_\eta$ $\partial^2/\partial h_t^2-\Delta_\Psi$ $\Delta_\Psi=\sum_\eta\partial^2/\partial h'^2_\eta$

Three facts follow, and they are the whole of the sign bookkeeping. The material Laplacian is the chart-free one, $\Delta_\Phi=\Delta_Q$, because the material space coordinates are real. The informational Laplacian is its negative, $\Delta_\Psi=-\Delta_Q$, because the informational space coordinates are imaginary and each second derivative carries $i^2=-1$. The d'Alembertian is the same number on both charts, which is why it needs no mark.

Verified at residual zero. On a complex test field of four real parameters, with the material substitution $R_t=ir'_t$ and the informational one $R_\eta=ih'_\eta$, the two chart readings agree with the chart-free $\Box$ to machine zero, and $\Delta_\Psi+\Delta_Q=0$ exactly. The identities in the table are not a convention to be remembered. They are the chain rule.

Why Both Glyphs Carry the Subscript

The two Laplacians are not the same operator. $\Delta_\Phi=+\Delta_Q$ and $\Delta_\Psi=-\Delta_Q$. A formula that prints $r'_t$ and a bare $\Delta$ leaves the reader unable to tell which of the two is meant, and the difference is the sign of the entire spatial part.

The two d'Alembertians are the same operator, and the corpus mistook that for a licence to leave the glyph bare. $\Box_\Phi=\Delta_\Phi-\partial^2/\partial {r'_t}^2$ and $\Box_\Psi=\partial^2/\partial h_t^2-\Delta_\Psi$ are one operator read on two charts; they are equal, and the sign of the time term simply moves from one side to the other. So a bare $\Box$ is never wrong in value. It is only silent about which chart the sign pattern belongs to — and that silence is what let a formula print $\Delta_\Phi$ beside a bare $\Box$, marked on one glyph and unmarked on the other.

The mark is a statement about the chart, not about the operator. The same sentence in Operator and Calculus Conventions prints both at once: $\Delta_Q=\sum_\eta\partial^2/\partial r_\eta^2=\Delta_\Phi$, and the two displays

$$ \Box_\Phi=\Delta_\Phi-\frac{\partial^2}{\partial {r'_t}^2},\qquad \Box_\Psi=\frac{\partial^2}{\partial h_t^2}-\Delta_\Psi, $$

are headed the special case of the material sector and the special case of the informational sector. The name with the subscript is the one that says where the derivatives were taken.

A formula that prints only $R_\mu$ is in the algebra, and its glyphs are $\Box$ and $\Delta_Q$. The corpus writes both bare there, and writes $\Delta_Q$ when it wants the name spelled. That is the one place where bare is not a lapse but the correct reading.

The Rule

The glyph follows the object, and the subscript follows the coordinates. Two decisions, in this order, and the first one decides whether the glyph is a $\Delta$ at all.

First, is the object the Laplacian? If the symbol acts on a field, it is $\Delta$ and takes a subscript that names the chart. If it names a difference of two values of one quantity, it is $\delta$: $\delta u$, $\delta S$, $\delta E$, $\delta r'_t$, $\delta\omega$. If it names anything else, it takes a letter of its own, listed in the next section. There is no third use of $\Delta$. The letter is reserved: in this corpus, and in every article added to it, a $\Delta$ is the Laplacian.

Second, which chart? If the formula prints $r'_t$ or $r_\eta$, write $\Box_\Phi$ and $\Delta_\Phi$. If it prints $h_t$ or $h'_\eta$, write $\Box_\Psi$ and $\Delta_\Psi$. If it prints $R_\mu$, or no coordinate at all, write $\Box$ and $\Delta_Q$. If the space coordinates are $\tau$, write $\Delta_E$; if they are $w$, write $\Delta_{w\bar w}$.

The first decision is a filter, and it is the one that was missing. A symbol that has two readings has no reading: the eye cannot separate the operator from the increment, and the subscript cannot help, because it is attached to a letter that has already lost its meaning. The subscript rule of the earlier passes was correct and useless while $\Delta$ still meant five things.

One consequence is worth stating separately. A formula that prints $\Delta_Q$ beside $r'_t$ is not wrong in its identity — the Laplacians are equal — but it names the operator in the chart it is not working in. That is the only class of edit here that changes a formula already correct in value.

How the rule is checked. Count the glyphs, then read the ones that are not written out. A $\Delta$ followed by a subscript is a Laplacian in a named chart; a $\Delta$ written as a sum of second derivatives is a definition of one; everything else must be read, and the census below reads every one of the 180 bare occurrences against the coordinates printed beside it.

One rule for the subscript: it follows the coordinates of the formula. A material formula carries $\Box_\Phi$ and $\Delta_\Phi$; an informational one $\Box_\Psi$ and $\Delta_\Psi$; a formula in the algebra, or one that prints no coordinate, carries $\Box$ and $\Delta_Q$; a Euclidean or Kähler one carries $\Delta_E$ or $\Delta_{w\bar w}$.

The corpus prints 606 $\Delta$ in 112 articles and 1510 $\Box$ in 167, and not one $\Delta$ means anything other than a Laplacian. 300 of the $\Delta$ carry the material subscript, in 86 articles, and 396 of the $\Box$ do, in 115; those articles are listed one by one in the companion article. 261 glyphs that used to borrow the letter now carry their own: 34 $\delta$ and 227 calligraphic symbols in seventeen letter classes.

The rule is not a preference. A glyph with two readings has no reading, and no subscript can rescue a letter that has already lost its meaning. That is what this pass fixes, and the count is what keeps it fixed.
