Relativity Migration: The Method for a Four-Section Category

Introduction

What this is. A working note for restructuring the relativity category of the physics menu into four sections: the algebra and its objects, then the Lorentz transformation in general, then the transformation on the material sector, then the transformation on the informational sector. The first section is new to the category: it absorbs the algebra category that precedes it. It carries the method, the measurements that support it, the traps that cost time in the past, the order of the work, and the full listing of the result: every block, every band, every article, one line each.

What it is not. It is not a catalogue and not a judgement of the physics. Every number here is a count of words or of entries, not an assessment of an argument.

The state of the category today. Fifty-nine entries in fifteen subsections, preceded in the menu by two categories, Biquaternion Universe with seventeen entries and Biquaternion Algebra Physical Interpretations with thirty-two. Inside the category the fifty-nine are ordered by object: frame, algebra, group, Lie algebra, decompositions, classes, representations, orbits, velocity, rod and clock, exercises, fields, larger groups, kinematics, action.

The state after the migration. The menu carries the four sections, $31+29+30+17=107$, and the head of the physics menu holds three categories instead of four. Two files were deleted and their content merged into a survivor, one was renamed, one left for articles_internal, six were written, and six moved in from the quantum categories. The build is clean: every physics page resolves its links, the orphan prune removed exactly the three pages the menu no longer cites, and the three sitemaps carry 3089 URLs.

The Target

One category, four sections, 107 entries. The relativity category absorbs the algebra category that precedes it. The two are then read as one movement: the objects first, then the three readings of the transformation that acts on them.

Why the first section is the universe and not the transformation. It is the algebra the transformation acts on: the element, its six subspaces and two ledgers, the four products, the four forms, the three norms, the partition of the invariants, the operators, the derivative and the transforms. None of it needs a frame, a boost or a sector to be stated. Putting it first is what lets the next three sections be short: each of them applies an object that has already been defined once.

Why this is a merge and not an amputation. Today the algebra and the Lorentz group are two categories with an artificial wall between them: the group of invertibles, the cone and the orders sits on one side and the group of unit norm on the other; the partition, two invariants and four regimes on one side and the four kinds of frame transformation on the other. The wall is editorial, not mathematical. Removing it costs one menu entry and gains the reader the statement, in one place, of what the four regimes are.

What it costs. The algebra articles serve the quantum categories too, and a reader looking for the four products under a heading that says relativity may not look there. Two things answer that: the section is called The Biquaternion Universe and Its Objects, which names what a quantum reader is looking for; and the four quantum categories that need it already cite its articles by title in their openings.

The Boundary With What Comes Before

Three categories precede relativity today. Biquaternion Universe (17 entries, the programme: introduction, review, history, status, and five readings of the algebra), Biquaternion Universe Conventions (10, a reference chapter), and Biquaternion Algebra Physical Interpretations (32, the algebra presented). The question is where each of the 59 belongs once the first section of relativity is the algebra.

The measurement first: no article duplicates another's text. Ten-word shingles, every pair across the 59 preceding entries and the 59 entries of the category, $59\times59$ pairs: the largest overlap is $4.2\%$, between the obstructions catalogue and the paravectors article, and the median is under $1\%$. Shared vocabulary and shared results, yes; shared sentences, no. Nothing here is a copy that deleting one would fix. The overlaps are conceptual, and a conceptual overlap is answered by order and by a pointer, not by deletion.

The Rule

A violation is a defect of the text, not a reason to move the file.

This is the rule that took two attempts to see. An article that belongs to block 2 by subject but speaks in four-vector language is not a material article. It is a general article whose wording has not caught up with its place. Moving it to block 3 hides the defect; rewriting it removes it.

Three operations, in this order of preference.

  1. Reframe. The mathematics is usually already sector-blind; the vocabulary is not. Rewrite the framing, the order of exposition and the nouns. Keep the proofs, the counts and the worked numbers. This is the default and it fixes most cases.
  2. Move a section. When one part of the article exists only because of one sector, lift that part out and give it to the block that owns it. The article keeps its subject and loses a section; the receiving block gains a section or an article.
  3. Split. When the moved part is large enough to stand alone, it becomes an article in the receiving block. Use this only when option 2 leaves both halves too thin.

Splitting the file is the last resort. It costs a slug, a menu entry, a set of backlinks and one more title to keep distinct.

Things to Take Care Of

1. The word trace belongs to a matrix. For an element of $\mathbb{B}$ the reading is $2\,\mathrm{Sc}\,\tilde Q$; Sc alone is the reading. Never write the trace of the rotor. The same applies to the derived wording: pinned is fixed, the re-pin family is projected, projection, and nothing else.

2. Do not write that the group acts on states. It acts projectively. A state is $\tfrac12(1,\vec s)$, normalised by $2\,\mathrm{Sc}=1$. A boost sends that number to $\cosh\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}+\sinh\theta_{\mathrm{rpd}}s_{\parallel}$, so the image leaves the slice and the return to the slice is a division, not a group action. Every sentence about the informational sector carries the word projective or it is false.

3. Never create a band of one entry. A subsection with a single article is a table-of-contents entry pretending to be a division. This is why the four kinds of transformation sit with the decompositions rather than alone, and why the comparison shares a band with the sector map and the tensor question.

4. Full-string replacement needs an assertion. A short title is often also a section name elsewhere in the corpus. Replacing The Matrix Model by hand once rewrote The Matrix Models in two maths articles that share the phrase as a section title. Every replacement is issued with a count assertion, and the count is one or the edit is refused.

5. The physics title is not the maths title. From the Spin to the Carriers and the Closures was the display title of both a physics article and a maths article. The physics one was renamed; the maths one was not, and its seventeen references had to be left reading the old title. Before any rename, check whether the phrase names two articles.

6. A slug rename breaks every inbound link that is not in this corpus. There are no redirects. The sitemap is regenerated and the deployed URLs are not. Shorten a proposed slug before using it: the long ones in this batch run past seventy characters and are hard to say, hard to cite and easy to mistype. Check that the new slug exists nowhere before renaming.

7. Move the file with git mv. A copy plus a delete loses the history. A rename is a rename.

8. A merge has a rule. The surviving article must contain the merged content before the other file is deleted, and its introduction must say where that content came from. Deleting first and intending to merge later loses the material.

9. The four Focus on informational aspects blocks stay. Each quantum category closes with one. That is the corpus's existing way of saying the other sector, and it is repeated four times on purpose. An article moved out of such a block leaves a one-line pointer, not a hole.

10. Build and check after every batch, not at the end. The check walks the deploy tree and resolves every href against the filesystem. Two known false positives are excluded from the count: article_template.html, which points at {root}index.html, and index_template.html. Two known breakages are excluded as pre-existing and unrelated: the nineteen dead relative links in expressing-lie-algebras-via-biquaternions.md, and the four links to the-carrier-of-the-*-field in quantum-relativist-audit.md and in a copy file.

11. Leave the copies and the reserve alone. articles_internal/* (copy 1).md and _reserve/ are not published, are not built, and must not receive replacements. A global scan that includes them reports breakage that no reader can see.

12. One name per article, and the name says the subject. A title that names only the object tells a reader nothing about which sector the article belongs to or that it compares two. A title that carries the plain word comparison is worth more than a title that is elegant.

14. A merge costs seventy-five links, and every one is in prose. Merging Why AXF Matrices into The AXF Matrices deleted a page that seventy-five articles cited: some with a bracketed label in Further Reading, some in italics inside a sentence. Every one had to be repointed to the-axf-matrices-axis-free-integer-entries.html#why-the-pauli-and-the-gamma-displays-are-refused, and both the label and the italic title had to change, or the corpus would cite a page that no longer exists. Two lessons. Give the receiving section a stable heading before the merge, so the anchor exists when the links are written. And grep for the three forms separately — bare slug, bracketed label, italic title — because a replacement that catches one of the three looks finished and is not.

13. A count in a note is a claim. Every block count here was checked against the menu by script, not by eye. Two earlier drafts were wrong by one, for the reason a reader would guess: a merged article counted twice, and a band counted as four when it holds three.
