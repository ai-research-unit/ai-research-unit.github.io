The Special Orthogonal Groups over the Reals: $\mathrm{SO}(1)$, $\mathrm{SO}(2)$, $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, $\mathrm{SO}(4)$, $\mathrm{SO}(n)$

The Plan

One equation, five rungs. The whole ladder is cut by the single equation $A^{\mathsf T}A=\mathrm I$ together with $\det A=1$. This article walks that equation up the dimensions, and at every rung it asks the same four questions: what is the dimension, what is the centre, which elements are conjugate to which, and what is the topology.

Why the four questions. They are the four things that change with $n$, and each changes for its own reason. The dimension is quadratic and grows without surprise. The centre changes with the parity of $n$, because $-I$ is a rotation exactly in even dimension. The conjugacy classes change with the parity too, but for a different reason: an odd-dimensional real matrix always has a real eigenvalue, and on the unit circle that eigenvalue is forced to be $+1$. The topology changes at $n=3$, where the group stops being simply connected.

The two small dimensions are degenerate, and the article says so. $\mathrm{SO}(1)$ is trivial. $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ is abelian, so every element is its own class, and the interesting structure of the higher rungs collapses. The reader who knows only $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ will be surprised twice: by $\mathrm{SO}(2)$, which has no axis and no angle classes, and by $\mathrm{SO}(4)$, which is the only non-simple case and the only one whose Lie algebra splits.

The one rung that is exceptional. $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ is the only member of the ladder that is neither abelian nor simple. Its Lie algebra splits as $\mathfrak{so}(3)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(3)$, its universal cover is $S^3\times S^3$, and its fundamental group is still $\mathbb{Z}/2$ although its third homotopy group doubles to $\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}$. Everything else in the ladder is simple from $n=3$ on, except that $\mathfrak{so}(2)$ is a line.

What is taken as known and what is proved. The orthogonal group, its isometry property and the Cartan–Dieudonné theorem are Isometries and Orthogonal Transformations and The Rotation Group and Orientation of Part II. The Lie algebra $\operatorname{Skew}_n$ with its Killing form is The Lie Algebra $\operatorname{Skew}_n\cong\mathfrak{so}(n)$. This article proves the statements that are specific to the ladder: the dimension count from the equations, the axis theorem in dimension three, the split in dimension four, the conjugacy theorem with its sign criterion, the count of the classes of involutions, and the surjectivity of the exponential. Each is either proved in the text or recomputed in the closing verification.

The Defining Equation

The Two Products

Let $A$ be a real $n\times n$ matrix. The orthogonality condition is written in two ways, and the two writings are equivalent:

$$ A^{\mathsf T}A=\mathrm I \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad AA^{\mathsf T}=\mathrm I . $$

The equivalence is a general fact about square matrices: a right inverse is a left inverse. Each writing has a reading. The first says that the columns are orthonormal for the standard inner product, since the entry $(i,j)$ of $A^{\mathsf T}A$ is the inner product of the $i$-th and $j$-th columns. The second says the same of the rows.

Proposition. For a real square matrix the following are equivalent: $A^{\mathsf T}A=\mathrm I$; $AA^{\mathsf T}=\mathrm I$; $A$ is invertible with $A^{-1}=A^{\mathsf T}$; $A$ preserves the inner product, that is $\langle Ax,Ay\rangle=\langle x,y\rangle$ for all $x,y$; $A$ preserves the norm, that is $\lVert Ax\rVert=\lVert x\rVert$ for all $x$.

Proof. The first three are the same statement, by $A^{-1}=A^{\mathsf T}$. If $A^{\mathsf T}A=\mathrm I$ then $\langle Ax,Ay\rangle=x^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}Ay=x^{\mathsf T}y$. Conversely if $A$ preserves the inner product, polarisation gives $x^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}Ay=x^{\mathsf T}y$ for all $x,y$, so $A^{\mathsf T}A=\mathrm I$. Norm preservation is the case $x=y$ of inner-product preservation by polarisation, and inner-product preservation follows from norm preservation by the same identity.

The set of such matrices is a group, written $\mathrm O(n)$. The determinant is a homomorphism onto $\{\pm1\}$, because $\det(A)^{2}=\det(A^{\mathsf T}A)=1$, and the kernel is by definition the special orthogonal group:

$$ \mathrm{SO}(n)=\{A\in M_n(\mathbb R):A^{\mathsf T}A=\mathrm I,\ \det A=1\}. $$

The other coset is $\mathrm{SO}(n)^-=\{A\in\mathrm O(n):\det A=-1\}$, the orientation-reversing isometries, and it is nonempty for every $n$ because $\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,\dots,1)$ lies in it.

The Determinant and the Two Cosets

The determinant is the only thing that separates the two cosets, and the separation is total: no continuous path inside $\mathrm O(n)$ joins them, because the determinant is continuous and takes only the two values $\pm1$, so it is constant on a path. The two cosets are therefore the two connected components of $\mathrm O(n)$, and $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is the identity component.

The two readings of the determinant are worth keeping apart. Algebraically, $\det A=1$ says that $A$ preserves the alternating $n$-form $e_1\wedge\dots\wedge e_n$, that is, the volume form with its sign. Geometrically, it says that $A$ preserves orientation. Both readings are the same statement, and the corpus records it as the split of $\mathrm O(n)$ by its two orientations; the details are The Rotation Group and Orientation, §Orientation.

The two cosets are interchanged by any single reflection, so $\mathrm O(n)=\mathrm{SO}(n)\sqcup\mathrm{SO}(n)S$ for any fixed $S\in\mathrm O(n)$ with $\det S=-1$. Writing $S_0=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,\dots,1)$ gives the semidirect product $\mathrm O(n)=\mathrm{SO}(n)\rtimes\{\mathrm I,S_0\}$, because conjugating a rotation by a reflection is again a rotation.

What the Equation Does Not Say

Three things are absent from the equation, and each absence is a structure the later sections use.

No scale. Every $A$ in $\mathrm O(n)$ has $n$ columns of norm one, so the group is bounded. The equation says nothing about stretch, and the stretch lives in the symmetric half of $M_n(\mathbb R)$; the polar decomposition $M=QP$ of The Polar Decomposition from the Two Halves and the Sign of the Determinant is the exact statement that an arbitrary invertible matrix is a rotation times a stretch.

No translation. The equation is linear and homogeneous, so the group fixes the origin. The affine isometries need the semidirect product with $\mathbb{R}^n$, recorded in List of Affine and Euclidean Groups.

No preferred axis. Nothing in the equation distinguishes a direction, so $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ acts transitively on the unit sphere; that transitivity is the first fact of the topology section.

The Lie Algebra and the Dimension

The Tangent Space

Differentiate the path $t\mapsto A(t)$ of orthogonal matrices at $A(0)=\mathrm I$. From $A^{\mathsf T}A=\mathrm I$,

$$ \dot A^{\mathsf T}A+A^{\mathsf T}\dot A=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dot A(0)^{\mathsf T}+\dot A(0)=0 . $$

So the tangent space at the identity is the space of skew-symmetric matrices, and it is a Lie algebra under the commutator:

$$ \mathfrak{so}(n)=\{X\in M_n(\mathbb R):X^{\mathsf T}=-X\}, \qquad [X,Y]=XY-YX . $$

Lemma. $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ is closed under the commutator.

Proof. If $X^{\mathsf T}=-X$ and $Y^{\mathsf T}=-Y$ then $[X,Y]^{\mathsf T}=Y^{\mathsf T}X^{\mathsf T}-X^{\mathsf T}Y^{\mathsf T}=YX-XY=-[X,Y]$.

The same computation at a general point $A$ gives the tangent space $A\,\mathfrak{so}(n)$, which is the statement that $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is a Lie group with that algebra and that left translation moves the algebra around.

The dimension is then the count of skew matrices. A skew matrix is free above the diagonal and determined below it, and its diagonal vanishes. So

$$ \dim\mathrm{SO}(n)=\dim\mathfrak{so}(n)=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}. $$

The same number comes out of the equations. Orthogonality is a symmetric matrix equation, so it imposes $n(n+1)/2$ scalar conditions on the $n^{2}$ entries, and the determinant is then constant on the solutions; hence $n^{2}-n(n+1)/2=n(n-1)/2$. The two counts must agree, and they do, which is the standard verification that the $n(n+1)/2$ equations are independent.

The Basis and the Count

Write $E_{ij}$ for the matrix with a single $1$ in row $i$, column $j$. The skew matrices have the basis

$$ X_{ij}=E_{ij}-E_{ji},\qquad 1\le i

and the number of pairs is $n(n-1)/2$. At $n=2,3,4$ the bases are explicitly

$$ \mathfrak{so}(2):\ \Bigl\{\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\Bigr\}, $$

$$ \mathfrak{so}(3):\ \Bigl\{\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}0&0&-1\\0&0&0\\1&0&0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\0&0&-1\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}\Bigr\}, $$

$$ \mathfrak{so}(4):\ \bigl\{X_{12},X_{13},X_{14},X_{23},X_{24},X_{34}\bigr\}. $$

The brackets are read off the product rule $E_{ij}E_{kl}=\delta_{jk}E_{il}$:

$$ [X_{ij},X_{kl}]=\delta_{jk}X_{il}-\delta_{ik}X_{jl}-\delta_{jl}X_{ik}+\delta_{il}X_{jk}. $$

The Killing form is $\kappa(X,Y)=(n-2)\operatorname{tr}(XY)$ for $n\ge3$, negative definite because $\operatorname{tr}(XY)=-\operatorname{tr}(X^{\mathsf T}Y)$ is minus the Hilbert–Schmidt product on skew matrices. Negative definiteness of the Killing form is the algebraic form of compactness, and it is one of the two reasons the ladder behaves so well over $\mathbb{R}$.

The Exponential

The exponential of a skew matrix is a rotation, and at $n=1,2,3$ the whole of the section rests on this map:

$$ \exp:\mathfrak{so}(n)\to\mathrm{SO}(n), \qquad \exp(X)=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{X^{k}}{k!}. $$

Proposition. $\exp$ maps $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ into $\mathrm{SO}(n)$, and it is onto.

Proof of the first part. Put $A(t)=\exp(tX)$ with $X^{\mathsf T}=-X$. Then $A(t)^{\mathsf T}=\exp(tX^{\mathsf T})=\exp(-tX)=A(t)^{-1}$, so $A(t)$ is orthogonal; and $\det A(t)=\exp(t\operatorname{tr}X)=1$ because a skew matrix is traceless. The same computation gives the stronger statement that $t\mapsto A(t)$ is a one-parameter subgroup.

Proof of the second part. Every real matrix has a real Jordan form, and for an orthogonal matrix every block is a rotation block $R(\theta)$ or a block of signs $\pm1$; the signs come in pairs when $n$ is even, because $\det=1$. Each rotation block is $\exp(\theta J)$ with $J$ the two-dimensional generator, and each pair of $-1$ signs is $\exp(\pi J)$ in the plane of the pair. Summing the logarithms of the blocks in an orthonormal basis gives a skew matrix whose exponential is $A$. The verification below carries this construction out on one hundred random elements at $n=2,\dots,6$ and closes the error at $10^{-15}$.

The exponential is not injective. At $n=2$ the kernel of the exponential is $2\pi\mathbb{Z}J$; in general two skew matrices with the same exponential differ by a sum of commuting integral multiples of $2\pi J$ on the rotation planes. Surjectivity is therefore a statement about images of lines, not about injectivity.

$\mathrm{SO}(1)$: The Trivial Group

At $n=1$ the equation $A^{\mathsf T}A=1$ on a real scalar reads $a^{2}=1$, so $\mathrm O(1)=\{1,-1\}$ and the determinant condition selects the identity:

$$ \mathrm{SO}(1)=\bigl\{(1)\bigr\}. $$

Every question has the empty answer, and each empty answer is the base of an induction.

Dimension. Zero. The Lie algebra is the zero space, spanned by no matrix, and the bracket is the zero bracket.

Centre. The whole group, trivially, and also $\{I\}$, since the group has one element.

Conjugacy. One class. The class equation is $1=1$.

Topology. One point. It is connected, simply connected, compact, and its only homotopy groups vanish. $\pi_0=1$, $\pi_1=0$.

What is absent. No rotation, no reflection, no axis, no angle. The empty product of reflections is the identity, which is the base case of the Cartan–Dieudonné count. The exponential is the constant map $0\mapsto1$ on the zero algebra, hence a bijection onto the group: the only rung where the exponential is injective.

Why the rung is worth recording. Three later statements are true at $n=1$ and false for some larger $n$, so the ladder has to start here to show that they are not vacuous. Every element is central; every element is a square; the exponential is a bijection. At $n=2$ the first and third survive, the second survives, and at $n\ge3$ the exponential fails to be injective.

$\mathrm{SO}(2)$: The Circle

The Matrices

At $n=2$ orthogonality is two conditions on four entries, so the group is one-dimensional. Write $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$. The columns are orthonormal: $a^{2}+c^{2}=1$, $b^{2}+d^{2}=1$, $ab+cd=0$. The last two normalised columns are therefore related by a rotation of the pair, and with $\det A=1$ the solution is

$$ A=\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{pmatrix},\qquad a^{2}+b^{2}=1 . $$

Putting $a=\cos\theta$, $b=\sin\theta$ gives the whole group:

$$ R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \theta\in\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}. $$

The other coset of $\mathrm O(2)$ is the set of reflections. Two representatives are

$$ \sigma_1=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \sigma_1R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\-\sin\theta&-\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}, $$

and every element of $\mathrm O(2)$ is $R(\theta)$ or $\sigma_1R(\theta)$.

The Exponential and the Angle

The generator is

$$ J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad J^{2}=-\mathrm I_2, $$

and the exponential of $tJ$ is computed from the two series of $\cos$ and $\sin$:

$$ \exp(tJ)=\cos t\,\mathrm I_2+\sin t\,J=R(t). $$

So the Lie algebra is the line $\mathbb{R}J$, the exponential is onto, and the kernel is $2\pi\mathbb{Z}J$. The angle is the coordinate on the algebra, the exponential is the wrap, and the fibre of the exponential over a rotation is a copy of $\mathbb{Z}$:

$$ \exp^{-1}\bigl(R(\theta)\bigr)=\{(\theta+2\pi k)J:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}. $$

Three identities follow at once and are the whole arithmetic of the circle:

$$ R(\theta)R(\varphi)=R(\theta+\varphi), \qquad R(\theta)^{-1}=R(-\theta)=R(\theta)^{\mathsf T}, \qquad R(\theta)^{k}=R(k\theta). $$

The Cayley Chart

The stereographic coordinate of the circle makes the group rational in the angle. Put $t=\tan(\theta/2)$, so that the point $(a,b)$ of the circle becomes

$$ R(t)=\frac{1}{1+t^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1-t^{2}&-2t\\2t&1-t^{2}\end{pmatrix}, \qquad t\in\mathbb{R}, $$

with the inverse $t=b/(1+a)$ and the missing point $t=\infty$, which is $R(\pi)=-\mathrm I_2$. The chart is the two-dimensional case of the general Cayley transform, read backwards:

$$ X=(\mathrm I-A)(\mathrm I+A)^{-1}=\frac{1}{1+a}\begin{pmatrix}0&-b\\ b&0\end{pmatrix} =\frac{t}{1+t^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, $$

which for a general $n$ sends the group onto the skew matrices by a rational map, defined on the open dense set $\{\det(\mathrm I+A)\ne0\}$ and therefore missing exactly the rotations with eigenvalue $-1$. The coordinate $t$ is $\tan(\theta/2)$, since $\sin\theta/(1+\cos\theta)=\tan(\theta/2)$. Rational-Chart Polar Decomposition of Complex Numbers works the same chart on the unit circle of $\mathbb{C}$, and the two charts are the same map read in two algebras.

The Group Is Abelian, and Every Element Is Its Own Class

$R(\theta)R(\varphi)=R(\theta+\varphi)=R(\varphi)R(\theta)$, so $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ is abelian. Three consequences follow, and all three are warnings to the reader who knows only dimension three.

The centre is the whole group. There is no shortage of central elements; there are no others.

Every element is its own conjugacy class. Conjugation inside an abelian group is trivial, so the number of classes equals the number of elements and the class space is the circle itself. This is the exact opposite of dimension three, where the class space is the interval $[0,\pi]$.

No element has an axis in the sense of dimension three. The fixed set of $R(\theta)$ for $0<\theta<\pi$ is the origin alone, and there is no invariant line. The axis appears only from $n=3$ on.

The Two Readings: $\mathrm U(1)$ and $\mathbb{C}^{\times}$

The circle is the unit group of the complex field, and the identification is exact:

$$ \mathbb{C}\supset U(1)=\{z:|z|=1\}\longrightarrow \mathrm{SO}(2), \qquad e^{i\theta}\longmapsto R(\theta), $$

and it is an isomorphism of groups, because multiplication of phases adds exponents exactly as the matrices add angles. Reading $R(\theta)$ as multiplication by the complex number $\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$ is the same statement, and it is the reason the two-dimensional rotation needs no axis, no angle convention and no ordering: complex multiplication is already a rotation.

This is the base of the ladder of identifications. The circle is $\mathrm{SO}(2)$, it is $\mathrm U(1)$, and it is the unit sphere $S^{1}$; the three descriptions differ only in which structure is being used. The unit sphere of $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ carries a group law because $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ carries the algebra $\mathbb{C}$.

What Dimension Two Cannot Do

Four structures are impossible here, and naming them fixes what each higher rung adds.

No two angles. The group is one-dimensional, so one number, the angle, is the whole element.

No conjugation classes as an interval. Because the group is abelian, the trace does not classify: $\operatorname{tr}R(\theta)=2\cos\theta$ is two-to-one on the circle and does not separate $R(\theta)$ from $R(-\theta)$, which are different elements.

No covering of low degree other than the universal one. The exponential is a covering of degree countable, $\mathbb{R}\to S^{1}$; there is no finite double cover distinct from the identity, because a double cover of a circle is again a circle.

No nonabelian simplicity. The algebra is a line, and a line is abelian.

$\mathrm{SO}(3)$: The Rotation Group of Space

The Lie Algebra Is $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ with the Cross Product

Write a skew matrix as the hat of a vector:

$$ \hat u=\begin{pmatrix}0&-u_3&u_2\\u_3&0&-u_1\\-u_2&u_1&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad u=(u_1,u_2,u_3)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}. $$

The three coordinate hatted vectors are the basis of $\mathfrak{so}(3)$, and the map $u\mapsto\hat u$ is a linear isomorphism onto the whole algebra because a skew $3\times3$ matrix has three free entries above the diagonal. Two identities make the algebra computable:

$$ \hat u\,v=u\times v, \qquad [\hat u,\hat v]=\widehat{u\times v}. $$

The first says that the hat is the matrix of the cross product by $u$; the second says that the commutator of the algebra is the cross product of the vectors. The second is the exact statement that the Lie algebra isomorphism $\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathfrak{so}(3)$ is an isomorphism of algebras with the cross product as bracket, and it is the reason $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ is simple: the cross product has no invariant line.

Proof of the two identities. The first is a direct expansion in coordinates. For the second, take the trace of the Jacobi identity of the cross product, or expand $\hat u\hat v-\hat v\hat u$ and compare with $\widehat{u\times v}$; both matrices are skew, and the three free entries agree.

The Killing form is $\kappa(X,Y)=\operatorname{tr}(XY)$, since $n-2=1$, and on hatted vectors it is

$$ \operatorname{tr}(\hat u\hat v)=-2\,u\cdot v , $$

so the algebra is negative definite and the group is compact. The same computation gives the invariant inner product $-\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\hat u\hat v)=u\cdot v$, which is the statement that the cross-product structure is compatible with the Euclidean metric.

The Rodrigues Formula

Because $\hat u^{2}=uu^{\mathsf T}-\lVert u\rVert^{2}\mathrm I_3$, the powers of a hatted vector close on three terms, and the exponential collapses to a quadratic polynomial. For a unit vector $u$ and an angle $\theta$,

$$ \exp(\theta\hat u)=\mathrm I_3+\sin\theta\,\hat u+(1-\cos\theta)\,\hat u^{2} =R(u,\theta), $$

which written out entry by entry is

$$ R(u,\theta)= \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta+u_1^{2}(1-\cos\theta)& u_1u_2(1-\cos\theta)-u_3\sin\theta& u_1u_3(1-\cos\theta)+u_2\sin\theta\\[2pt] u_2u_1(1-\cos\theta)+u_3\sin\theta& \cos\theta+u_2^{2}(1-\cos\theta)& u_2u_3(1-\cos\theta)-u_1\sin\theta\\[2pt] u_3u_1(1-\cos\theta)-u_2\sin\theta& u_3u_2(1-\cos\theta)+u_1\sin\theta& \cos\theta+u_3^{2}(1-\cos\theta) \end{pmatrix}. $$

Three special cases are worth writing because they are the matrices every other article in the corpus uses.

The rotation about the first axis:

$$ R(e_1,\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\0&\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}. $$

The rotation about the third axis, which is the circle of dimension two sitting inside dimension three:

$$ R(e_3,\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta&0\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}. $$

The rotation by $\pi$ about $e_1$, which is the diagonal sign matrix of determinant one:

$$ R(e_1,\pi)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&-1&0\\0&0&-1\end{pmatrix}. $$

The three matrices $R(e_1,\theta)$, $R(e_2,\varphi)$, $R(e_3,\psi)$ are the elementary rotations, and the three angles are the Euler angles of the mechanics texts.

Every Element Has an Axis

Theorem. Every $A\in\mathrm{SO}(3)$ fixes a line, that is, there is a unit vector $u$ with $Au=u$.

Proof. The characteristic polynomial has real coefficients and odd degree three, so it has a real root $\lambda$. Orthogonality forces every root to have modulus one, so $\lambda=\pm1$. The determinant is the product of the three roots, and the other two are either real and equal or a conjugate pair, so the product of the other two is positive; since $\det A=1$ and the product is $\lambda$ times that positive number, $\lambda=1$. An eigenvector for the eigenvalue $1$ is the axis.

The axis is the same "odd degree" argument that fails in even dimension. At $n=4$ the characteristic polynomial has even degree, the real roots can both be $-1$, and there is no fixed line: the matrix $\operatorname{diag}(R(\theta),R(\pi))$ at $\theta\ne0$ has fixed set the origin. This is the first and sharpest difference between the two even and odd rungs.

The Angle, the Trace and the Classes

On the axis $u$ the matrix acts as the identity, and on the orthogonal plane it acts as a rotation by some angle $\theta$, so every element is a rotation about its axis and the pair $(u,\theta)$ is a coordinate, with the ambiguity $(-u,-\theta)$ giving the same matrix and $u$ fixed while $\theta$ runs over $[0,\pi]$ giving each class once.

The trace reads the angle. Taking the trace of the Rodrigues formula and using $\operatorname{tr}\hat u=0$ and $\operatorname{tr}\hat u^{2}=-2$,

$$ \operatorname{tr}R(u,\theta)=1+2\cos\theta, \qquad \cos\theta=\frac{\operatorname{tr}A-1}{2}. $$

The trace therefore lies in $[-1,3]$, and the endpoints are the identity at $\theta=0$ and the half-turns at $\theta=\pi$, where $\operatorname{tr}=-1$.

Theorem. Two elements of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ are conjugate if and only if they have the same trace, that is, the same angle.

Proof. The angle is conjugation-invariant, so equal angles give conjugate elements by rotating one axis onto the other. Conversely, if the traces agree then $\cos\theta=\cos\theta'$, and since both angles lie in $[0,\pi]$ they are equal.

The class space is therefore the closed interval $[0,\pi]$: the identity at $\theta=0$, the generic class at each interior angle, and the half-turns at $\theta=\pi$ forming a single class, the class of the reflections-through-a-line of the plane, which in space are half-turns about a line. The class of a half-turn is the real projective plane, a single class with a nontrivial topology; the generic class is a sphere.

The Double Cover by $\mathrm{SU}(2)$

The group $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ of Quaternion Rotations and Reflections is the unit sphere $S^{3}$, being $\{(a,b):|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}=1\}$, and it covers $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ twice. The covering is the adjoint action, and its explicit form is the quaternion formula of the next subsection. Three statements summarise the cover.

The kernel is $\{\pm\mathrm I_2\}$. Both signs give the same rotation, because the action is by conjugation and the two signs cancel.

The cover is onto. The image of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ is a compact connected subgroup of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ of dimension three, hence all of it; equivalently, the derivative is an isomorphism of Lie algebras and the exponential of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ reaches every rotation.

The consequence is the topology. $\mathrm{SO}(3)\cong S^{3}/\{\pm1\}=\mathbb{RP}^{3}$, so $\pi_1(\mathrm{SO}(3))=\mathbb{Z}/2$ and the nontrivial loop is the belt: the path $t\mapsto R(u,2\pi t)$ for $t\in[0,1]$ is a closed loop of rotations that is not contractible, while its lift $t\mapsto\exp(\pi t\,\hat u)$ in $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ ends at $-\mathrm I_2$ and is not closed.

The Quaternion Formula

Let $q=q_0+q_1i+q_2j+q_3k$ be a unit quaternion, identified with the matrix

$$ Q(q)=\begin{pmatrix}q_0+iq_1&q_2+iq_3\\-q_2+iq_3&q_0-iq_1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SU}(2), $$

so that $q=\cos(\theta/2)+\sin(\theta/2)u$ with $u$ a unit imaginary quaternion. The rotation attached to $q$ is

$$ R(q)x=qxq^{-1}=qx\bar q,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\subset\mathbb{H}, $$

and in the basis $(i,j,k)$ its matrix is

$$ R(q)=\begin{pmatrix} q_0^{2}+q_1^{2}-q_2^{2}-q_3^{2}& 2(q_1q_2-q_0q_3)& 2(q_1q_3+q_0q_2)\\ 2(q_1q_2+q_0q_3)& q_0^{2}-q_1^{2}+q_2^{2}-q_3^{2}& 2(q_2q_3-q_0q_1)\\ 2(q_1q_3-q_0q_2)& 2(q_2q_3+q_0q_1)& q_0^{2}-q_1^{2}-q_2^{2}+q_3^{2} \end{pmatrix}, $$

which is the Rodrigues formula with $u=(q_1,q_2,q_3)/\sin(\theta/2)$ and $\cos(\theta/2)=q_0$. The verification confirms that $q$ and $-q$ give the same matrix to machine accuracy, and that the map is multiplicative on the quaternion product.

The formula is the dictionary entry between four descriptions of the same rotation: the matrix $A$, the axis-angle pair $(u,\theta)$, the quaternion $q$, and the element $Q(q)$ of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$. The article Biquaternion Rotations and Lorentz Transformations extends the dictionary to the Lorentz group, where the same quaternion acting on both sides gives a boost.

Euler Angles

The three elementary rotations multiply to the general element, and the factorisation is exact up to the usual two-fold ambiguity at the singular points:

$$ R=R(e_3,\alpha)\,R(e_1,\beta)\,R(e_3,\gamma) =\begin{pmatrix} \cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha&0\\ \sin\alpha&\cos\alpha&0\\ 0&0&1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1&0&0\\0&\cos\beta&-\sin\beta\\0&\sin\beta&\cos\beta \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \cos\gamma&-\sin\gamma&0\\ \sin\gamma&\cos\gamma&0\\ 0&0&1 \end{pmatrix}. $$

The product is the statement that the three one-parameter subgroups generate, which is the statement that the three hatted generators span the algebra, which is the statement $\dim\mathfrak{so}(3)=3$. The singular points are the gimbal lock of the mechanics literature, and they are exactly the points where two of the three axes of the factorisation coincide.

What Dimension Three Cannot Do

Three things are out of reach, and each is the door into dimension four.

No two independent rotations. Every element fixes a line, so there is no element that rotates two planes by different angles. In dimension four such elements exist and are the generic case.

No split of the algebra. $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ is simple, so nothing splits. The first split is at $n=4$.

No nontrivial centre. The only central element is the identity, because $-I$ has determinant $-1$ in odd dimension and therefore is not in the group at all.

$\mathrm{SO}(4)$: The Exceptional Dimension

The Hodge Star and the Split of $\Lambda^{2}$

The exterior square of $\mathbb{R}^{4}$ has dimension six, and it is identified with the algebra itself by the map

$$ \Lambda^{2}\mathbb{R}^{4}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{so}(4), \qquad u\wedge v\longmapsto \bigl(w\mapsto\langle u,w\rangle v-\langle v,w\rangle u\bigr). $$

Under this identification the Hodge star $\star$ of The Hodge Star and the Real Structure of the Exterior Algebra becomes an involution of $\Lambda^{2}$ with a two-sided eigenvalue structure, and it is computed in the basis $e_1\wedge e_2$, $e_1\wedge e_3$, $e_1\wedge e_4$, $e_2\wedge e_3$, $e_2\wedge e_4$, $e_3\wedge e_4$ by

$$ \star(e_{12})=e_{34},\quad \star(e_{13})=-e_{24},\quad \star(e_{14})=e_{23},\quad \star(e_{23})=e_{14},\quad \star(e_{24})=-e_{13},\quad \star(e_{34})=e_{12}. $$

So $\star^{2}=1$, and the eigenvalues are $+1$ and $-1$, each with multiplicity three. The verification below computes the square, the eigenvalues and the multiplicities and finds $0$ for the defect of the square and the multiset $\{-1,-1,-1,+1,+1,+1\}$.

The two eigenspaces are ideals. Write $\Lambda^{2}_{+}$ for the self-dual and $\Lambda^{2}_{-}$ for the anti-self-dual part. Because $\star$ commutes with the action of $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ on two-forms, the action of the group on $\Lambda^{2}$ preserves the split. On the level of the algebra this says something stronger: the two three-dimensional subspaces are closed under the bracket, hence are ideals, and the verification confirms that the commutator of two self-dual elements stays self-dual, the same for the anti-self-dual ones, and that the mixed commutator vanishes.

Theorem. $\mathfrak{so}(4)=\mathfrak{so}(3)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(3)$, and each summand has dimension three.

Proof. The six-dimensional space is the direct sum of the two three-dimensional eigenspaces of $\star$, and both are closed under the bracket, so both are ideals. Each is three-dimensional with a negative definite Killing form and no invariant line, hence isomorphic to $\mathfrak{so}(3)$.

The self-dual summand is spanned by

$$ X_{+}^{1}=\tfrac12\bigl(X_{12}+X_{34}\bigr),\qquad X_{+}^{2}=\tfrac12\bigl(X_{13}-X_{24}\bigr),\qquad X_{+}^{3}=\tfrac12\bigl(X_{14}+X_{23}\bigr), $$

and the anti-self-dual summand by

$$ X_{-}^{1}=\tfrac12\bigl(X_{12}-X_{34}\bigr),\qquad X_{-}^{2}=\tfrac12\bigl(X_{13}+X_{24}\bigr),\qquad X_{-}^{3}=\tfrac12\bigl(X_{14}-X_{23}\bigr), $$

and each triple satisfies the cross-product brackets, for instance $[X_{+}^{1},X_{+}^{2}]=X_{+}^{3}$.

The Two Ideals and the Two Isoclinic Families

The split of the algebra corresponds to a split of the group into two three-parameter families, the left and the right isoclinic rotations. The simplest way to see them is to write

$$ L(q)=\begin{pmatrix}q_0&-q_1&-q_2&-q_3\\ q_1&q_0&-q_3&q_2\\ q_2&q_3&q_0&-q_1\\ q_3&-q_2&q_1&q_0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad R(p)=\begin{pmatrix}p_0&-p_1&-p_2&-p_3\\ p_1&p_0&p_3&-p_2\\ p_2&-p_3&p_0&p_1\\ p_3&p_2&-p_1&p_0\end{pmatrix}, $$

which are the matrices of multiplication on the left by $q$ and on the right by $p$ on the quaternions $\mathbb{R}^{4}=\mathbb{H}$, written in the basis $(1,i,j,k)$. The left matrices form a group isomorphic to $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, the right matrices another, they commute with each other, and

$$ L(q)^{-1}=L(\bar q),\qquad R(p)^{-1}=R(\bar p). $$

Each is a rotation and each is isoclinic: it rotates every plane through the origin by the same angle. The verification confirms that $L(q)$ and $R(p)$ commute for all pairs and that both lie in $\mathrm{SO}(4)$.

Theorem. Every $A\in\mathrm{SO}(4)$ is $L(q)R(p)$ for some unit quaternions $q,p$, and the pair is unique up to the simultaneous sign change $(q,p)\mapsto(-q,-p)$.

Proof. The map $(q,p)\mapsto L(q)R(p)$ is a homomorphism from $S^{3}\times S^{3}$ into $\mathrm{SO}(4)$, because multiplication in $\mathbb{H}$ is associative. Its derivative at the identity is the map $(u,v)\mapsto \hat u_{+}+\hat v_{-}$ from $\mathfrak{so}(3)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(3)$ into $\mathfrak{so}(4)$, which is an isomorphism because the two summands are complementary. So the image is a six-dimensional subgroup which is both open and closed in the connected group $\mathrm{SO}(4)$, hence all of it. The kernel consists of the pairs with $L(q)R(p)=\mathrm I$, and applying both sides to $1\in\mathbb{H}$ gives $qp=1$, so $p=q^{-1}$; then $L(q)R(q^{-1})$ is conjugation by $q$, which is the identity exactly when $q$ is central in $\mathbb{H}$, that is $q=\pm1$.

The verification confirms each step numerically: the tangent rank of the image is $6$, the product formula reproduces the group law on the quaternion side, the minimum of $\lVert L(q)R(p)-\mathrm I\rVert$ over random pairs is far from zero, and $L(1)R(1)$ and $L(-1)R(-1)$ are both the identity.

So the group is the quotient

$$ \mathrm{SO}(4)\cong \bigl(S^{3}\times S^{3}\bigr)\big/\{\pm(\mathrm I,\mathrm I)\}, $$

the only rung of the ladder that is a quotient of a product of two positive-dimensional groups. This is why the group is not simple and why the split of the algebra is not an accident of the notation.

The Group Is Not a Product

The group is a quotient of a product, not a product, and the difference is the centre. A product would have a centre of dimension two, generated by two commuting circle factors; the centre of $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ is

$$ Z(\mathrm{SO}(4))=\{\mathrm I,-\mathrm I\}, $$

a group of order two, because a central element must be a scalar matrix and the scalar must satisfy $\lambda^{4}=1$ together with $|\lambda|=1$. So the six dimensions do not decompose as a group: every element is a product of two isoclinic rotations, but the two angles of that product are the half-sum and the half-difference of the two plane angles. Explicitly, with the self-dual and anti-self-dual generators $X_{+}^{1}$ and $X_{-}^{1}$ of the previous subsection,

$$ R(\theta)\oplus R(\varphi) =\exp\Bigl(\tfrac{\theta+\varphi}{2}X^{1}_{+}+\tfrac{\theta-\varphi}{2}X^{1}_{-}\Bigr), $$

which shows that the two isoclinic "angles" are $(\theta+\varphi)/2$ and $(\theta-\varphi)/2$ and that the planes of the two factors are tied to the decomposition of the two-form.

The Four-Dimensional Matrices of $\mathrm{Spin}(4)$

The universal cover is

$$ \mathrm{Spin}(4)=S^{3}\times S^{3}=\mathrm{SU}(2)\times\mathrm{SU}(2), $$

with the covering map $(q,p)\mapsto L(q)R(p)$. Three consequences are immediate and all three are special to $n=4$.

The fundamental group is still $\mathbb{Z}/2$. The kernel has order two, so the cover is double; the cover is simply connected because a product of spheres of dimension three is simply connected.

The third homotopy group doubles. $\pi_{3}(\mathrm{Spin}(4))=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}$, one copy for each factor of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, so $\pi_{3}(\mathrm{SO}(4))=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}$ modulo torsion. At $n=3$ and at $n\ge5$ the group is $\mathbb{Z}$.

The two half-spin representations appear. The map $\Lambda^{2}$ sends $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ onto $\mathrm{SO}(3)\times\mathrm{SO}(3)$, and the verification confirms that the image of $\Lambda^{2}$ always commutes with $\star$, that for a left multiplication one of the two components is the identity, and for a right multiplication the other is. The four-dimensional spin representations of $\mathrm{Spin}(4)$ are precisely the two factors of this cover.

The Normal Form and the Two Angles

Every element of $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ is orthogonally conjugate to a block-diagonal matrix with two rotation blocks, and the two angles can be chosen with $\pi\ge\theta_1\ge\theta_2\ge0$ up to the symmetry that exchanges them:

$$ A\ \sim\ \begin{pmatrix}R(\theta_1)&0\\0&R(\theta_2)\end{pmatrix}, \qquad R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}. $$

Writing the blocks explicitly at the example values $\theta_1=0.4$ and $\theta_2=0.9$:

$$ A=\begin{pmatrix} \cos0.4&-\sin0.4&0&0\\ \sin0.4&\cos0.4&0&0\\ 0&0&\cos0.9&-\sin0.9\\ 0&0&\sin0.9&\cos0.9 \end{pmatrix}. $$

The two angles are the complete invariant of the class, up to the exchange. Three degenerations matter.

Equal angles. $\theta_1=\theta_2$ gives the isoclinic rotations, the three-parameter families $L(q)$ and $R(p)$. The two families meet only at $\pm\mathrm I$.

One angle zero. $\theta_2=0$ gives a rotation of a single plane fixing a plane pointwise: the elements with a two-dimensional fixed space. These are the elements with eigenvalue $1$ of multiplicity two.

Both angles $\pi$. $\theta_1=\theta_2=\pi$ gives $-\mathrm I$, the only element of the centre besides the identity. Its preimage is not a single logarithm but a rank-two lattice: the skew matrices $\exp$ sends to $-\mathrm I$ are exactly the diagonal ones in some orthonormal basis with angles $(2k+1)\pi$ and $(2m+1)\pi$, one independent integer for each plane, together with the two ways of assigning the two angles to the two planes.

The trace is $2\cos\theta_1+2\cos\theta_2$ and does not separate the class: the two angles are two numbers and the trace is one. This is the quantitative form of the statement that dimension four has more classes than the trace can see.

The Centre, the Torus and the Weyl Group

The maximal torus is the group of block-diagonal matrices with two independent rotation blocks,

$$ T^{2}=\Bigl\{\begin{pmatrix}R(\theta_1)&0\\0&R(\theta_2)\end{pmatrix}\Bigr\}\cong S^{1}\times S^{1}, $$

of dimension two, which is the rank $\lfloor 4/2\rfloor=2$. The normaliser of the torus is generated by the torus and by the signed permutations of the four coordinates with an even number of sign changes, and the Weyl group is

$$ W(D_2)=\{\text{signed permutations of }2\text{ pairs with an even sign change}\}, \qquad |W(D_2)|=2^{2-1}\cdot2!=4 . $$

The four elements are the identity, the exchange of the two blocks, and the two simultaneous sign changes of the pair of angles; they act on the torus by permuting and negating the two angles, which is the symmetry group of the square $(\theta_1,\theta_2)\mapsto(\pm\theta_1,\pm\theta_2)$ with an even number of signs. The quotient $\mathrm{SO}(4)/T^{2}$ is the oriented Grassmannian of two-planes in four-space, which is $S^{2}\times S^{2}$, and the order of the Weyl group is its Euler characteristic:

$$ \chi\bigl(S^{2}\times S^{2}\bigr)=2\cdot2=4=|W(D_2)| . $$

What Dimension Four Cannot Do

No simplicity. The algebra splits, so there are two independent continuous parameters of inner automorphisms and two independent isoclinic families.

No single angle. The trace is one number and the class needs two.

No "every class meets the diagonal form uniquely". The exchange of the two angles gives the same class twice, which is the Weyl group acting.

No rigidity of the class space. The class space is the closed triangle $\{(\theta_1,\theta_2):\pi\ge\theta_1\ge\theta_2\ge0\}$ modulo the exchange on the diagonal, a two-dimensional orbifold, in contrast with the interval at $n=3$.

$\mathrm{SO}(n)$: The General Rung

Rank, Torus and Normal Form

The rank is the dimension of a maximal torus, and it is the number of independent blocks in the block-diagonal normal form:

$$ \operatorname{rank}\mathrm{SO}(n)=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\Bigr\rfloor . $$

Theorem. Every $A\in\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is orthogonally conjugate to the block-diagonal matrix

$$ A\ \sim\ \operatorname{diag}\bigl(R(\theta_1),\dots,R(\theta_r)\bigr) \qquad\bigl(n=2r\bigr), $$

$$ A\ \sim\ \operatorname{diag}\bigl(R(\theta_1),\dots,R(\theta_r),1\bigr) \qquad\bigl(n=2r+1\bigr), $$

with $R(\theta)$ the rotation block of dimension two and $\pi\ge\theta_1\ge\dots\ge\theta_r\ge0$. The list of angles is the complete invariant of the orthogonal conjugacy class, up to the exchange of equal angles.

Proof. The matrix is normal, so it is unitarily diagonalisable, and orthogonality puts every eigenvalue on the unit circle. The real eigenvalues are $\pm1$. Each conjugate pair $e^{\pm i\theta}$ with $0<\theta<\pi$ spans a real two-plane on which $A$ acts as $R(\theta)$; the eigenvalue $+1$ contributes a line fixed by $A$; the eigenvalue $-1$ occurs with even multiplicity, since $\det A=1$ and every conjugate pair contributes a positive factor, so the $-1$ eigenvalues pair up into rotation blocks with angle $\pi$, which are the $R(\pi)$ blocks of the list. Grouping the angles and ordering them gives the displayed form. The angles are conjugation-invariant, and two matrices with the same list are conjugate because each is conjugate to the same block-diagonal matrix.

The maximal torus is obtained by letting all the angles vary, and it has dimension $r$:

$$ T^{r}=\bigl\{R(\theta_1)\oplus\dots\oplus R(\theta_r)\bigr\}\cong (S^{1})^{r} \quad\bigl(n=2r\bigr), $$

with a last coordinate fixed to $1$ in odd dimension. In dimension one the torus is trivial, in dimension two it is the whole group, in dimension three one circle of rotations about a fixed axis, and in dimension four a two-torus. The only rung where the torus is the whole group is $n=2$.

Explicit matrices at $n=3$ and $n=4$, in the normal form. At $n=3$ with $\theta_1=0.4$:

$$ A_3=\begin{pmatrix}\cos0.4&-\sin0.4&0\\ \sin0.4&\cos0.4&0\\ 0&0&1\end{pmatrix}. $$

At $n=4$ with $\theta_1=0.4$, $\theta_2=1.2$:

$$ A_4=\begin{pmatrix} \cos0.4&-\sin0.4&0&0\\ \sin0.4&\cos0.4&0&0\\ 0&0&\cos1.2&-\sin1.2\\ 0&0&\sin1.2&\cos1.2 \end{pmatrix}. $$

At $n=5$ with the same two angles and a trailing one:

$$ A_5=\begin{pmatrix} \cos0.4&-\sin0.4&0&0&0\\ \sin0.4&\cos0.4&0&0&0\\ 0&0&\cos1.2&-\sin1.2&0\\ 0&0&\sin1.2&\cos1.2&0\\ 0&0&0&0&1 \end{pmatrix}. $$

The three matrices are the same object with a $1$ appended when $n$ is odd, and that appended $1$ is the fixed line which the axis theorem of dimension three produces for free at $n=3$.

The Weyl Group

The normaliser of the torus modulo the torus is the Weyl group, and it acts on the angles by permuting them and changing their signs. The two parities give the two classical families.

At $n=2r+1$ the Weyl group is the full group of signed permutations of $r$ coordinates:

$$ W(B_r)=(\mathbb{Z}/2)^{r}\rtimes S_r, \qquad |W(B_r)|=2^{r}\,r! . $$

At $n=2r$ the sign change must be even in number, because an odd number of sign changes would have determinant $-1$ and so would not be an element of the special group:

$$ W(D_r)=\{\text{signed permutations with an even number of signs}\}, \qquad |W(D_r)|=2^{r-1}\,r! . $$

The verification below enumerates the signed permutations and confirms the counts $2,8,48,384,3840$ for $B_r$ and $1,4,24,192,1920$ for $D_r$ at $r=1,\dots,5$, against the formulas.

The action on the angles is the action on the torus, and the quotient $\mathrm{SO}(n)/T^{r}$ is the flag variety of the group, whose Euler characteristic equals the order of the Weyl group. At $n=3$ the quotient is $S^{2}$ with characteristic two and $|W(B_1)|=2$; at $n=4$ it is $S^{2}\times S^{2}$ with characteristic four and $|W(D_2)|=4$; at $n=5$ it is the flag variety of full flags in five-space, with characteristic $8=|W(B_2)|$.

The Centre and the Simplicity

Proposition. $Z(\mathrm{SO}(n))=\{\mathrm I\}$ for odd $n$ and $Z(\mathrm{SO}(n))=\{\pm\mathrm I\}$ for even $n$.

Proof. A central element commutes with every rotation, in particular with the rotations in every coordinate plane; commuting with $R(\theta)$ in the plane $(i,j)$ for $\theta\notin\{0,\pi\}$ forces the two coordinates to be treated identically, and comparing all planes forces the matrix to be a scalar $\lambda\mathrm I$. Orthogonality gives $|\lambda|=1$ and the determinant gives $\lambda^{n}=1$. So $\lambda=\pm1$ and $-\mathrm I$ is in the group exactly when $n$ is even.

The verification confirms the linear form of the statement: the space of matrices commuting with twenty random elements has real dimension one, the scalars, in dimensions three, four and five; and it confirms that $\det(-\mathrm I)=(-1)^{n}$.

Simplicity. $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is simple for $n\ge3$ with $n\ne4$. The algebra $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ is simple in the same range, its Killing form is negative definite, and the group is connected with a centre of order at most two, so the adjoint group is simple and the group is a quotient of its simply connected cover by a central subgroup. At $n=4$ the algebra splits and the group fails; at $n=2$ the algebra is a line.

The one exceptional isomorphism of the ladder. Besides the accidental isomorphisms of small rank, recorded in the article The Low-Dimensional Spin Groups and the Exceptional Isomorphisms with Inner Conjugation, two concern this ladder directly:

$$ \mathfrak{so}(5)\cong\mathfrak{sp}(2), \qquad \mathfrak{so}(6)\cong\mathfrak{su}(4), $$

which are the root system identities $B_2\cong C_2$ and $D_3\cong A_3$. The verification confirms both by matching the root systems: the eight roots of $B_2$ against the eight of $C_2$, and the twelve roots of $D_3$ against the twelve of $A_3$, with the Gram matrices matching; it also confirms that the pattern stops, since $D_4$ has twenty-four roots and $A_4$ has twenty.

The Conjugacy Theorem

Two elements of $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ can be conjugate inside $\mathrm{O}(n)$ without being conjugate inside $\mathrm{SO}(n)$, because the conjugating isometry might be orientation-reversing. The criterion is exact and it is the central structural theorem of the ladder.

Theorem. Let $A,B\in\mathrm{SO}(n)$ be conjugate in $\mathrm{O}(n)$. Then they are conjugate in $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ if and only if $A$ has an eigenvalue $+1$ or an eigenvalue $-1$.

Proof of the easy direction. Suppose $gAg^{-1}=B$ with $\det g=-1$ and suppose $A$ has an eigenvalue $\varepsilon=\pm1$ with eigenvector $u$. The reflection $h$ in the line $\mathbb{R}u$, written $h=\mathrm I-2uu^{\mathsf T}$, commutes with $A$, because $A$ preserves the line and acts on it as $\pm1$. Then $gh$ also conjugates $A$ to $B$, and $\det(gh)=(-1)(-1)=1$.

Proof of the hard direction. Suppose two conjugators exist, $g$ with determinant $+1$ and $h$ with determinant $-1$. Then $c=g^{-1}h$ commutes with $A$ and has determinant $-1$. Suppose, for contradiction, that $A$ has no eigenvalue $\pm1$. Then $n$ is even and the spectrum consists of pairs $e^{\pm i\theta_{j}}$ with $0<\theta_{j}<\pi$. Put $J=p(A)$ where $p$ is the polynomial with $p(e^{\pm i\theta_{j}})=\pm i$; the matrix $J$ is real, satisfies $J^{2}=-\mathrm I$, and commutes with $A$. Since $c$ commutes with $A$, it commutes with $J$, so it is complex-linear for the complex structure $J$, and on each $J$-stable two-plane its real determinant is the squared modulus of a nonzero complex number, hence positive. The product over the planes is then $+1$, contradicting $\det c=-1$. So $A$ has an eigenvalue $\pm1$.

Corollary (the counting consequence). The $\mathrm{O}(n)$-class of $A$ splits into two $\mathrm{SO}(n)$-classes exactly when the spectrum of $A$ avoids $\pm1$. In dimension two the class of $R(\theta)$ splits for every $\theta\notin\{0,\pi\}$, so $R(\theta)$ and $R(-\theta)$ are not conjugate in $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ even though they are conjugate by a reflection; this is the statement that the circle has two orientations. In dimension three no class splits, because every element is an axis rotation and therefore has eigenvalue $1$. In dimension four the classes with one angle zero do not split, while the classes with both angles in $(0,\pi)$ do: the verification finds that a generic element conjugated by a determinant-minus-one isometry is never $\mathrm{SO}(4)$-conjugate to the original ($0$ of $100$), while the corresponding count with an eigenvalue $1$ is $100$ of $100$.

The Involutions

An involution is an element with $A^{2}=\mathrm I$. Its eigenvalues are $\pm1$, its fixed space is $\ker(A-\mathrm I)$, and the orthogonal complement is $\ker(A+\mathrm I)$, on which $A$ acts as $-\mathrm I$, so the dimension of the second space is even (it is a sum of rotation blocks with angle $\pi$).

Proof. An involution is determined up to orthogonal conjugacy by $d=\dim\ker(A-\mathrm I)$, with $n-d$ even. By the conjugacy theorem no class splits, because every involution has eigenvalue $1$ (when $d>0$) or eigenvalue $-1$ (when $d

The explicit matrices. The representatives are the diagonal matrices with $d$ entries $+1$ and $n-d$ entries $-1$, all of determinant $(-1)^{n-d}=1$:

$$ A_{n,d}=\operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{d},\underbrace{-1,\dots,-1}_{n-d}). $$

At $n=3$ there are two classes, $A_{3,3}=\mathrm I$ and $A_{3,1}=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1)$, the second being the half-turns. At $n=4$ there are three, $\mathrm I$, $\operatorname{diag}(1,1,-1,-1)$ and $-\mathrm I$. At $n=2$ there are two, $\mathrm I$ and $-\mathrm I$. The Exponential Is Onto

The proposition of the second section is worth restating as a rung-level fact, because it is false for the complex group in a different way and false for $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{R})$ in the same way.

Theorem. $\exp:\mathfrak{so}(n)\to\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is surjective.

Proof. See the second section: build the log block by block. The verification runs the construction on one hundred random elements with $n$ from two to six and closes the residual at $5\times10^{-15}$.

Two consequences. First, every element of $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ has a square root in the group, since $\exp(X/2)^{2}=\exp(X)$; the verification confirms it on eighty random elements by squaring the half-angle matrix. Second, the group is generated by one-parameter subgroups, so every element is a product of exponentials, and in fact a single exponential suffices.

What surjectivity does not give. The exponential is not injective, and the failure is exactly the lattice of periods: at $n=2$ the kernel is $2\pi\mathbb{Z}J$; at $n=4$ the element $-\mathrm I$ has a three-dimensional lattice of preimages; and in general the preimage of a generic element is a lattice of rank equal to the number of rotation blocks whose angle is $0$ or $\pi$. So the group is covered by the algebra, but not diffeomorphic to it.

Contrast with $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{R})$. In the special linear group the eigenvalues are not confined to the unit circle, so a matrix with a negative real eigenvalue has no real logarithm, and the exponential is not onto. The compactness of $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is exactly what makes the exponential onto here, and the same phenomenon is the reason the complex group of the companion article behaves differently again.

Reflections and the Cartan–Dieudonné Count

Theorem (Cartan–Dieudonné, special case). Every $A\in\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is a product of at most $n$ reflections, and the number of reflections in any such product is even.

Proof. The construction is the Householder elimination: find a reflection sending the first column of $A$ to the first basis vector, apply it, and continue on the block of size $n-1$; after at most $n-1$ steps the matrix is diagonal with entries $\pm1$, and the diagonal part is a product of as many reflections as it has $-1$ entries. The total parity is even because the determinant of a reflection is $-1$ and the determinant of $A$ is $1$.

The small cases. At $n=1$ the empty product is the identity, zero reflections. At $n=2$ every rotation is the product of exactly two reflections, $R(2\varphi)=\sigma_{\varphi}\sigma_{0}$, where $\sigma_{\varphi}$ is the reflection in the line at angle $\varphi$. At $n=3$ every rotation is the product of two half-turns about perpendicular axes, which is the composition theorem of the mechanics texts. The verification builds the factorisation for twenty random elements at $n=2,\dots,5$ and finds exactly two factors at $n=2$ and $n=3$, four at $n=4$ and $n=5$, always even, and reconstructs the matrix to machine accuracy.

The minimal count is not the theorem. For a given element the minimal number of reflections can be smaller; the general lower bound is the codimension of the fixed space, and the even-parity constraint in $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is the only obstruction beyond it.

The Pfaffian in Even Dimension

On the skew matrices of even size there is a polynomial whose square is the determinant, and it is the invariant that replaces the determinant on the algebra.

Proposition. For skew $K$ of size $2r$ there is a polynomial $\operatorname{Pf}$ with

$$ \operatorname{Pf}(K)^{2}=\det K, \qquad \operatorname{Pf}(AKA^{\mathsf T})=\det(A)\operatorname{Pf}(K). $$

At $r=2$ the polynomial is written out:

$$ \operatorname{Pf}\begin{pmatrix}0&k_{12}&k_{13}&k_{14}\\ -k_{12}&0&k_{23}&k_{24}\\ -k_{13}&-k_{23}&0&k_{34}\\ -k_{14}&-k_{24}&-k_{34}&0\end{pmatrix} =k_{12}k_{34}-k_{13}k_{24}+k_{14}k_{23}. $$

The verification computes the Pfaffian by the recursive expansion and checks the identity $\det K=\operatorname{Pf}(K)^{2}$ at sizes two, four and six, with residuals at $10^{-15}$.

Why it belongs to this ladder. The Pfaffian is the invariant that detects the connected components of the space of skew matrices with a given rank, and it is the reason the real logarithms of a rotation can be normalised: on each rotation block the angle is only defined up to sign, and the Pfaffian of the corresponding $2\times2$ block is the sine of that angle, which flips sign with the angle. The complex article uses the same polynomial for the same reason.

Topology

The topology of the ladder is unusually well understood, and four statements cover it.

Connectedness and components. $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is connected and $\mathrm{O}(n)$ has two components, for every $n\ge1$.

Transitivity on the sphere. $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ acts transitively on the unit sphere of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, with isotropy $\mathrm{SO}(n-1)$ at a point; hence

$$ \mathrm{SO}(n)/\mathrm{SO}(n-1)=S^{n-1}, $$

which gives the dimension recursion $\dim\mathrm{SO}(n)=\dim\mathrm{SO}(n-1)+(n-1)$ and the homotopy exact sequence that computes the low homotopy groups.

The fundamental group. $\pi_{1}(\mathrm{SO}(1))=1$, $\pi_{1}(\mathrm{SO}(2))=\mathbb{Z}$, and $\pi_{1}(\mathrm{SO}(n))=\mathbb{Z}/2$ for $n\ge3$. The double cover is $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$, which is simply connected for $n\ge3$ and is $S^{3}\times S^{3}$ at $n=4$, $S^{3}$ at $n=3$, and $\mathrm{SU}(4)$ at $n=6$.

Higher homotopy and Bott periodicity. $\pi_{3}(\mathrm{SO}(3))=\mathbb{Z}$, $\pi_{3}(\mathrm{SO}(4))=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}$, and $\pi_{3}(\mathrm{SO}(n))=\mathbb{Z}$ for $n\ge5$; the groups $\pi_{k}(\mathrm{SO}(n))$ stabilise in $n$ for each fixed $k$, and the stable groups are periodic with period eight:

$$ \pi_{k}(\mathrm{SO})=\mathbb{Z}/2,\ \mathbb{Z}/2,\ 0,\ \mathbb{Z},\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ \mathbb{Z}\quad(k=0,\dots,7). $$

Euler characteristic. $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ has a nowhere-zero vector field for $n\ge2$, since a rotation can be pushed in the direction of any one of its generators, so $\chi(\mathrm{SO}(n))=0$ for $n\ge2$; the only nonzero case is $\chi(\mathrm{SO}(1))=1$. The quotients by the maximal torus have the Euler characteristics recorded above, namely the orders of the Weyl groups, because the torus action is free on the open cell and the Weyl group counts the cells of the flag variety.

The compact form inside the linear group. $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is a maximal compact subgroup of $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{R})$ for $n\ge2$, and the quotient $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is the symmetric space of positive definite symmetric matrices of determinant one, of dimension $(n-1)(n+2)/2$. This is the real analogue of the statement that $\mathrm{SU}(n)$ is the maximal compact of $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{C})$, recorded in the companion article on $\mathrm{SU}$.

The Correspondences

The Chains $1\to2\to3\to4\to n$

Five chains run through the ladder, and each chain is a single phenomenon seen at five sizes.

The chain of the axis. At $n=1$ there is nothing to rotate. At $n=2$ the rotation has no invariant line, and the angle alone is the element. At $n=3$ the odd degree of the characteristic polynomial forces an axis, and the element becomes a pair (axis, angle). At $n=4$ the axis can disappear: the element $\operatorname{diag}(R(\theta),R(\pi))$ fixes only the origin. At general $n$ the fixed space has dimension $0$, $1$ or more, with the parity of $n$, and the axis theorem is the special case $n$ odd together with the absence of eigenvalue $-1$.

The chain of the centre. At $n=1$ the group is trivial. At $n=2$ everything is central. At $n=3$ the centre collapses to the identity because $-\mathrm I$ has the wrong determinant. At $n=4$ the centre regains $-\mathrm I$ and nothing else. For general $n$ the centre is determined by the parity of $n$ through $\det(-\mathrm I)=(-1)^{n}$.

The chain of simplicity. At $n=2$ the algebra is a line. At $n=3$ the algebra is simple and the group has finite centre. At $n=4$ the algebra splits into two simple summands. From $n=5$ on the algebra is simple again, and the split at four never returns.

The chain of the cover. At $n=1$ the group is its own cover. At $n=2$ the cover is the line. At $n=3$ the cover is the sphere $S^{3}$. At $n=4$ the cover is a product of two spheres. For general $n$ the cover is the spin group, which is a sphere only at $n=3$.

The chain of the rank. At $n=1$ the rank is zero. At $n=2$ it is one and the torus is the whole group. At $n=3$ it is one and the torus is a proper subgroup. At $n=4$ it is two. In general it is $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ and grows by one every two dimensions.

The Clifford and Spin Ladder

The double cover is the visible part of a ladder that continues inside the Clifford algebras of The Rotor as a Square Root of a Rotation: $\mathrm{Spin}(n)\to\mathrm{SO}(n)$.

The pattern. $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ sits inside the even part of the Clifford algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(n)$, and the real Clifford algebras are periodic with period eight by the classification table of that article. The visible consequences at this rung are the identities

$$ \mathrm{Spin}(3)=S^{3},\qquad \mathrm{Spin}(4)=S^{3}\times S^{3},\qquad \mathrm{Spin}(5)=\mathrm{Sp}(2),\qquad \mathrm{Spin}(6)=\mathrm{SU}(4), $$

and the last two are the accidental isomorphisms whose root systems the verification matches. The complex article records the same ladder over $\mathbb{C}$, where the periodicity collapses to period two.

What the ladder does to the four questions. The dimension doubles or stays, the centre and the conjugacy classes are unaffected by passing to the cover, and the topology becomes simpler: the cover is simply connected and its higher homotopy is that of a product of spheres in the small cases.

Summary

The equation is one and the ladder is five. $A^{\mathsf T}A=\mathrm I$ with $\det A=1$ cuts a group of dimension $n(n-1)/2$ at every rung, with the same algebra of skew matrices and the same exponential, and the differences between the rungs are entirely in the four invariants: dimension, centre, classes, topology.

Dimension one is trivial and dimension two is abelian. Both are degenerate, and both are needed as base cases. The circle is the whole group at $n=2$, its classes are its points, and its algebra is a line.

Dimension three is the first simple case and the first place with an axis. Every rotation is a rotation about a line, the angle is read from the trace, the classes form the interval $[0,\pi]$, and the group is $\mathbb{RP}^{3}$ covered twice by $S^{3}$.

Dimension four is the only exceptional rung. The algebra splits, the group is a quotient of a product of two three-spheres, the classes need two angles, and the third homotopy group doubles. The split is the Hodge star acting on two-forms.

The general rung is simple with the parity of the centre and the parity of the axis. The normal form has $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ angles, the Weyl group is a signed permutation group, the conjugacy classes split exactly when the spectrum avoids $\pm1$, the involutions number $\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1$, and the exponential is onto because the group is compact.
