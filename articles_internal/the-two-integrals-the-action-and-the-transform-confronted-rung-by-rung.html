The Two Integrals: The Action and the Transform, Confronted Rung by Rung

Introduction

Sixteen articles rest on an integral, and nobody has put the integrals side by side. The eight Lagrangian articles of the fluid block rest on the action integral. The eight Fourier articles rest on the transform integral. Both integrate over the same variable, both are linear in the same unknown, and each article is written as though the other integral did not exist. This article puts them side by side, one rung at a time, and records what falls out.

The results, in order of how much they are worth. First, every Fourier article's support is the pole set of its Lagrangian article's kernel; the shell is the pole, the residue is the mode's weight, and a coupling that enters linearly translates the pole set and cannot reshape it. Second, the action is invariant under a change of chart and the transform is not, so the pair's eight "invisible objects" are eight measurements of one non-commuting square, and the obstruction is linearity and nothing else. Third, "invisible" carries two different meanings in the sixteen articles — not determined, and not diagonal — and separating them classifies every object in the block by its degree in the field. Fourth, the object the transform cannot see is in every case a correlation, and the quantum potential's non-additivity measures it as the interference's mean square weighted by the square of the label separation. Fifth, three different objects — the conserved charge, the constant configuration and the partition function — are the same point of the label space, which is why the two integrals can be compared at all. Sixth, the two integrals fail on two different cones: the chart fails where the field's norm vanishes, the propagator where the symbol's norm vanishes, and the mass is the height that separates the two level sets. At $k_m=0$ the level sets coincide and both failures land on one and the same wave.

What this article is. An internal synthesis. No new equation, no new field, no re-derivation of an article's result. Five statements are numerical; each gives its numbers and its lattice correction. The rest are readings of sentences the sixteen articles already write, placed so that they face each other.

The Three Integrals

Two integrals over spacetime and one over configurations.

$$ S[\tilde\Xi]=\int d^{n}x\;\mathcal L\bigl(\tilde\Xi,\partial\tilde\Xi\bigr), \qquad \tilde\Xi_{\mathrm{trf}}(\tilde K_\Phi)=\int d^{n}x\;\tilde\Xi(x)\,e^{\,i\tilde K_\Phi\cdot x}, \qquad Z[J]=\int\mathcal D\tilde\Xi\;e^{\,iS[\tilde\Xi]/\hbar\,+\,i\langle J,\tilde\Xi\rangle}. $$

The transform carries a subscript and no accent. The series writes no hat, so the transform of the field is $\tilde\Xi_{\mathrm{trf}}$, the transform of any other glyph carries the same subscript, and the subscript is the only mark that separates the amplitude from the field it came from. The label is what the subscript is read at.

The first two are over the same variable. They differ in what they do with the integrand: the action adds it up, the transform adds it up against a character. The third is over a different space, and it is not a third rival but the bridge, because it is the second integral applied to the exponential of the first,

$$ Z[J]=\bigl(e^{\,iS/\hbar}\bigr)_{\mathrm{trf}}(J), \qquad Z=Z[0]. $$

So the three are one transform and two evaluations of it. That is standard and the block has never needed it. It becomes useful below because it puts the partition function, the conserved charge and the constant configuration at one point of one space, and that point is where the sixteen articles meet.

Why a variational article and a Fourier article form a pair. The variational article asks what is stationary under a change of the field at fixed argument. The Fourier article asks what is repeated under a translation of the argument. Stationarity is a statement about the shape of the field; repetition is a statement about its symmetry. A linear equation has both, and the two questions are answered by two different objects: the kernel's inverse and the kernel's symbol.

The Support Is the Pole Set

Every Fourier article in the block is a statement about a support, and the support is the pole set of its partner's kernel. The claim is not an analogy. It is four steps, each an algebraic consequence of the one before, and no physics enters at any of them.

First step, the free action is quadratic. $S=\tfrac12\int\tilde\Xi\,\mathcal O\,\tilde\Xi$ with $\mathcal O$ of constant coefficients, so the field equation is linear, $\mathcal O\tilde\Xi=0$. That is the whole of what the eight Lagrangian articles supply here.

Second step, the character diagonalises the operator. Constant coefficients mean translation invariance, and a translation-invariant operator acts on a character by multiplication:

$$ \mathcal O\,e^{\,i\tilde K_\Phi\cdot x}=\mathcal N(\tilde K_\Phi)\,e^{\,i\tilde K_\Phi\cdot x}. $$

The multiplier $\mathcal N(\tilde K_\Phi)$ is the symbol. The character is the only function that admits this treatment, and that is why a Fourier integral and not some other integral appears in the eight articles: a function that is an eigenfunction of every translation is a character, and there is nothing else.

Third step, a mode is where the multiplier vanishes. A single character solves the equation if and only if its multiplier does:

$$ \mathcal N(\tilde K_\Phi)=0 . $$

One scalar equation in one label defines a hypersurface, not a region. So "the support" in each Fourier article is a hypersurface of the label space, and "supported on the shell" is the statement that the zeros are the shell.

Fourth step, the propagator's symbol is the reciprocal — and, off the centre, an adjugate over a norm. Where the symbol is a single number the inverse is the reciprocal multiplier,

$$ G_{\mathrm{prop}}(\tilde K_\Phi)=\frac{1}{\mathcal N(\tilde K_\Phi)},\qquad \mathcal N\ \text{central}, $$

and where the symbol takes values in the algebra there is no reciprocal of an element. The inverse of $L_\Sigma$ is then left multiplication by

$$ \Sigma^{-1}=\frac{\overline{\Sigma}}{N(\Sigma)},\qquad N(\Sigma)=\sum_\mu\Sigma_\mu^{2}, $$

because $\Sigma\overline{\Sigma}=\overline{\Sigma}\Sigma=N(\Sigma)e_t$. The denominator is one complex-valued function in both cases, and that is why the pole set survives the generalisation: the zeros of the symbol are the poles of the propagator whether the symbol is a number or an element, and in the element case the numerator is an adjugate. Inversion of the Biquaternion Fourier Transform proves the form, names it the adjugate over the norm, and adds the order count that the notation hides: the pole's order is not the symbol's degree and not the block determinant's either.

The zeros of the symbol are the poles of the propagator, and the identification is complete. The four steps in one table:

step what it uses what it yields
the action is quadratic the field equation is linear a mode is a character
the coefficients are constant the operator is a multiplier the symbol exists
a mode solves the equation the multiplier vanishes the support is the zero set
the kernel is invertible off the zeros the inverse's symbol is reciprocal the zero set is the pole set

The Klein–Gordon case, worked in full. The kernel is $\mathcal O=\Box_\Phi-k_m^{2}$, the corpus's Klein–Gordon operator, with $\Box_\Phi=\partial_{t'}^{2}-\sum_\eta\partial_\eta^{2}=\Delta_\Phi-\partial_{t'}^{2}$. On a character the derivatives act as $\partial_{t'}\mapsto-ik_t'$ and $\partial_\eta\mapsto ik_\eta$, so $\Box_\Phi\mapsto-k_t'^{2}+\lvert\vec k_\Phi\rvert^{2}$ and the symbol is

$$ \mathcal N(\tilde K_\Phi)=k_t'^{2}-\lvert\vec k_\Phi\rvert^{2}-k_m^{2}. $$

Its zero is the free shell, $k_t'^{2}=\lvert\vec k_\Phi\rvert^{2}+k_m^{2}$. The Klein–Gordon Fourier article states the same locus with the quantum potential in it, as a radius: $E^{2}/c^{2}-\lvert\vec p\rvert^{2}=m^{2}c^{2}+2mQ_{\mathrm{qnt}}$, which in the wave-number units of this section is $k_t'^{2}=\lvert\vec k_\Phi\rvert^{2}+k_m^{2}+2mQ_{\mathrm{qnt}}/\hbar^{2}$. Setting the quantum potential to zero recovers the pole exactly. So the pole set is the locus on which that potential vanishes, its position — not its residue — is what the potential moves, and the article's first sentence, that a mode is a point where the four-velocity is a unit, is the same equation read as a velocity rather than as a symbol. The two articles are one computation in two charts, and the chart in which the support is a single clean sheet is the chart in which the symbol is one number per label.

How far the numbers sit from the pole, and what the residue is. Put $\lvert\vec k_\Phi\rvert=0$ and $k_m=1$, and approach from below with $k_t'=k_m-\epsilon$. The symbol has magnitude $\lvert\mathcal N\rvert=2\epsilon-\epsilon^{2}$ and the propagator's has

$$ \left\lvert\frac{1}{\mathcal N}\right\rvert=\frac{1}{2\epsilon}+\frac14+\frac{\epsilon}{8}+O(\epsilon^{2}), $$

and the measured values are $50.251256$, $5000.250013$, $500000.249980$: the first is $50+0.25+0.00125$, the second is $5000+0.25+0.0000125$, and the third agrees with $500000+0.25$ to seven figures, the rest being round-off at $10^{6}$. The numbers are the series above and nothing else, so the pole is simple and the residue's magnitude is $\tfrac12$.

And the residue is the mode's weight. A simple zero of the symbol means $\mathcal N\simeq\mathcal N'\,(k_t'-k_{\mathrm{shell}})$ near the shell, so the propagator is $R/(k_t'-k_{\mathrm{shell}})$ with $R=1/\mathcal N'$, and $R$ is what the mode contributes to the spectral measure of the free field — the Källén–Lehmann weight, a delta at the shell of that size. The pole's position gives the dispersion and the pole's residue gives the normalisation, and a Fourier article that says "the amplitude at a wave number" is reading both from the same object. So a statement in the block about the measure on a support is a statement about residues, and not about the points of the support.

The residue is not the inverse of the derivative of the symbol, and off the centre it is not a number. At a simple null point the residue is $\overline{\Sigma}(\omega_0)/N'(\Sigma)(\omega_0)$: the numerator has norm zero, so the residue is itself a null element, and left multiplication by it has rank four of eight. The temptation to write $\Sigma'(\omega_0)^{-1}$ is wrong, because the symbol does not vanish at the pole — its norm does. The $\tfrac12$ measured above is the central case, where the numerator is a number and the formula coincides with $1/\mathcal N'$.

And the residue expansion needs one frequency variable. In two or more variables the complexified singular set is a hypersurface of real codimension two, no small sphere links a point, there is no isolated pole and no sum of residues; what replaces it is a residue current over a real cycle. The codimension is the same two in both spaces — the label's singular set and the value's null cone are each one complex equation — and that is the next section's subject.

A coupling that enters linearly does not reshape the pole set, it translates it. Minimal coupling replaces $\partial_\mu$ by $\partial_\mu+iϙA_\mu/\hbar$, which on a character is one substitution, $\tilde K_\Phi\mapsto\tilde K_\Phi+ϙ\tilde A_\Phi/\hbar$. The symbol is the same function of a shifted argument, so its zeros move rigidly with the shift and the derivative at each zero is untouched. Three articles state the same fact and this is why they agree: the charged Klein–Gordon Fourier article writes "a constant potential translates the support of the transform and leaves its radius alone", the charged spin Fourier article writes "the whole of the coupling's action on a mode is a translation of its label", and the residue argument above says both. The radius is untouched because a translation does not change a derivative, and the radius is the residue.

Where the identification fails, and why. At the spinor rungs the multiplier is a matrix, so invertibility is a determinant and the pole set is the zero set of $\det\mathcal N$. A determinant's zeros are a set, and a set is not a sheet: nothing there plays the part of a single dispersion relation, and the residue is a matrix rather than a number. A symbol exists wherever the operator is a multiplier; a scalar symbol, and with it one dispersion relation and one residue per label, exists only where the multiplier is one number per label. The Fourier article at those rungs reports the translation of the label and the invariant that survives it, the loop, and reports no pole set at all. The block's four charged articles are four answers to whether the multiplier is one number.

The Two Cones

The block has two failures and they are in two different spaces. The fluid chart fails where the value of the field is a zero divisor, $N(\tilde\Xi)=0$ — a condition on the field at an event. The transform's reciprocal fails where the norm of the symbol vanishes, $N(\Sigma(\tilde K_\Phi))=0$ — a condition on the label. The two are one algebraic object read in two spaces, and the corpus's two cones are not the same cone. This section separates them, because every sentence in the block that says "the equations are not defined there" is about one of the two and has been read as being about the other.

The Norm, and What Its Two Cases Cost

The maths the block has been using without naming. N-Regularity and N-Degeneracy in the Biquaternion Algebra proves four things that the fluid block assumes:

one norm decides invertibility $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=\sum_\mu Q_\mu^{2}$ is the only multiplicative norm, $\langle\tilde Q_2\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=\langle\tilde Q_2\rangle_{\natural}\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}$

The norm is complex, so its zero set is not a surface but a hypersurface over $\mathbb{C}$ and a codimension-two set over $\mathbb{R}$. The scalar case hides this: a central element has norm $\Xi_t^{2}$, one complex number squared, and its zero set is the element's zero set. Everything the block says about the chart failing is therefore invisible at the scalar rungs and structural at the others.

What the cone costs the chart is a theorem, not a defect. The Polar Decomposition of an N-Regular Biquaternion proves the four-factor word $\tilde Q=r\varkappa_{\mathrm{phs}}\tilde U_{\mathrm b}\tilde u_{\mathrm r}$ unique, and states in its uniqueness section that the word exists exactly on the regular elements: "on the null cone the norm vanishes, the modulus is zero, and no modulus–unit form exists". The Polar Decomposition of an N-Degenerate Biquaternion then supplies what does exist there,

$$ \tilde\Xi=\tilde\Xi_H\tilde\Xi_0,\qquad \tilde\Xi_H=a\,e_t+\frac{i}{2a}\vec W,\qquad a=\sqrt{\frac{\langle\tilde\Xi\rangle_{*}}{2}}, \qquad \tilde\Xi_H^{+}=\frac{\tilde\Xi_H}{4a^{2}}, $$

a Hermitian factor of rank one and a partial isometry. So "the equations are not defined on the cone" is a statement about the parametrisation. The field has a value at every event; the chart has coordinates only off the cone; and the four extractions — density $r^{2}$, phase $\tfrac12\arg N$, velocity from the boost, spin from the rotor — fail together, because they are four reads of one normalised unit.

One Identity Relates the Two Spaces

The corpus writes the label as $\tilde K_\Phi=ik_t'e_t+\vec k_\Phi$, so the norm of the label is available and is the symbol. With $\partial_{t'}\mapsto-ik_t'$ and $\partial_\eta\mapsto ik_\eta$ the Klein–Gordon kernel gives

$$ N(\tilde K_\Phi)=(ik_t')^{2}+\lvert\vec k_\Phi\rvert^{2}=\lvert\vec k_\Phi\rvert^{2}-k_t'^{2}, \qquad \mathcal N(\tilde K_\Phi)=-N(\tilde K_\Phi)-k_m^{2}, $$

so the pole set is a level set of the same norm:

$$ \mathcal N(\tilde K_\Phi)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad N(\tilde K_\Phi)=-k_m^{2}. $$

The null cone of the label space is the level set $0$; the pole set is the level set $-k_m^{2}$; the mass is the height that separates them. The two sets meet exactly at $k_m=0$. Checked at three labels: at $k_t'=1$, $\lvert\vec k_\Phi\rvert=0$, $k_m=1$ the norm is $-1$ and the symbol vanishes; at $k_t'=2$, $\lvert\vec k_\Phi\rvert=\sqrt3$, $k_m=1$ the norm is $-1$ again and the symbol vanishes; at $k_t'=3$, $\lvert\vec k_\Phi\rvert=3$, $k_m=0$ the norm is $0$ and the symbol vanishes. The first two are the massive shell, off the cone; the third is the cone itself, and there the symbol is a non-zero zero divisor.

The two failures, side by side.

space the function that vanishes its zero set what the zero costs
the value, $\tilde\Xi$ $N(\tilde\Xi)=\sum_\mu\Xi_\mu^{2}$ the null cone no polar word: no density, no velocity, no spin
the label, $\Sigma(\tilde K_\Phi)$ $N(\Sigma)=\sum_\mu\Sigma_\mu^{2}$ the frequency set $Z(P)$ no inverse symbol: no propagator, only a least-squares inverse

Both zeros are one complex equation, and both are therefore of real codimension two. That is why the fluid chart's boundary is populated rather than empty — a codimension-two set is not left by a generic perturbation in one direction, as The Domain of the Fluid Reading checks by perturbing a zero divisor along each of the eight real coordinates — and why no residue expansion exists in two or more frequency variables: the same codimension, in the other space, destroys the same contour.

At Zero Mass Three Failures Coincide on One Wave

Set $k_m=0$ and the three statements of the section collapse into one object. The label of a massless mode lies on the label cone, so the symbol is a non-zero zero divisor, left multiplication by it has rank four of eight, and the algebraic inverse does not exist — and that rank count is the first-order rung's: on the scalar rung the symbol is a single number which vanishes outright at that label, so the rank lost there is eight of eight. The Massless Fluid: The One Wave Where Both Charts Fail proves the containment and keeps the two counts apart. The solution carrying that label is the one-sided wave, $\tilde\Xi=\tilde\Xi_0e^{i\tilde K_\Phi\cdot X}$ with $\tilde\Xi_0$ a non-zero zero divisor, and its value lies on the value cone at every event, so the polar word fails everywhere. One wave, one point of the parameter space, and both charts fail on it: the massless one-sided wave is the only solution at which the two cones are met at once.

What replaces each inverse, and why the two formulas are one formula.

the label $\Sigma^{-1}=\overline{\Sigma}/N(\Sigma)$ $\sigma^{+}=\sigma^{*}/(2\lVert\sigma\rVert^{2})$, rank four
the value $\tilde\Xi^{-1}=\tilde\Xi^{\natural}/N(\tilde\Xi)$ $\tilde\Xi_H^{+}=\tilde\Xi_H/(4a^{2})$, rank one

The two replacement formulas coincide, because $2\lVert\sigma\rVert^{2}=4a^{2}$ when $\sigma$ is null: the label's Moore–Penrose inverse and the value's Hermitian-factor inverse are one computation, and the block's two-factor decomposition is that computation read as fluid data. Fourier Inversion on the Degenerate Set proves the three ranks — $\operatorname{rank}L_\sigma=8$ for a unit, $4$ for a non-zero null element, $0$ at the origin — and warns that the inverse off the set is not the algebraic inverse; the polar article's $\tilde\Xi_H$ of rank one is the same warning in the other space.

The Corner Has a Value

The cone is a corner of the chart, not a hole in it, and the block already knows what stands in the corner. The Massless Limit as a Boundary Value of the Polar Word approaches the cone along a path of diverging rapidity with the scale collapsing at the same rate, and finds that the word converges to a non-zero rank-one null element, an imaginary multiple of a Hermitian idempotent. The maths gives the identity behind it: a non-zero null element is a scalar multiple of an idempotent,

$$ \tilde Q=2Q_t\tilde\Pi,\qquad \tilde\Pi^{2}=\tilde\Pi,\qquad \tilde\Pi=\frac{\tilde Q}{2Q_t}\ \ (Q_t\neq0), $$

with the pure stratum — $Q_t=0$, $\tilde Q=2$-step nilpotent — as the other half of the cone.

So the block's two boundary values are the same identity in the two spaces. The label's word converges to a scalar times an idempotent; the value's two-factor decomposition is a scalar times an idempotent, $\tilde\Xi_H$ of rank one and $\tilde\Xi_0$ a partial isometry. A reader who stops at "undefined" misses that the corner is inhabited: what is lost is not the field but the split of the two frames, and what survives is the scale and the direction.

Which is the precise form of the standing objection. A reader says that the quantum fluids must reach a limit as they approach the null cone and that the equations are not defined there. Both halves are right, and this section separates them: the limit exists, and the equations that fail are the ones built from ratios. what fails there is the separation of the four factors, and with it every quantity in the block that is defined by a ratio of two of them — the velocity, the direction, the quantum potential. That is not an accident of notation and not an incomplete calculation. It is the statement that the chart is a parametrisation of the unit group, and that a parametrisation has no value at the boundary of the set it parametrises.

Chart-Blind and Chart-Sighted

The action is one functional in every chart, and the Lagrangian articles say so as their conclusion. The Lagrangian of the Schrödinger Fluid has the section for it: the map between the field's chart, the polar chart and the fluid chart is a bijection away from the nodes, and the action is one functional written three ways. Every one of the eight says the same, in its own words: the coupling is one substitution, the mass is one coefficient, the stress is one current. None of that survives a change of chart in the transform.

The transform does not descend. A chart change is nonlinear in the field, so in general $\bigl(f(\tilde\Xi)\bigr)_{\mathrm{trf}}\ne f\bigl(\tilde\Xi_{\mathrm{trf}}\bigr)$. The square therefore does not close, and the failure is not a gap in the block's technique but a fact about the operation:

from operation to
the field polar chart the fluid's data
the field Fourier the amplitude on the support
the fluid's data Fourier no image
the amplitude's data polar chart no image

The eight "invisible objects" are the eight entries of the third row. The scalar rungs lose the density and the quantum layer; the spinor rungs lose the scale and the rotor; the vector rung loses the direction and with it the vorticity. Eight names, one obstruction, and the obstruction is that a character is a homomorphism of the additive group: the transform respects sums and not products, and every chart change in the block is a product.

What that buys the reader. The reader who wants to know whether an object can be read mode by mode does not need the object's physics. The question is whether the object is linear in the field. If it is, the transform sees it at a label. If it is not, the transform sees only its correlation, and the correlation is supported at differences of labels — which is the next section's subject.

Degree, and the Two Meanings of Invisible

One word, two statements. In the sixteen articles "invisible" means either not determined by the transform or not diagonal in the label, and the two are different. Everything is determined, because every object in the block is a functional of the field and the field's transform determines the field. Almost nothing is diagonal. The articles use it for the second property. A reader who takes it for the first will conclude that the transform loses information, and it does not.

The degree is what decides. Under $\tilde\Xi\to\lambda\tilde\Xi$:

degree objects in the label
1 the field, the field equation of a quadratic action, the left side of every constraint, the label diagonal
2 the density, the stress, the field strength's energy, the right side of the charged constraint a correlation
0 the velocity, the direction, the quantum potential, the equation of state no label at all

$$ \tilde\Xi_{\mathrm{trf}}\ \text{is one element per label}, \qquad \bigl(\lvert\tilde\Xi\rvert^{2}\bigr)_{\mathrm{trf}}=\tilde\Xi_{\mathrm{trf}}*\tilde\Xi_{\mathrm{trf}}^{\natural}, \qquad \bigl(\tilde\Xi/\lvert\tilde\Xi\rvert\bigr)_{\mathrm{trf}}\neq\frac{\tilde\Xi_{\mathrm{trf}}}{\lvert\tilde\Xi\rvert_{\mathrm{trf}}} . $$

A degree-two object's label is a difference. Two modes at $k_1=1$ and $k_2=3$ give $\rho=2+2\cos 2x$: the transform has weight $2$ at the origin and $1$ at each of $\pm2$, and no weight at all at $k_1+k_2=4$. Measured on a grid of $4096$ points, the peaks are at $0$ and $\pm2$ with those amplitudes, and the bins that move when the grid is refined are artefacts. That is the beat, and the beat is the off-diagonal at its most literal: the only labels a density carries are the differences of the labels its amplitude carries. It is the same correlation the charged Proca Fourier article writes on the right of its constraint as $\tilde A_{\mathrm{trf}}*\tilde A_{\mathrm{trf}}^{\natural}$.

A degree-zero object has no label at all. The quantum potential is unchanged when the amplitude is scaled: $\langle\nabla^{2}r/r\rangle$ measured $11.910638999227$ at $\lambda=0.5$, at $\lambda=1$ and at $\lambda=7$, to twelve digits. A degree-zero object is a ratio, and a ratio of two transforms is not the transform of a ratio. That single sentence is the block's whole difficulty with the fluid chart, and it is why the vector rung's "direction" behaves like the scalar rung's "quantum potential" although one is a vector and the other a scalar.

The Invisible Object Is a Covariance

The claim. In each of the eight pairs the object the transform cannot see is a correlation of the field with itself, and both integrals present that same object: the action as a term of the density, the transform as a failure of additivity. It is one object seen once as a number and once as a defect.

The measurement. Superpose two modes of amplitudes $1$ and $\eta$ at labels $k_1$ and $k_2$, write the interference term $I(x)=2\eta\cos(\Delta k\,x)$ with $\Delta k=k_2-k_1$, and measure $\mathcal Q=\langle\nabla^{2}r/r\rangle$, with $Q=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\mathcal Q$. The deficiency is quadratic in $\eta$ — the ratio is $4.01$ per halving of $\eta$, and $1.9996$ at $\eta=0.025$ — and it is

$$ \Delta\langle\mathcal Q\rangle=\frac{(\Delta k)^{2}}{4}\,\overline{I^{2}} =\tfrac12\,\eta^{2}\,(\Delta k)^{2}, \qquad \overline{I^{2}}=2\eta^{2}. $$

The numbers, at $\eta=10^{-4}$. Measured $0.499599$, $1.993585$, $7.897726$, $4.467569$, $30.388546$ for $\Delta k=1,2,4,3,8$, against $\tfrac12(\Delta k)^{2}=0.5,2,8,4.5,32$. The residual is the lattice's, and it falls by a factor $16$ each time the step falls by $4$ — $3.208\times10^{-3}$, $1.998\times10^{-4}$, $1.181\times10^{-5}$ — so its form is $(h\Delta k)^{2}/12$ and the coefficient is exact in the continuum.

What the formula says. The deficiency is the mean square of the interference term, weighted by the square of the label separation. That is the shape of a second moment, and it is why the word covariance is the right one: a covariance is a spread in the labels carried by the interference between them, and with two labels present the spread is $\Delta k$ and the carrier is $\overline{I^{2}}$.

And the response starts at second order for a reason. A small admixture cannot move the mean of a degree-zero object at first order: $Q$ is unchanged by a rescaling of the field, so the first-order term is a degree-one statement and there is none to make. The deficiency is quadratic in $\eta$ because the object is degree zero, and that is the same degree argument the previous section used to explain why no label carries $Q$.

So the quantum potential at a superposition is not a number about the superposition's size. It is a number about how far apart its labels are, weighted by the interference that joins them — and both factors are unavailable to a transform that reads single labels.

Which is why the block's two integrals keep meeting at the same object. The action integrates a density whose off-diagonal part is this covariance; the transform was never able to diagonalise it, and what it can do instead is name its support, which is the label difference. A covariance is exactly the object that a linear basis cannot diagonalise and a quadratic action cannot avoid.

Where the Label Originates

Three objects of three different articles sit at $\tilde K_\Phi=0$, and they are one point.

The charge. The action's symmetry supplies a current, and $Q=\int j^{0}\,d^{3}x=j^{0}_{\mathrm{trf}}(\vec K=0)$. Measured on $j^{0}=e^{-x^{2}}$: the transform at $K=0$ gives $1.7724538509$, the integral gives $1.7724538509$, and at $K=1$ the transform has fallen to $1.3803884470$. Noether's charge is a single point of the transform, and the point is the origin of the spatial label. Noether's Theorem in Biquaternionic Form states the current; the transform states where its integral lives.

The constant configuration. A spatially constant field has one non-zero bin, at $K=0$: measured, one bin out of $4096$, value $3.000000$. The Proca Fourier article's subtitle is this instance — the frame carries no momentum — and the same holds for the three constant brackets of a spinor mode, which are why a mode's fluid is a rigid body in uniform translation.

The partition function. $Z[J]=\bigl(e^{iS/\hbar}\bigr)_{\mathrm{trf}}(J)$, so $Z=Z[0]$ is the same point again, now in the source variable. The Feynman Propagator in Biquaternionic Form computes the object that the pole set of the previous section describes; the partition function is that object's value at the origin.

What follows. The action's conserved quantities and the transform's constant configurations are the same point of the label space. A symmetry of the action produces a quantity at the origin of the label; a configuration with no label puts all its weight there. That coincidence is why the two integrals can be compared at all: they have exactly one point in common, and it is not a coincidence of notation but the origin of the group of translations under which both are defined.

Hydrodynamics: The Observable With No Mode

The equation of state is a ratio of two degree-two objects, so no mode can test it.

$$ c_s^{2}=\frac{\delta p}{\delta\epsilon}, \qquad \epsilon\ \text{and}\ p\ \text{both degree }2 . $$

At the scalar and the spinor rungs the denominator vanishes on a mode. A mode's density is constant, so $\delta\epsilon=0$ and the ratio is not defined at a label. This is the transform's way of stating what The Lagrangian of the Klein–Gordon Fluid states as an absence of a term: there is no equation of state at these rungs, and a mode is precisely the configuration at which the question cannot be asked.

At the vector rung the denominator does not vanish, and the ratio exists. A mode of the Proca fluid has an oscillating scale, so its density oscillates, so its pressure responds to a density change. The Lagrangian of the Proca Fluid finds the mass term producing $\epsilon=p=\tfrac12 k_m^{2}r^{2}$, so the fluid is stiff, $c_s^{2}=1$. The one rung whose fluid has a sound speed is the one rung whose modes carry a density oscillation, and that is not two facts but one.

And the modes respect the sound speed. The dispersion $k_t'^{2}=\lvert\vec k_\Phi\rvert^{2}+k_m^{2}$ gives a group velocity $\lvert\vec k_\Phi\rvert/k_t'$:

$\lvert\vec k_\Phi\rvert$, $k_m=1$ $0.1$ $0.5$ $1$ $2$ $5$ $20$
$v_g$ $0.099504$ $0.447214$ $0.707107$ $0.894427$ $0.980581$ $0.998752$

Bounded by the stiff sound speed and approaching it. So the rung with a constitutive law is the rung whose modes travel asymptotically at the speed that law names, and the three rungs without one are the three whose modes never change their density. This is a consistency and not a derivation: it does not produce $c_s^{2}=1$ from the dispersion, it only records that the two agree where both exist.

The other hydrodynamic absence. The stress at the quantum rungs carries a gradient term with no thermal counterpart, which is why the block places the fluid in the Korteweg class. A mode has no gradient term. So the Korteweg stress is invisible to the transform for exactly the reason the quantum potential is: it is built from the second derivative of a magnitude, and its support is at label differences rather than at labels.

Quantum Physics: One Quantity the Two Integrals Disagree About

The quantum potential is zero on every mode and is the whole of the equation of motion. The transform's verdict is arithmetic: a mode's magnitude is constant, so $Q=0$ identically, and a reader who worked only from the eight Fourier articles would conclude that the quantum sector is empty. The action's verdict is the opposite: the equation is the classical one plus $Q$, and $Q$ is what the fluid's acceleration law carries that Newton's does not.

The disagreement is not a contradiction, and the degree resolves it. $Q$ is degree zero: no scaling of the field can reveal it, no label carries it, and its support is at differences. A transform that looks for support finds nothing, and an action that looks for a term finds everything, and both are reading correctly.

The two integrals are the two halves of the quantum formalism, one per rung. The action carries the phase, and with it interference and the semiclassical limit; the transform carries the modulus, and with it the density and the Born rule. The block's two articles per rung are the two halves, and the object that neither can hold alone is the covariance of the previous section: it is a term in the action and a failure of additivity in the transform, and the two descriptions are the same fact.

Limits

The pole-set identification needs a linear equation. It is a statement about a quadratic action and a diagonal symbol. Where the coupling enters a bracket, as at the two spinor rungs, the symbol is not diagonal, the article reports a translation instead of a pole set, and the section's first result has no content. The four charged articles are the useful comparison precisely because they fail in four different ways.

The residue statement needs one frequency variable and a central symbol. In two or more variables the singular set is of real codimension two and no residue sum exists; off the centre the residue is a null element of rank four rather than a number. The measured $\tfrac12$ is the free scalar case, and it is quoted as that.

Constant coefficients. The transform's algebraic reading needs them, as the Fourier articles say in their own Limits sections. A position-dependent potential makes the symbol a function of the point and the transform a convolution, and none of the eight articles uses one.

Three integrals, two spaces. Only two of the three are over the same variable. The path integral is over configurations, and $Z[J]=\bigl(e^{iS/\hbar}\bigr)_{\mathrm{trf}}(J)$ is the whole of its relation to the transform here — a remark, not a derivation.

No new equation. Nothing in this article adds a result to the sixteen. Five statements are measured, all on a periodic lattice, all with the lattice correction displayed; they confirm what the articles already assert and carry no physics of their own.

Not the non-quantum limit. That audit is a different cut of the same corpus, by family rather than by integral, and it is The Eight Families. The two cuts should agree where they overlap, and the scalar rows are where a reader can check it cheapest.

Open Questions

  1. Is $v_g\le c_s$ general? The stiff rung satisfies it. A barotropic fluid with $c_s^{2}\ne1$ would not, in this reading, and the reason may be that a mode is not a sound wave but a single label. What the transform would have to see to make the comparison is a pair of labels, and pairs are the covariance's territory.

  2. Is there a transform that descends to the fluid chart? The obstruction is linearity. A Legendre transform on the label, which is what the velocity is to the wave number, is nonlinear and might descend where the Fourier transform does not. Nothing in the block does this.

  3. Does the deficiency formula survive where the density is not $\lvert\phi\rvert^{2}$? At the relativistic rungs the fluid's density is $\gamma$-weighted, so the second moment would be taken against a different measure. The coefficient $\tfrac12\eta^{2}(\Delta k)^{2}$ is the free non-relativistic case and no more.

  4. Is there a fluid reading of the cone itself? The two-factor decomposition survives there and carries a scale and a direction, so a fluid of two data exists where the four-factor fluid does not. Whether those two data obey a transport law of their own is not asked anywhere in the block, and the obstruction is the one this article keeps meeting: the velocity and the spin are ratios of factors, and on the cone the factors are not separately there.

  5. What replaces the pole set when the action is the fluid's own? The Korteweg action is not quadratic in any variable that carries a label, so there is no symbol. Whether the fluid's own variational principle has a transform at all is open, and the question is the natural end of this article.

Summary

Two integrals over one variable, and a third over the configurations that connect them. The action adds the density up; the transform adds it up against a character; the path integral is the transform of the exponential of the action, read at zero source. The sixteen articles of the block are eight pairs, one of each kind per rung, and each pair is one computation in two charts.

Every Fourier article's support is its Lagrangian partner's pole set. The symbol's inverse — the reciprocal where the symbol is central, an adjugate over a norm where it is not — is the propagator, the pole is the shell, the residue is the weight, and a coupling that enters linearly translates the pole set without reshaping it. Three of the block's articles state that translation from three directions and this article states the reason: a constant connection shifts an argument.

The action is chart-blind and the transform is chart-sighted. A character respects sums and not products, every chart change in the block is a product, so the square does not close and does not close for one reason. The eight "invisible objects" are eight entries of one failure.

Invisible means not diagonal, and the degree decides. Degree one is diagonal; degree two is a correlation supported at differences of labels, which is the beat; degree zero has no label at all and is invisible in the stronger sense. A degree-zero object is a ratio, and a ratio of two transforms is not the transform of a ratio — the block's difficulty with the fluid chart, in one sentence.

The object the transform cannot see is a covariance. Its measure is $\tfrac12\eta^{2}(\Delta k)^{2}$: the interference's mean square, weighted by the square of the label separation. The action states it as a term, the transform as a failure of additivity, and it is the one object present in all sixteen articles.

Three things sit at the origin of the label — the Noether charge, the constant configuration and the partition function — and that is why the two integrals can be compared at all.

Two cones, two spaces, and the mass between them. The chart fails where the field is a zero divisor and the propagator where the symbol is; the algebra is one norm, so the pole set is the level set $N(\tilde K_\Phi)=-k_m^{2}$ and the label's null cone is the level set $0$. The mass is the height that lifts one off the other, and only at $k_m=0$ do they meet — on the one-sided wave, where the label is null, the symbol has rank four and the value has no word. The corner is inhabited by a rank-one factorisation, which is what both boundary values of the block are.

And one rung has a sound speed. The Proca fluid is stiff, $c_s=1$, its modes are bounded by that speed and approach it, and the three rungs below have no sound speed because a mode never changes their density. The equation of state is the one fluid observable with no mode.
