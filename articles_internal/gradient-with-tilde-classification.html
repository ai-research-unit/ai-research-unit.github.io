The Gradient with the Tilde: Where the Subscript Stands

Introduction

What this is. A census of every gradient the physics corpus writes with a tilde, and of the sector subscript the corpus puts on it or leaves off. The prose comes first. Every list of articles is at the end of the document.

The headline. $200$ of the $470$ articles write the tilde, and they print it $4170$ times. The mark standing on a glyph decides its class: $3185$ carry the material subscript $\Phi$, $50$ the informational $\Psi$, $440$ a label, $140$ a conjugation with no sector, $165$ the definition $\tilde\nabla=\dots$, $52$ a power, $35$ another superscript, and $103$ no mark at all, in $8$ articles. These figures are the state after every correction recorded below. $243$ glyphs received a mark they did not carry — $127$ on the Dirac pair, $30$ on the material chart of case B, $86$ in the reading of §III. A further $16$ were retired rather than marked, $13$ leaving the text with the sentence that carried them and $3$ taking the material subscript. $23$ product factors and $1198$ respellings were set right.

The partition of the $200$. Three print bare glyphs that must stay bare, because the article defines the operator there (list B). Five print a bare glyph for a reason each list C gives. The remaining $192$ print no bare glyph of any kind, and eight of them are the articles that received their subscript at a site the article already fixed (list A). The arithmetic is $200=3+5+192$.

The rule. The subscript is printed where the operator is applied at the material parameters. It is left off for a power ($\tilde\nabla^{2}$), a labelled variant ($\tilde\nabla_{\mathrm{gco}}$, $\tilde\nabla_\kappa$, the kernel label $\tilde G_{\Phi,\tilde\nabla}$), a definition ($\tilde\nabla=e_t\,\partial_{R_t}+\dots$), and an article that fixes no chart at all and works at the eight coordinates $R_\mu$.

The second device. A bare glyph that stands in a sentence rather than in a formula is a mention of the operator, not an application of it. Where the mention adds nothing the formula does not already carry, the sentence was rewritten and the glyph removed: "the parent's with that operator in place of $\tilde\nabla$" became "in place of the gradient". Sixteen glyphs left the corpus this way. The rule that decided which ones is in §III and the sites are in list C.

The Glyph

The operator.

$$ \tilde\nabla=e_t\frac{\partial}{\partial R_t}+\vec e_x\frac{\partial}{\partial R_x}+\vec e_y\frac{\partial}{\partial R_y}+\vec e_z\frac{\partial}{\partial R_z}, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial R_\mu}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial r_\mu}-i\,\frac{\partial}{\partial r'_\mu}. $$

The two parts.

$$ \tilde\nabla=\tilde\nabla_\Phi+\tilde\nabla_\Psi, \qquad \tilde\nabla_\Phi=-i\,e_t\frac{\partial}{\partial r'_t}+\sum_\eta\vec e_\eta\frac{\partial}{\partial r_\eta}, \qquad \tilde\nabla_\Psi=e_t\frac{\partial}{\partial r_t}-i\sum_\eta\vec e_\eta\frac{\partial}{\partial r'_\eta}. $$

The subscript names the chart: $\Phi$ the material chart, the imaginary time $r'_t$ and the real space numbers $r_\eta$; $\Psi$ the informational chart.

One spelling for the conjugate. Where both marks stand on one glyph the sector goes low on the operator and $\natural$ goes outside, $(\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}$. This equals the other order, $(\tilde\nabla^{\natural})_\Phi$, because $\natural$ negates the vector part and leaves the scalar imaginary alone, so it carries each sector to itself. The corpus carried $1198$ glyphs in the other order, across $138$ articles, and every one was respelled. The algebra is unchanged; only the writing is.

I. The One Defect

The defect is a product of two factors in different sectors. When $\Box$ is built from the material gradient it is written $\tilde\nabla_\Phi(\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}$, and both factors carry $\Phi$. In $23$ places a factor was written bare: $16$ times the second, $\tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde\nabla^{\natural}$, and $7$ times the first, $(\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}\tilde\nabla$. A bare factor is the sum of both sectors, so the product was not the element the equality asserts.

All $23$ are patched. Each bare factor now carries its $\Phi$, so both sides of the equality hold the same element. This is a change of sector, not of spelling.

II. The Three Articles Whose Bare Glyphs Need No Subscript

$97$ glyphs in three articles carry no subscript and should not: $77$ in Operator and Calculus Conventions, $18$ in Physics Units and Common Elements Conventions, and $2$ in Matter Makes Space. In all three the bare glyph is the operator at the eight coordinates — inside a definition, inside a theorem that holds before any sector is fixed, or inside the notation table. None of the three applies a gradient to a field at a chart.

The two conventions articles are definitions by design. Operator and Calculus Conventions and Physics Units and Common Elements Conventions define the operator, name it, state the general theorems that hold on the eight coordinates, and list it in the notation table. Their statements hold before any sector is fixed, and marking them would print a sector on a statement that is not about one.

The comparison article writes no chart. Matter Makes Space defines $\tilde\nabla=\sum_\mu e_\mu\partial/\partial R_\mu$ to set the corpus beside another framework, and never writes $r'_t$; the two glyphs are the definition and the notation row.

The kernel label is not an application. $\tilde G_{\Phi,\tilde\nabla}$ names the first-order kernel, and its low slot already holds a label. The glyph inside it names the operator the kernel inverts, not an operator applied at a chart. A second subscript there would collide with the label, and the sector of the kernel is already printed: $\Phi$. This is why The Biquaternion d'Alembertian and Its Green's Functions, Maxwell's Equations in the Biquaternionic Formulation and Exercise: The Retarded Potentials and the Green's Function print no bare glyph under the counting rule, although the four were once grouped here: their unmarked glyphs sit inside the label or in a definition, and neither is an application.

III. The Thirty-Six Articles That Printed No Subscript

Thirty-six articles printed no sector subscript anywhere. All thirty-six have now been read one by one, and the question put to each was physical, not clerical: what does this gradient differentiate, and on which chart does the field it acts on vary? When the reading began twenty-four of them still printed a bare glyph, $77$ between them. Nineteen now print none. Five still print a bare glyph, $6$ between them, and in each the glyph is either the entry of a notation table or a formula about the operator on all eight coordinates. They are list C.

The rule, quoted. The conventions state it twice. In Operator and Calculus Conventions, §Summary: "A gradient read on one part always carries that part's subscript and never stands bare: $\tilde\nabla_\Phi$ on the material part, $\tilde\nabla_\Psi$ on the informational one. A bare $\tilde\nabla$ means the operator on all eight coordinates, and it is the only symbol that may appear without a part subscript." In the same article, §The Two First-Order Equations and Their Symbol, paragraph Marks.: "The gradient keeps its own rule: $\tilde\nabla$ bare when it means the operator on all eight coordinates, and a part subscript, $\tilde\nabla_\Phi$ or $\tilde\nabla_\Psi$, wherever one part's chart is meant." The subscript is not a variant and not a decoration. It is the claim that the gradient differentiates in one sector's four coordinates.

The directions, which is what decides. The parts take disjoint coordinates:

$$ \tilde\nabla_\Phi:\ \partial_{r'_t},\ \partial_{r_\eta}, \qquad \tilde\nabla_\Psi:\ \partial_{r_t},\ \partial_{r'_\eta}. $$

A glyph read on the material part differentiates in $r'_t$ and $r_\eta$. A glyph that means the operator on all eight coordinates is written in $R_t$ and $R_\eta$. The two are different operators on the same algebra, and the mark says which one is meant.

The Dirac pair, as the worked example

The corpus settles the question by contradicting itself in two articles, and the contradiction is instructive because both are about the same equation.

The Dirac Equation in Biquaternionic Form writes $\tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},R}=k_m\tilde{Ξ}_{\mathrm{sp},L}$ with the subscript, and The Polar Form of the Dirac Equation in Biquaternionic Form — the article that reads the word's angle in $r'_t$ — now writes it with the subscript too.

What is true between them. The operator is the same operator and the equation is the same equation. Where the field varies only in $\vec r$ and $r'_t$, the informational terms of the gradient vanish, $\tilde\nabla_\Psi\tilde{Ξ}=0$, and $\tilde\nabla\tilde{Ξ}=\tilde\nabla_\Phi\tilde{Ξ}$. The two spellings name the same value; the mark records which fact the article is using.

Which of the two is convention-correct. The one with the subscript, whenever the article applies the gradient to the Dirac field. The rule speaks about a gradient read on a part, and the pair reads it there by construction.

The general lesson. The subscript records a fact the article already knows — the chart its own equation is written in. The reading of §III is nothing more than making that fact visible at every site where the gradient is applied.

The pair, named in a gloss. "with $\tilde\nabla_\Phi$ the biquaternionic gradient and $(\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}$ its quaternion conjugate" — the sentence that follows the printed pair must name the symbols that were printed. Five articles carried this inconsistency.

What the reading left, and why. Six bare glyphs survive in five articles, and no site is an oversight. Two kinds of site account for them.

A row of a notation table. Four of the six are the entry that names the operator: $\tilde\nabla$ beside "the biquaternion gradient, acting by left multiplication" in Operator Chirality, beside "the biquaternionic gradient $\sum_\mu e_\mu\partial/\partial R_\mu$" in The Differentiable Field, beside "the biquaternionic gradient and the d'Alembertian" in Quantum Chromodynamics, and beside "the gradient, its quaternion conjugate and the d'Alembertian" in Bhabha Multi-Mass. The symbol column is the definition of the entry: the row exists to say what the glyph means before any chart is fixed, and a subscript there would define a different object from the one the row introduces.

A definition and the two formulae that stand on it. The Square-Zero Element prints $\tilde\nabla=e_t\partial/\partial R_t+\sum_\eta\vec e_\eta\partial/\partial R_\eta$ as a display, then $\tilde\nabla\tilde\nabla^{\natural}=\Box$ as an identity and $(\tilde\nabla,\tilde\nabla^{\natural})$ as the supercharge pair. All three are statements about the operator on all eight coordinates, and all three are formulae, not remarks.

The second pass: the sentence rewritten instead of the glyph marked. The first pass marked what it found. It left $22$ bare glyphs in what was then a list C of ten articles, and most of them were prose: a phrase in a proof, a comparison in a table cell, a clause naming the operator between two formulae. A glyph in a sentence is a mention, not an application, and the cheaper repair is to change the sentence. Sixteen bare glyphs were retired this way — thirteen words replaced the phrase that carried the glyph, and three rows took the material subscript, as list C records. Where the glyph was inside an identity that also had to hold, the identity was respelled in the material chart, $\tilde\nabla\tilde\nabla^{\natural}=\Box$ becoming $\tilde\nabla_\Phi(\tilde\nabla_\Phi)^{\natural}=\Box$, which is the corpus's own spelling and the element list E patched.

A correction to the earlier draft of this audit. The first version of this section split the group by the coordinate the article happens to print, and gave case C the verdict "no verdict is possible". That was too strong, and the reading shows it. The question is not what letter the article writes but what its physics differentiates: an article that prints the chiral pair, or a polar word whose angle is a function of $r'_t$, is differentiating in the material chart whether or not it ever writes $\partial/\partial R_\mu$. Of the eleven case-C articles, eight were repaired on that ground alone. The earlier splits failed the same way twice: the first test recognized $r'_t$ in one spelling only and missed $r_{t}^{\prime}$; the second recognized the material chart in two spellings and missed $x_\mu$ with $R_t=i x_t$, which is the same chart and is how The Nonlinear Dirac Equation and the Thirring Model declares it.

IV. What Was Actually Added

Eight articles received a subscript at a chart the article already fixed, and none of them on a label. They are list A.

Two hundred and forty-three glyphs now carry a mark they did not carry. $127$ are the Dirac pair of list G, the same pair the conventions define with $\Phi$. $30$ are the applied gradients of §III case B, each a bare glyph that became the material one. $86$ are the sites of the reading in §III, list H, spread over twenty-one articles and decided by the physics each article does rather than by the letter it writes for its chart.

Sixteen bare glyphs were retired rather than marked, in the second pass. Thirteen stood in prose, and the sentence was rewritten around them: a phrase in a proof, a cell of a comparison table, a clause naming the operator between two formulae. Three stood in notation rows and took the material subscript. With the conjugates and definitions that shared the rewritten sentences, the corpus prints $21$ fewer glyphs than it did: $4170$ against $4191$. List C records all sixteen.

Twenty-three product factors, in sixteen articles, each a bare factor that became the material one.

Eleven hundred and ninety-eight respellings, in $138$ articles, moving the sector inside the parenthesis and $\natural$ outside. This one changes no element.

No glyph was added where the low slot already held a label, and none was added to a glyph that defines the operator on all eight coordinates or states an identity that holds for every element. Those are the survivors of list C. No glyph was marked in a notation row that defines the operator either; the row either stayed bare or, where the article reads one chart throughout, named that chart.
