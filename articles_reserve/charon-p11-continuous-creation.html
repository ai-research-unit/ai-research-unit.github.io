Charon: Continuous Creation

Introduction

This article records the corpus's answer to where matter comes from: it is still being made, at a measured rate, in the present epoch. RC 085 states the number — $2\times10^{38}$ g s$^{-1}$, which the corpus glosses as "l'équivalent de cent mille soleils par seconde". The claim is a modern-day steady-state creation term, and it is refuted by the same observations that killed steady-state cosmology, plus one of its own.

The claim, verbatim. RC 085: "$\tau = \tau_a \quad \frac{dM}{d\tau} = 2 \cdot 10^{38} \text{ g/s}$", "soit l'équivalent de cent mille soleils par seconde (une masse solaire = $2 \cdot 10^{33}$ g)". The rate is zero at $\tau = 0$ and at the minimum of $M$ at $\tau = 345$ Myr, and it later reverses into annihilation: "Pendant la période de contraction de l'Univers, après la demi-période $T/2$, la création de matière est remplacée par l'annihilation de matière."

The verdict is [FALS]. The corpus's own mass budget is the sharpest instrument: sustained at that rate, creation supplies the entire mass of the observable universe several times over during the epoch we can actually observe. And the observed high-redshift universe already contains mature galaxies at 290 Myr, with fully processed stellar populations — matter that the corpus's curve says had not yet been made.

A note on sources. The claim is inventory §8.6, sourced to RC ch. V §5, ch. XI and Esp §VII.13/§VII.20. The corpus's own mass curve is RC 084–085. The related articles are P9 (the antimatter era), P10 (the vacuum) and P12 (Axiom 6); the related chain is R10's neighbourhood.

Conventions. Gaussian cgs. $\mathrm{d}M/\mathrm{d}\tau$ is the corpus's creation rate, $M$ the mass of the universe, $\tau_a$ the present age, $M_{\odot} = 1{,}989\times10^{33}$ g. The observable-universe mass is taken as $\approx 1{,}5\times10^{56}$ g of total (dark + baryonic) matter.

Part 1 — The number, and what it accumulates to

The corpus's rate is given at the present epoch. The accumulation is a one-line multiplication, and it is worth doing because it is the whole argument.

The rate. $2\times10^{38}$ g s$^{-1}$, unchanged, over the $13{,}8$ Gyr of cosmic history is

$$2\times10^{38}\ \mathrm{g\ s^{-1}} \times 4{,}354\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{s} = 8{,}7\times10^{55}\ \mathrm{g} = 4{,}4\times10^{22}\ M_{\odot}.$$

Compare the universe. The total mass of the observable universe is about $1{,}5\times10^{56}$ g — that is, $8{,}7\times10^{55}$ g is roughly 60 % of everything there is. So the corpus's rate, taken at face value for the age of the universe, means that most of the mass of the universe has been created during the era in which we can watch galaxies form.

The corpus's own curve softens this, and not enough. RC 085 has the rate zero at $\tau = 0$, zero at $\tau = 345$ Myr, positive thereafter, and reversing to annihilation after $T/2 \approx 2371$ Gyr. So the average rate since the bang is below the present value. Even so, the corpus's own §8.8 target — $M \approx 1{,}14\times10^{56}$ g today, growing — requires that the great majority of $M$ was created after the first 345 Myr, i.e. after the epoch whose galaxies we can see.

And RC 084 says so about the stellar budget. Its argument for small early $G$ is that it "a interdit la formation d'étoiles et de galaxies pendant cette période", and RC 085 has the creation rate off before 345 Myr. So the corpus's own curve puts essentially no matter in the sky before 345 Myr. The observation puts a galaxy at 290 Myr (Part 3).

Part 2 — What killed steady-state creation

The corpus's creation term is not the steady-state model — it is a cyclic model with creation in the expansion phase — but it is exposed to the same three tests, and it fails each.

BBN. The light-element abundances fix the baryon density at 1–200 s. A universe that manufactures baryons later cannot have produced those abundances, and adding baryons later does not change a photon-to-baryon ratio fixed at BBN. The corpus's creation raises $\eta$'s denominator problem: it makes today's universe baryon-richer than it was at BBN, in the wrong direction from what the abundances require.

The CMB. Creation is a source that changes the expansion history and injects energy. A creation rate of $2\times10^{38}$ g s$^{-1}$ today, at a time when the total mass is $\sim10^{56}$ g, is a fractional rate of $\sim2\times10^{-18}$ s$^{-1}$ — of the same order as the Hubble rate itself. That is not a small perturbation; a source term of the order of $H$ changes the Friedmann solutions qualitatively. Its integrated effect on the distance-redshift relation would be visible in the CMB acoustic scale and in the supernova Hubble diagram, and both are fitted by $\Lambda$CDM with no creation term. This is the decisive quantitative point: the corpus's creation rate is not merely unobserved, it is comparable to the expansion rate.

Galaxy counts and evolution. Steady-state cosmology's creation term was killed in the 1960s by the counts of radio sources and quasars: a universe with ongoing creation has a different source-count-versus-flux curve than an evolving universe. The observed counts require strong evolution — a high-redshift galaxy population different from today's — which a continuous-creation model does not supply. And modern deep surveys measure a stellar-mass density that is essentially complete by $z \approx 2$ (10 Gyr ago), with the cosmic star-formation rate peaking at $z \approx 2$ and declining by an order of magnitude since. The corpus's rise of $M$ towards today is the opposite of the observed history of the stellar mass.

Part 3 — The dated test

The corpus's creation curve has a date, and the date has a counter-example.

RC 085 puts the creation rate at zero before 345 Myr. RC 084 says no stars or galaxies form before then because $G$ is too small. So the model's first possible galaxy is at $\approx 345$ Myr, i.e. $z \approx 10{,}5$.

JWST finds galaxies earlier, and mature ones. JADES-GS-z14-0 at $z = 14{,}32$ (290 Myr) has a stellar mass of order $4\times10^{8}$ $M_{\odot}$ and a spectrum showing hot stellar light. Its existence requires (i) baryons, (ii) star formation, and (iii) time to build $4\times10^{8}$ solar masses — all before the corpus's creation epoch opens.

And the mass budget problem is independent of the individual object. The total stellar mass density in galaxies at $z > 6$ (within the first billion years) is measured to be already of order $10^{7}$–$10^{8}$ $M_{\odot}$ Mpc$^{-3}$. A model whose matter is still being manufactured towards the present cannot supply a mature galaxy population at $z = 14$. This is P9's argument in the language of the mass budget rather than the bang, and the two articles should be read as one falsification of the corpus's cosmology's chronology.

Summary

The corpus has matter created continuously at $2\times10^{38}$ g s$^{-1}$ — one hundred thousand solar masses per second — in the present era. The claim is [FALS]. The rate is of the order of the Hubble rate itself (a fraction $\sim2\times10^{-18}$ s$^{-1}$ of the total mass), so it would change the expansion history visibly in the CMB acoustic scale and the supernova Hubble diagram, both of which are fitted without it. Accumulated over 13.8 Gyr it gives $8{,}7\times10^{55}$ g, about 60 % of the mass of the observable universe, so the model needs most of its mass created after the first 345 Myr. But JWST finds mature galaxies — JADES-GS-z14-0 at $z = 14{,}32$, 290 Myr, $4\times10^{8}$ $M_{\odot}$ — before the corpus's creation epoch opens, and the observed stellar-mass history peaks at $z \approx 2$ and declines, the opposite of the corpus's rising $M$. Verdict [FALS].
