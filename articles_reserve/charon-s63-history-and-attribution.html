Charon — History and Attribution (S63)

Consolidation, sixth article. Where each borrowed term and each borrowed idea comes from, so that the reissued articles claim neither too much priority nor too little.

Introduction

This article is a provenance page: for every term and every idea the corpus borrowed, it names the source, or states that the source is not in the transcription. It exists because the reissued articles will be read by people who know the literature, and there are two opposite ways to get attribution wrong. The first is to claim priority for something older — to present the complexification of spacetime, the spin-2 mediator, or the word holon as Charon's inventions. The second is to lose priority for something genuinely his — to let the eight named choices, the two-sheet interpretation, or the input/output method be read as mere restatements. Both errors are avoidable from the corpus's own pages, because Charon cites his sources more carefully than his manner suggests.

The evidence base is precise and it is limited. The corpus carries two bibliographies in the transcribed text: Esp 117–118 (the 1983 book, twenty-six numbered references plus twelve endnotes) and RC 087 (the 1987 book, fifteen numbered references). Where a name appears in those lists, the attribution is documented and this article records it. Where a name appears nowhere in the 88 + 119 transcribed scans, this article says so and does not infer a borrowing. An absence from two bibliographies is evidence about two books, not about a corpus, and the distinction is observed throughout.

And one caution about the corpus's own style that shapes the whole question. Charon writes as a physicist addressing a general reader; he names de Broglie and Tonnelat in the references and uses their machinery without crediting it in the running text. A reader who only skims the body of the corpus will over-credit Charon; a reader who only skims the bibliography will under-credit him. This article is the bridge.

1. The entry point is Einstein's own question

The corpus's starting point is stated as an inheritance from Einstein, and it is the single most important attribution in the programme. Esp 117's reference 6 quotes Einstein's The Meaning of Relativity (1953 edition) at length: Einstein notes that all current formalisms are tied to the assumption that "le point est considéré comme ayant une existence objective", speculates that there might be representations "dans lesquelles l'identité du point ne sera pas conservée", and then writes, plainly: "Je n'ai, cependant, aucune idée sur la façon de construire une théorie relativiste, dans ce sens plus large." Charon's own gloss follows immediately: Einstein "paraît sans nul doute avoir l'intuition qu'un progrès pourrait être réalisé en prêtant au point mathématique des caractéristiques propres se transformant comme des grandeurs géométriques", and then: "C'est notamment le cas des spineurs d'action $h$ dont nous doterons, en Relativité générale quantifiée, le point mathématique."

Read that passage carefully, because it settles the programme's provenance claim. The complexified theory did not begin from a vision of two worlds; it began from a question Einstein wrote down and could not answer. The programme's own framing — "the point's identity is not conserved" — is therefore not Charon's thesis but his answer to Einstein's open problem, and the action-spinor $h$ at the mathematical point is the mechanism he proposes for it. Any article in this list that presents the complexification as a wholly original move is misstating the record, and any article that presents it as a restatement of Einstein is missing what Charon added: the identification of the two sheets and the working out of the consequences.

Status of the attribution: documented in the corpus itself, in Charon's own words. This is the one entry in this article that needs no external check — it is a citation the corpus makes about itself.

What Charon's move is, and what it is not, stated plainly. The corpus does not add a fifth dimension: it complexifies the four it has, and its own account of the difference is at RC 009, where Kaluza's fifth dimension is read as "un nouvel indice de la présence d'une région Imaginaire (mais cependant représentable) de notre Univers total" — a fifth dimension reframed as the imaginary part rather than added as an extra real one. The two-sheet reading (RÉEL / IMAGINAIRE with the imaginary sheet the informational sector) is Charon's; the complex-metric formalism is not.

And the precision that the companion file supplies and this article adopts. Complex and biquaternion metrics were studied by Einstein, Schrödinger and Lanczos, and later by 't Hooft (complex general relativity) and in Euclidean quantum field theory. So the complexification is a representation choice with a real precedent, not a discovery — and the same sentence, read the other way, is the source of the Lanczos and Einstein–Mayer parallel discussed in §8 below.

3. de Broglie: three debts, all cited

The corpus's largest single intellectual debt is to Louis de Broglie, and Charon cites all three of the works that matter. The attributions are exact and the debts are structural rather than decorative.

de Broglie work the corpus's reference where it is load-bearing
Théorie générale des particules à spin (méthode de fusion), 1954 Esp 117 ref 8 (with the gloss "un ouvrage qui m'a servi de référence depuis le tout début de mes travaux") and RC 087 ref 4 the spin formalism: S5's transcription and S35's $\alpha$ chain are written in de Broglie's fusion language
Une tentative d'interprétation causale et non linéaire de la Mécanique ondulatoire, 1956, ch. 3 Esp 118 ref 19 the causal, non-linear reading of the wave function — the pilot-wave family that S54 keeps as the ψ-wave ontology
La Thermodynamique de la particule isolée, 1964, ch. 4 Esp 118 ref 20 the thermodynamics of a single isolated particle — the lineage of S56's memory-as-integration-constants

And Charon's own note telling us which of the three came first. Esp 117's reference 8 says the 1954 fusion book "m'a servi de référence depuis le tout début de mes travaux, intuitivement persuadé depuis le départ qu'un formalisme unitaire devrait associer espace de Riemann et spineurs." That is the programme's methodological seed, dated and credited: Riemannian space plus spinors, from de Broglie's fusion method. The complexification is the later layer.

The consequence for attribution is precise. The pilot-wave interpretation (S54) is not Charon's invention and must never be presented as his; what is his is the two-sector setting of it — ψ as a phase wave on the imaginary sheet with a real trace at the point. Likewise the thermodynamics of the isolated particle (S56) is de Broglie's; what is Charon's is the identification of the integration constants with memory. In both cases the inherited part is the formalism and the original part is the interpretation, and the reissued articles must say so in that order.

4. The geometry: Cartan, Lichnerowicz, Souriau

The corpus's differential-geometric apparatus is standard and correctly cited.

É. Cartan Esp 118 ref 15 (Leçons sur la géométrie des espaces de Riemann, 1951) the curvature conditions — S53 audits Charon's statement of them as correct, and notes that Cartan's three conditions are the hypotheses of Cartan's (1922) four-dimensional uniqueness result, generalised by Lovelock (1971) but unaffected in the four-dimensional case
A. Lichnerowicz Esp 118 ref 17 (Théories relativistes de la gravitation et de l'électromagnétisme, 1955) the relativistic theory of gravitation and electromagnetism
J.-M. Souriau Esp 118 ref 16 (Géométrie et Relativité, 1964, p. 340 ff.) the geometric frame for relativity
E. Noether Esp 118 ref 12 and RC 087 ref 5 (Nach. Ges. Göttingen, 1918) the conservation laws; the corpus's use of the theorem is [EST]/[MATH]

The attribution point that matters here is S53's, and it is a limit on a claim rather than a debt. Charon states Cartan's conditions correctly, and the conditions themselves are established. What he adds — the identification of curvature with Born probability — is [SPEC], and the shorthand is wrong by two powers: with the corpus's own $R = \xi^{2}\rho^{2}+4\Lambda$, the curvature goes as the square of the probability density. So the geometry is inherited and correct, and the identification is Charon's and speculative; the two must be attributed separately, and S53's name for the inherited part is Cartan–Lovelock, not "Charon's conditions".

5. Tonnelat, and the spin-2 particle that the rewrite needs

This is the most consequential entry in the article, and it is a case of a latent debt. The corpus cites Marie-Antoinette Tonnelat four times — Esp 118 refs 21 and 22 and RC 087 ref 7 — and each citation is to a paper about the spin-2 particle:

  • "Une nouvelle forme de théorie unitaire : étude de la particule de spin 2", Annales de Physique, 11, 1942;
  • "Les phénomènes de gravitation, étude des interactions entre la matière et la particule de spin 2", Annales de Physique, 19, 1944.

Read those titles against the rewrite's own open lead. S64 records that Charon's source tensor $T_{\alpha\beta} = \xi^{2}\rho^{2}u_\alpha u_\beta$ is symmetric rank-two, that its symmetric traceless part must be carried — that is, the spin-2 part — and that the framework carries exactly such an object through $h_{\mu\nu} = \langle\varepsilon_\mu,\delta\tilde E_\nu\rangle + \langle\delta\tilde E_\mu,\varepsilon_\nu\rangle$ with $16 = 10+6$. The programme's spin-2 question is therefore not an accident of the complexified formalism: it is inherited directly from Tonnelat's two papers, which are about the spin-2 particle mediating gravitation.

Why this matters for the reissued articles. A reader who takes the spin-two lead (S64) as a fresh observation about Charon's source tensor is rediscovering Tonnelat's programme from the far end. The correct framing is: the symmetric traceless part of the source is the spin-2 mediator, the corpus cites the literature on it twice in each book, and whether Charon's version of that particle can be written as a frame perturbation is the open question. Stating it this way neither over-credits Charon nor loses the lead; it places him, correctly, in the 1940s French unified-field school around Tonnelat. And the citation form should follow the corpus: Esp 118 refs 21–22 and RC 087 ref 7, dated 1942 and 1944.

Two observations on this table. 7. The names the corpus names: the gnostic éon and Plato's cave

Two of the programme's central words are borrowed from outside physics, and Charon says so himself. This is the cleanest part of the attribution record, because the corpus supplies the etymologies.

The éon. Esp 098 asks whether the leptonic model is a possible model for the "Connaissant élémentaire", and gives its own gloss in parentheses: "que nous voulons appeler « éon » (le porteur élémentaire de l'esprit dans la Gnose du premier siècle de notre ère)" — the elementary bearer of spirit in the Gnosis of the first century of our era. So the term is stated by the corpus to come from first-century gnosticism, and any article that presents "éon" as a coined physics term is misstating the source. What is Charon's is the identification of the gnostic bearer with the imaginary micro-universe's structure — not the word, and not the concept of an emanation.

Plato's cave. The corpus uses the cave twice, and both times for the same structural point. Esp 042 records the recognition beside its own figure: "On reconnaît ici la « caverne de Platon »." And Esp 058, introducing the Système L, names the Knower as a prisoner: "le système d'unités qu'aurait choisi un observateur (le Connaissant) qui se trouverait prisonnier dans le micro-univers leptonique fermé (de nouveau la « caverne » de Platon!)". The attribution is exact and the analogy is structural, not decorative: the "unités L" are what an observer inside the closed leptonic universe could measure, and the cave is the frame that makes the choice of units a perspectival fact. This is one of the corpus's better moments and it should be quoted in full rather than paraphrased.

And the third borrowed word, recorded with its modern neighbours. The companion's annexe 10.10 lists four borrowings and this article adopts its findings:

term source what Charon keeps and what he changes
"holon" Koestler, The Ghost in the Machine, 1967 — a whole that is also part of a larger whole he keeps the word, changes the object entirely: a pulsating curvature lump with a radial pulsation and a half-integral action (RC 014, RC 058)
"éon" Valentinian gnosticism — an emanation of the divine he keeps the word and the "emanation" flavour, changes the domain to the imaginary micro-universe
"préon" a real term in the 1970s–80s compositeness literature (Pati–Salam 1974; Harari 1979) he keeps the word, and his préon is a different object
"quark" / "gluon" Gell-Mann's coinages he keeps the names, changes the objects — his quark is an éon with a fractional charge, his gluon a préon/antipréon pair

The rule this table yields, and it is the practical one: a borrowed word is not a borrowed claim, but it is a citation duty. Each of the four words should be introduced in a reissued article with its source named and the change of meaning stated in one sentence. That single sentence is what prevents both failure modes at once — the reader cannot think Charon coined "holon", and cannot think he is merely restating Koestler.

8. The names that do not appear, and the rule for absences

Three names the programme's own discussion raises — Lanczos, Einstein–Mayer and Koestler-as-source — do not occur anywhere in the 88 + 119 transcribed scans. The finding was checked name by name across RC/*_improved.md and Esp/*_improved.md, and this article records it as a fact about the transcription and nothing more.

What the companion file says, and what this article does with it. The companion records two historical parallels and this article preserves them as leads for a historian, explicitly not as citations the corpus makes:

  • The Lanczos parallel. The companion states that complex/biquaternion metrics were studied by Einstein, Schrödinger and Lanczos, and later by 't Hooft; that the Lanczos complex-displacement parallel to the corpus's imaginary pulsation direction is genuine; and that the "Lanczos/Einstein–Mayer parallel is a real and underexplored historical connection" — the parallel being to the corpus's proposal of an ξ-operator with mass as eigenvalue and no spin greater than 1 (companion §11.5–11.6).
  • The Einstein–Mayer parallel. The same connection: Einstein's five-dimensional unified attempts, made with Walther Mayer in the early 1930s, belong to the same family of moves that the corpus's ξ-operator and complex displacement make.

The rule for handling an absence, stated so that it is not re-litigated. An absence from these two bibliographies is evidence about these two books. It is not evidence that Charon did not know a work, and it is certainly not evidence that he did. Accordingly, a reissued article may write: "the ξ-operator, mass as an eigenvalue, and the exclusion of spin greater than 1 have antecedents in the Lanczos and Einstein–Mayer literature; the corpus does not cite them" — and may not write either "Charon borrowed from Lanczos" or "Charon was unaware of Lanczos". The one exception is Koestler, where the borrowing is lexical and the companion can date it (1967) — and even there the corpus does not cite him, so the correct form is "the term is Koestler's", not "Charon took it from Koestler".

One thing this section deliberately does not do: it does not resolve the parallels. Establishing whether the corpus's ξ-operator is the Lanczos–Einstein–Mayer operator would need the 1977 first edition, which is not in the corpus (S61's item 9), and a historian's reading of the 1930s papers. What this article can say with certainty is that the move — an operator whose eigenvalue is the mass — is not new with Charon, and that the corpus's novelty is in which operator and what it is made of.

9. The priority rules for the reissued articles

Six rules, each drawn from a case above, so that a reissued article can be written without either fault.

  1. Credit the entry point to Einstein. The programme begins from Einstein's written question about the point's identity (Esp 117 ref 6). Say so; it strengthens rather than weakens the corpus.
  2. Distinguish the borrowed formalism from the original interpretation, in that order. de Broglie's fusion, the pilot-wave reading and the isolated-particle thermodynamics are inherited; the two-sector setting and the memory reading are Charon's.
  3. Name the school, not the individual, where the move is school-wide. The complexification belongs to Einstein–Schrödinger–Lanczos–'t Hooft; the fifth dimension to Kaluza–Jordan–Thiry–Podolanski; the spin-2 mediator to Tonnelat.
  4. Never present the spin-two lead as fresh. It is Tonnelat's territory, cited twice in each book, and the open question is whether Charon's source can be written as a frame perturbation.
  5. Give every borrowed word its source in one sentence. holon (Koestler 1967), éon (first-century Gnosis, self-stated at Esp 098), préon (Pati–Salam 1974; Harari 1979), quark/gluon (Gell-Mann).
  6. Treat an absence as an absence. For Lanczos and Einstein–Mayer: state the parallel, note that the corpus does not cite them, and stop there.

And the summary sentence this article exists to make possible. The programme's entry point is Einstein's question, its formalism is de Broglie's and Tonnelat's, its geometry is Cartan's, its vocabulary is Koestler's and the gnostics', and what is Charon's is the two-sheet interpretation, the eight named choices, the input/output method, and the refusal to leave the question where Einstein left it.

Summary

The corpus's provenance, stated once: the entry point is Einstein's own question about the point's identity; the formalism is de Broglie's and Tonnelat's; the geometry is Cartan's; the vocabulary is Koestler's and the gnostics'; and what is Charon's is the two-sheet interpretation, the eight named choices, the input/output method, and the working out of the consequences.

Einstein. Esp 117 reference 6 quotes Einstein's The Meaning of Relativity (1953): all current formalisms assume "le point est considéré comme ayant une existence objective"; he speculates about representations "dans lesquelles l'identité du point ne sera pas conservée"; and he writes, plainly, "Je n'ai, cependant, aucune idée sur la façon de construire une théorie relativiste, dans ce sens plus large." Charon's gloss identifies the action-spinor $h$ at the mathematical point as his answer. The complexification is therefore a reply, not a vision.

The unified-field lineage. Weyl (gauge, Esp 117 ref 1 and RC 009), Einstein (Esp 117 refs 2–3), Kaluza–Jordan–Thiry (RC 009, Esp 010), Podolanski (Esp 117 ref 5, the foliated six-dimensional space), Crumeyrolle (Esp 117 ref 4), Bergman, Wheeler–Misner–Thorne. Charon does not add a fifth dimension; he complexifies the four, and reads Kaluza's extra dimension as the imaginary sheet. The complex-metric formalism itself has precedents (Einstein, Schrödinger, Lanczos, later 't Hooft); the two-sheet reading is his.

de Broglie, three debts all cited: the 1954 fusion method (Esp 117 ref 8 — "un ouvrage qui m'a servi de référence depuis le tout début de mes travaux"), the 1956 causal non-linear reading (Esp 118 ref 19, the pilot-wave family behind S54), and the 1964 thermodynamics of the isolated particle (Esp 118 ref 20, behind S56's memory-as-integration-constants). The formalism is inherited; the two-sector setting and the memory reading are Charon's.

Cartan, Lichnerowicz, Souriau, Noether. The curvature conditions are stated correctly and are Cartan's (S53, Cartan 1922 / Lovelock 1971); the identification of curvature with Born probability is Charon's and is [SPEC], and the shorthand is wrong by two powers.

Tonnelat — the most consequential entry. Four citations to two papers about the spin-2 particle (Esp 118 refs 21–22; RC 087 ref 7; 1942 and 1944). The rewrite's spin-two lead is therefore inherited, not fresh: the symmetric traceless part of Charon's source is the spin-2 mediator, and the open question is whether it can be written as a frame perturbation.

The borrowed words, with sources: holon — Koestler, The Ghost in the Machine, 1967; éon — first-century Gnosis, self-stated at Esp 098 ("le porteur élémentaire de l'esprit dans la Gnose du premier siècle de notre ère"); préon — a real 1970s–80s compositeness term (Pati–Salam 1974; Harari 1979), different object; quark/gluon — Gell-Mann's coinages, different objects. Plato's cave is used structurally, twice (Esp 042, Esp 058), and the second use — the Knower as a prisoner in the closed leptonic universe, which is why the "unités L" are perspectival — is among the corpus's best passages and should be quoted in full.

The absences. Lanczos, Einstein–Mayer and any citation of Koestler appear nowhere in the 88 + 119 transcribed scans. The rule: an absence from these two bibliographies is evidence about these two books. The Lanczos and Einstein–Mayer parallels to the ξ-operator, mass-as-eigenvalue and the spin ceiling are recorded as leads for a historian, not as citations the corpus makes.

And the two observations about the corpus's reading. It is older than its publication dates suggest — the newest physics references are d'Espagnat (1979) and Skolimowski (1984), with the Standard Model's consolidation essentially absent, which is one reason the particle identifications failed. And Charon cites himself accurately, calling his own 1974 Théorie unitaire "une étape vers la Relativité complexe": the corpus's self-attribution is more careful than its novelty rhetoric.
