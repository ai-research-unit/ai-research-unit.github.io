Test of Operator Notation

Introduction

This is a test, not a result. It takes the main equations of the biquaternion corpus as the corpus prints them, rewrites each one with the side marks of the operator conventions, and records what the rewrite adds, what it costs, and where it fails. Every number quoted below was recomputed for this article, and every norm is the operator norm.

The test exists because the corpus changed its base. Under gamma matrices an equation has one shape: a matrix on the left of a column,

$$ \bigl(\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu-m\bigr)\psi=0 , $$

and there is nothing to decide. A column admits $A\psi$ and does not admit $\psi A$; the second multiplication has no meaning until a row is brought in, and a row is a different object. The rewrite removed the gamma matrices and with them the reason the second multiplication was missing. In the biquaternion algebra the field is an element, an element has two sides, and both sides carry physics: the frame cells are two-sided actions, the chirality cell is a pure right multiplication, and one of the corpus's own articles writes the gradient acting on the right. So every equation now has two candidate writings where it had one, and the notation has to choose.

The test asks one question. For each equation, which of the two writings states the physics and which states less? The answer is not the same for all of them, and that is the finding.

The Two Options

Option A, position. The side is stated by where the operator stands. The operator is written before the field and acts on it: $\tilde\nabla\tilde\Psi$. Nothing is marked. This is the corpus's habit, and it is correct whenever the operator really is on the left.

Option B, arrow. The side is stated by a mark on the actor: $\overrightarrow{\tilde\nabla}\tilde\Psi$ for the left action, $\tilde\Psi\overleftarrow{\tilde\nabla}$ for the right, and two arrows pointing inward when the operator acts on both sides, $\overrightarrow{a}\psi\overleftarrow{b}$.

Where the side is stated by the place of the operator by the mark on the actor
Marks per equation none one or two
Cost of learning none one rule
States a right action no yes
States a two-sided action no yes
States which factor of three a derivative owns no no
Can be misread as a left action that is really a right one a mark that carries nothing

The rule behind option B is short. The long arrow sits on the actor and its tip points at the operand. It is compulsory on a derivative, because a derivative has no element reading and nothing else fixes which factor acts. It is optional on an element, because an element's glyph does not carry its side and the writing may already have fixed it. It is never placed on an operand, and never on a name: an algebra, a space or a cell is not an action on a field and takes no arrow.

One mark the corpus does not have. There is no glyph for a two-sided operator with no operand in view. Test 8 asks whether a double-headed arrow should supply one. The verdict there is negative for general use, and the reason is a collision with quantum field theory.

Test 1. The Gradient Against a Field

As the corpus writes it.

$$ \tilde\nabla\tilde\Psi . $$

Under option A this is one object. Under option B it is two, and the two are different:

$$ \overrightarrow{\tilde\nabla}\tilde\Psi=\sum_\mu e_\mu\,\partial_\mu\tilde\Psi , \qquad \tilde\Psi\overleftarrow{\tilde\nabla}=\sum_\mu\bigl(\partial_\mu\tilde\Psi\bigr)e_\mu . $$

The difference is a sum of commutators, and it vanishes only when each partial derivative of the field commutes with the unit it is contracted with:

$$ \overrightarrow{\tilde\nabla}\tilde\Psi-\tilde\Psi\overleftarrow{\tilde\nabla} =\sum_\mu\bigl[e_\mu,\partial_\mu\tilde\Psi\bigr]. $$

Worked examples. Take the gradient along $\vec e_x$ alone, so that only the $x$ term survives, and three fields:

Field $\tilde\Psi$ Left, $\overrightarrow{\tilde\nabla}\tilde\Psi$ Right, $\tilde\Psi\overleftarrow{\tilde\nabla}$ Equal
$x\,\vec e_y$ $\vec e_z$ $-\vec e_z$ no
$x\,e_t$ $\vec e_x$ $\vec e_x$ yes
$x\,(\vec e_y+\vec e_z)$ $-\vec e_y+\vec e_z$ $\vec e_y-\vec e_z$ no

The first row is the whole argument. The two readings differ by a sign, and the sign is not a convention: it is $\vec e_y\vec e_x=-\vec e_x\vec e_y$. A notation that prints $\tilde\nabla\tilde\Psi$ and leaves the reader to guess has hidden a sign.

The second row is the exception that keeps option A honest. When the field lies along the identity, the two readings agree exactly and no mark is needed. So the question is never whether an equation needs a mark in general; it needs one when the field carries vector units.

Verdict. The arrow is needed, and it is needed on the derivative more than anywhere else. This is the only test in which option A cannot be defended at all.

Test 2. The Chiral Pair of the Dirac Equation

As the corpus writes it.

$$ \tilde\nabla\tilde\Psi_R=m\tilde\Psi_L , \qquad \tilde\nabla^{\natural}\tilde\Psi_L=m\tilde\Psi_R . $$

Under option B.

$$ \overrightarrow{\tilde\nabla}\tilde\Psi_R=\overrightarrow{m e_t}\tilde\Psi_L , \qquad \overrightarrow{\tilde\nabla^{\natural}}\tilde\Psi_L=\overrightarrow{m e_t}\tilde\Psi_R . $$

The mass arrow is the one mark that may be dropped, and the test shows why. The mass multiplies as a scalar, so its left action and its right action are the same operator:

$$ \overrightarrow{m e_t}\psi\overleftarrow{e_t}=m\psi , \qquad \lVert L_{m e_t}-R_{m e_t}\rVert=0 . $$

The mass is the only actor in these equations for which this is true. Written as a cell it is $mT_{e_t,e_t}$, and a length-zero cell is the identity: the mass enters on neither side. That is a fact about the physics, and the arrows print it instead of hiding it.

Now the finding. Look at what distinguishes the two equations of the pair. It is not the side: both gradients act on the left. It is the conjugation, $\natural$. So the corpus's chiral pair separates the two chiralities by transposing the field, not by moving it to the other side of the derivative.

That is one of the two ways to say chirality that now exist. The other is Test 3, and it is a different statement.

Verdict. Option A is adequate here and option B adds two marks of which one is empty. But the pair as printed conceals the asymmetry: two left actions presented as a pair, in a formalism whose whole point is that left and right are both available.

Test 3. The Chirality Cell

As the corpus writes it.

$$ \mathsf C_5=-i\,T_{e_t,e_z}=-iR_{e_z}, \qquad \mathsf C_5^2=1 . $$

Under option B, a pure right multiplication is a two-sided cell whose left actor is the identity:

$$ -i\,\overrightarrow{e_t}\psi\overleftarrow{\vec e_z}=-i\,\psi\overleftarrow{\vec e_z}, $$

The two sides agree exactly, residual $0$. The left arrow is present and carries nothing. That is the honest picture: a one-sided operator is a two-sided one whose other side is the identity, and the convention prints the empty side rather than pretending it is absent.

No left-multiplication form exists. $R_b=L_a$ requires $xb=ax$ for every $x$, which forces $a=b$ and $b$ central. The unit $\vec e_z$ is not central, so no $a$ works. The two operators already disagree on one basis element, $\vec e_yR_{\vec e_z}=\vec e_x$ against $L_{\vec e_z}\vec e_y=-\vec e_x$, and the distance between them is

$$ \lVert R_{\vec e_z}-L_{\vec e_z}\rVert=2 . $$

Recomputed.

Check Value
$\mathsf C_5^2-1$ $0$
$\mathsf C_5\mathsf C_\mu+\mathsf C_\mu\mathsf C_5$ over the four $\mu$ $0,0,0,0$
$\operatorname{tr}(\mathsf C_5\mathsf C_0\mathsf C_1\mathsf C_2\mathsf C_3)$ $-4i$
$\sum_\mu\mathsf C_\mu\mathsf C_\mu$ $-2$

Verdict. This is the test where option A has no answer at all. The chirality operator is a right multiplication; it has no one-sided left form; and a notation that can only write operators on the left cannot write it. The arrow does not help either, because an arrow needs an operand: what states the operator is the cell, and the arrow states only which side of the cell is the identity.

Test 4. The Chiral Projectors

As the corpus writes them.

$$ P_\pm\Psi=\tfrac12\bigl(\Psi\mp i\,e_t\Psi e_z\bigr), \qquad P_\pm=\tfrac12\bigl(1\pm\mathsf C_5\bigr). $$

Under option B the two arrows point inward at the same field:

$$ P_\pm\Psi=\tfrac12\bigl(\Psi\mp i\,\overrightarrow{e_t}\Psi\overleftarrow{e_z}\bigr). $$

This is the one equation of the nine where the bare product is as good as the marked one. The product $e_t\Psi e_z$ already shows the field standing between two actors; the arrows add the direction of each actor, which is the same information in a form that cannot be misread. Neither is shorter, and here they agree.

The two projectors differ by the sign of the only arrowed term. So the arrows localise the difference: everything in the pair is common except one marked term.

Recomputed, as $4\times4$ arrays.

$P_\pm^2-P_\pm$ $0$
rank $P_\pm$ $2$
trace $P_\pm$ $2$
$P_++P_-$ identity
$P_+P_-$ $0$

Verdict. Option B and option A tie. This is also the case that shows the limit of the pair of arrows: they say which actor acts from which side, and they do not say which of three factors a derivative would own. Here there is no derivative, so the limit does not bite.

Test 5. The Minimal Coupling

As the corpus writes it.

$$ \tilde\Psi\overleftarrow{\tilde\nabla}+\frac{i\varrho}{\hbar}\,\tilde\Psi\tilde A , \qquad \tilde\Psi\overleftarrow{\tilde\nabla}:=\sum_\mu\bigl(\partial_\mu\tilde\Psi\bigr)e_\mu . $$

Under option B, the sum is two right actions and both are marked:

$$ \tilde\Psi\overleftarrow{\tilde\nabla}+\frac{i\varrho}{\hbar}\,\tilde\Psi\overleftarrow{\tilde A} . $$

The second mark is the repair. The term $\tilde\Psi\tilde A$ is a right action by an element, $\tilde\Psi\tilde A=T_{e_t,\tilde A}\tilde\Psi=R_{\tilde A}(\tilde\Psi)$, exactly as the gradient term is a right action by a derivation. The printed form marks only the derivative, and so says that the algebraic half is not an action. It is one.

Why the corpus left it out. The census is clear: in the three articles that use the arrow as a side mark it is always on a derivative, never on an algebraic operator. The rule the corpus was following is that the arrow is for what has no element reading, and under that rule the omission is consistent. The test asks whether the rule is right. The answer is that it is right about where the arrow is compulsory and wrong about where it is enough.

Worked example. With $\tilde\Psi=e_t+2\vec e_y$ and $\tilde A=\vec e_x$,

$$ \tilde\Psi\tilde A=\vec e_x-2\vec e_z , \qquad \tilde A\tilde\Psi=\vec e_x+2\vec e_z , $$

so the two orders differ by $4\vec e_z$. There is no sense in which the unmarked product is the product.

Verdict. One mark missing, in the one equation of the corpus whose subject is the difference between the left and the right form. This is the clearest single repair the test finds.

Test 6. Maxwell's Theory in Quaternion Form

As the corpus writes it, with the tilde restored per the operator conventions:

$$ \tilde\nabla F+\tfrac12\bigl(\tilde\nabla G+G^+\overleftarrow{\tilde\nabla}\bigr)=-J . $$

Under option B.

$$ \overrightarrow{\tilde\nabla}F+\tfrac12\bigl(\overrightarrow{\tilde\nabla}G+G^+\overleftarrow{\tilde\nabla}\bigr)=-J . $$

Three marks, one of which carries information. Both left arrows are redundant by position. The right arrow is not: it is the only mark that separates the term acting from the right from the two acting from the left, and that separation is the content of the equation.

Verdict. Option B is right and is at its best here. This is the equation that a reader can read only if the arrow exists: a sum of one left-acting and one right-acting term, with nothing but the mark to tell him which is which.

Test 7. The Mass-Shell Cell

As the corpus writes it.

$$ D(p)=\sum_\mu\mathsf C_\mu p_\mu+m\,T_{e_t,e_t}, \qquad \mathsf C_0=iT_{e_t,e_x},\quad \mathsf C_k=iT_{e_k,e_y} . $$

Under option B there is no form. There is no field in the expression, so there is no operand, so an arrow has nothing to point at. The cells carry their own sides: $T_{a,b}=L_aR_b$, and the double subscript is the pair of arrows written in one glyph.

The test's answer is therefore a boundary. The arrow marks an action on a written operand. A cell is an operator without an operand, and it takes no mark. So the notation has two levels and each is complete on its own: marked actions at the display level, cells at the operator level.

Recomputed at $m=1.3$, $p=(1.7748,0.4,-0.7,0.9)$, on shell, $p_t^2-\lvert\vec p\rvert^2=m^2$:

Check Value
$\lVert D^2-2mD-\bigl(\sum_\mu p_\mu p_\mu-m^2\bigr)T_{e_t,e_t}\rVert$ $2.4\times10^{-16}$
trace $D$ $4m=5.2$
length of $D$, the rank of its coefficient array in the sixteen cells $2$
$\sum_\mu\mathsf C_\mu\mathsf C_\mu$ $-2$

Verdict. Option A is not applicable, option B is not applicable, and the cell level is the only level that works. That is a real limit of the arrow, and an article that proposes the arrow should say so.

Test 8. The Double Arrow

The proposal. A two-sided operator with no operand in view has no mark. Write a double-headed arrow on it,

$$ \overleftrightarrow{T_{a,b}}=L_aR_b , $$

and read it as acting from both sides, left factor first.

The test. Three questions.

Does it name the right object? Yes. $\overleftrightarrow{T_{a,b}}\psi=\overrightarrow{a}\psi\overleftarrow{b}$, and the two writings agree on every basis element.

Does it compose? Only up to a reversal it cannot show. The product law is

$$ T_{a,b}\,T_{c,d}=T_{ac,db}, $$

residual $0$, so the right factor's subscript comes out reversed. A reader who chains two double arrows will write $T_{a,b}T_{c,d}=T_{ac,bd}$ and be wrong, and the glyph gives no warning.

Does it collide? Yes, with quantum field theory. There $\overleftrightarrow{\partial}_\mu$ is the fixed difference $\overrightarrow{\partial}_\mu-\overleftarrow{\partial}_\mu$, chosen to make a bilinear Hermitian, as in $\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(T^aU\overleftrightarrow{\partial}_\mu U^\dagger\bigr)$. There the two heads are subtracted; the double arrow proposed here multiplies them. The corpus contains no occurrence of $\overleftrightarrow{\partial}$ at all, so nothing conflicts on the page — but a reader trained on QFT will bring the wrong reading, and this is a notation meant to be read by physicists.

Question Answer
Names the object yes
Carries the composition law no, the second slot reverses
Collides with the antisymmetrized derivative of QFT yes, in meaning
Needed where a field is written no, the inward pair already says it

Verdict. Do not adopt it generally. Adopt it, if at all, only as a name for a cell inside a display that has no field, and print the definition once. The inward pair is better wherever an operand exists, and an operand exists in every equation of the corpus.

Test 9. The Gauge Transformation

As the corpus writes it.

$$ \tilde A_2=\tilde A-\tilde\nabla\Gamma . $$

Under option B.

$$ \tilde A_2=\tilde A-\overrightarrow{\tilde\nabla}\Gamma . $$

The mark states that the gradient acts on $\Gamma$, which the writing had already fixed by putting the gradient first, and that it acts from the left, which the writing had also fixed.

Verdict. The mark is true and carries nothing. This is the cost of applying option B everywhere: a reader who meets surplus marks learns to skip marks, and then misses the one that mattered. The rule must be allowed to stop.

The cost, counted on the nine. Three tests need the arrow, 1, 5 and 6. Three would run as well without it, because position already states the side, 2, 4 and 9. One uses the arrow to name a side that is the identity, 3. One admits no arrow at all, 7. One was tested and rejected, 8. Test 6 does both things at once: its two left arrows are redundant and its one right arrow is the whole content.

The decision rule that follows, in tiers.

  1. Two elements, value wanted: no mark. An element's arrow is free; $\overrightarrow{a}b=a\overleftarrow{b}=ab$.
  2. Operator before its operand: no mark. Position already says the side.
  3. Operator after its operand: mark. This is where option A has no answer.
  4. Two operators, one field between them: two marks, pointing inward.
  5. A cell, an array, a named operator: no mark at all. The object carries its own sides.
  6. A name, an algebra, a space: never a mark.

The Duplication

This is the part of the test that the notation was not built for, and it is the largest thing the rewrite exposed.

The chirality cell is a right multiplication, $\mathsf C_5=-iR_{e_z}$. Its left-handed twin is $-iL_{e_z}$, and the twin is not the same operator:

$$ \lVert R_{\vec e_z}-L_{\vec e_z}\rVert=2 . $$

Nor is one a substitute for the other, because they commute:

$$ \lVert L_aR_b-R_bL_a\rVert=0 $$

for every pair $a,b$. Left and right never compete. So the algebra carries two independent gradings, one by $\tfrac12(1\mp iR_{e_z})$ and one by $\tfrac12(1\mp iL_{e_z})$, and a field has both a right chirality and a left chirality. That is four sectors, each of complex dimension one, where the gamma matrices give two.

Under gamma this duplication cannot be written at all. A column spinor has one chirality operator, $\gamma_5$, acting on the left. The twin has no meaning, because $\psi A$ has no meaning. The rewrite did not create the twin; it stopped hiding it.

What the duplication does to the equations. Every equation that uses $\mathsf C_5$ on the left has a mirror with it on the right, and the mirror is a different equation. The corpus writes only one of each. So the count of physical statements is larger than the count of printed equations, and the missing ones are not missing by error: they were unprintable in the notation the corpus inherited.

The sharp form of the point. Chirality in this corpus can be said in two ways that are not the same: by transposing the field, $\natural$, as the Dirac chiral pair does, or by moving the chirality operator to the other side, as the right multiplication does. The first is a statement about conjugation; the second is a statement about sides. Test 2 uses the first and Test 3 uses the second, and no article states that they are alternatives.

What Became Visible

Chirality is a side, not a matrix. $\mathsf C_5=-iR_{e_z}$ acts on the right. Written as an array it is a matrix; written as a cell it is a right multiplication; written with an arrow it is one empty left actor and one real right actor. The three readings agree, and the last two say what the operator is.

The mass is the only central actor. It is the only term whose arrow may be dropped, residual $0$ on both sides. Every other actor in the corpus is non-central, so its side is content. That is one sentence, and it decides the whole notation: a mark may be dropped exactly when the actor is central.

Left and right never compete. Every left action commutes with every right action, residual $0$. So the two-sided operator is not a compromise between two structures; it is their product, $T_{a,b}=L_aR_b$, and it exists because the two families commute.

The two-sided product reverses its second slot. $T_{a,b}T_{c,d}=T_{ac,db}$, residual $0$. That is the one place where a bare display can go wrong with no sign of it, and it is an argument for the cell notation over any display shorthand.

Length is independent of the marks. The cells have length one, the mass-shell cell has length two, the spin-resolved cell four. Arrows change nothing about length, so the notation costs no structure and decides no physics.

What The Test Does Not Settle

Three factors and one derivative. The inward pair says which two sides the actors take; it does not say which of three factors a derivative differentiates. The bound is stated in the conventions and the test confirms it: every equation of this set has at most one derivative, so the bound is never reached. A second derivative would reach it.

Leibniz. On a product the right gradient obeys

$$ (\tilde\Psi X)\overleftarrow{\tilde\nabla}=\tilde\Psi\bigl(X\overleftarrow{\tilde\nabla}\bigr)+\bigl(\tilde\Psi\overleftarrow{\tilde\nabla}\bigr)X , $$

and neither term alone is the action. No arrow marks a Leibniz split, and none should: the equation is the statement. With $\tilde\Psi=x\vec e_y$ and $X=x\vec e_t$ the two terms are $-\vec e_z$ each and the sum is $-2\vec e_z$, while the right gradient of the product is the sum of both, and the second term is the half in which only $\tilde\Psi$ is differentiated. So the arrow on a field is not a spelling of the right gradient, and the notation must not pretend that it is.

The arrow on a field. It is permitted, no article uses it, and Test 1 shows what it would mean: the composition $L_{\tilde\Psi}\circ\tilde\nabla$, not a value. Anyone who writes it must know that it is not the right gradient.

The opposite algebra. $L_b^{\mathrm{opp}}=R_b$ states the same relation as the arrow, in one letter. The conventions keep the superscript for algebras only, which is right: it names an algebra, not a side, and a superscript on an operator is a misuse.

Summary

The arrow is needed in two of the nine tests and useful in one. Tests 1 and 6 cannot be read without it, and Test 5 is one mark short without it. In Tests 2, 4 and 9 the position already states the side and the mark is surplus. In Test 7 no mark is possible. In Test 8 the double arrow is rejected.

The two options are not rivals. Option A is correct wherever the operator is written first, which is most of the corpus. Option B is correct wherever it is not, which is the case the corpus's own physics forces. The rule that covers both is one sentence: the mark is on the actor, it is compulsory on a derivative, optional on an element, and absent on a name.

The duplication is the real finding. The chirality operator splits into a right grading and a left grading, the two commute, both are non-trivial, and gamma matrices could print only one. The notation did not create the second; it made it writable. Every chirality equation in the corpus now has an unwritten mirror, and the mirror is not an error to repair — it is a statement that was not available before.
