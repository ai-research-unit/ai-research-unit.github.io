Biquaternion decompositions — the algebra alone, then the forms

Internal note. It is not an article, it is not a plan, and it changes no corpus file.

Last updated: 2026-09-27.

The order of the file. The decompositions of the algebra come first (§1), because they are prior to any form: they use the product, the coefficient of $e_t$, and the characteristic polynomial. Then the four forms and their norms (§2). Then the decompositions that use a form (§3–§7). Then the queue (§8).

The cut. A form on $\mathbb{B}$ is the scalar part of a product: every one of the four forms is $\operatorname{Sc}$ applied to $x\,c(y)$, where $c$ is a conjugation on the second slot. So a decomposition either evaluates the scalar part or it does not. If it does not, it uses no form, and it is in §1. If it does, it uses exactly one of the four forms, and it is in §3–§6. That is the first and only cut.

1. The form-free layer

This is the layer before the forms. It uses the product, the scalar part as a coefficient, and the characteristic polynomial. No pairing of two vectors occurs anywhere in it. In maths.md it is filed as two sections, in this order: The Frame (the frame, §1.1) and Spectral Algebra (the element, §1.2–§1.5). The frame comes first because it needs only an idempotent, while every spectral statement is read in it. LU is recorded at the end and rejected: it is algebraic, not spectral, and it is listed only to close the list.

1.1 Peirce: the frame

Relative to an idempotent $e$ with $f=e_t-e$,

$$ \mathbb{B}=e\mathbb{B}e\;\oplus\;e\mathbb{B}f\;\oplus\;f\mathbb{B}e\;\oplus\;f\mathbb{B}f, \qquad Q=eQe+eQf+fQe+fQf . $$

The blocks are the four Peirce corners. A complete frame is a pair $\{e,f\}$ of orthogonal idempotents summing to $e_t$; in $\mathbb{B}$ the four corners of a complete frame of primitive idempotents are one-dimensional, so the Peirce coordinates are the matrix entries of the matrix model.

Two facts used below.

  • Corner product. $(e\mathbb{B}f)(e\mathbb{B}f)=e\mathbb{B}(fe)\mathbb{B}f=0$. Every element of an off-diagonal corner is square-zero.

  • Single corner. In a complete frame, a non-zero element supported in exactly one corner has rank one; the corner is diagonal or off-diagonal according to whether the element is idempotent-like or nilpotent.

  • Corpus: covered by Ideals, Peirce Decompositions and Spinors in the Biquaternion Algebra, Complementary Idempotents and the Peirce Decomposition, and the Peirce Coordinates articles of the $M_2$ and $M_4$ series.

  • Status: covered.
  • Why it is form-free: idempotents, corners and blocks are defined by the product.

1.2 The characteristic polynomial

For $Q=Q_te_t+\vec Q$, the vector square is the scalar

$$ B^2:=Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2,\qquad \vec Q^2=-B^2e_t,\qquad \vec Q\times\vec Q=0 . $$

The characteristic polynomial is

$$ p_Q(X)=(X-Q_t)^2+B^2 . $$

It is intrinsic: with $Q^{\nu}=Q_te_t-\vec Q$ the conjugate, $(X-Q)(X-Q^{\nu})=p_Q(X)e_t$ is central, so $p_Q$ is the polynomial of the subalgebra $\mathbb{C}[Q]$. No determinant and no matrix is used.

Quantity Value Meaning
trace $2Q_t$ twice the scalar part
constant term $Q_t^2+B^2$ the scalar of $QQ^{\nu}$
discriminant $-4B^2$ zero iff the two roots coincide
eigenvalues $Q_t\pm iB$ $B$ is a chosen square root of $B^2$; $i$ is internal to the algebra
minimal polynomial see the table below the four spectral types

The four types, which organise the whole section:

Type Condition $p_Q$ $m_Q$
scalar $\vec Q=0$ $(X-Q_t)^2$ $X-Q_t$
semisimple $B^2\ne0$ $(X-Q_t)^2+B^2$ $=p_Q$
Jordan $B^2=0$, $\vec Q\ne0$, $Q_t\ne0$ $(X-Q_t)^2$ $(X-Q_t)^2$
nilpotent $B^2=0$, $Q_t=0$, $Q\ne0$ $X^2$ $X^2$

Vocabulary, matching the corpus. $Q$ is spectrally regular when $B^2\ne0$ and spectrally degenerate when $B^2=0$. The degenerate set splits into the Jordan type, the nilpotent type, and the scalar line, where the last is semisimple with a single eigenvalue and is the vertex of the degenerate set.

The degree is two, the discriminant is $-4B^2$, and the four rows of the table are the four possible minimal polynomials. Everything below is a reading of one row.

1.3 Jordan–Chevalley: the element in the frame

$$ Q=S+N,\qquad SN=NS,\qquad S\text{ semisimple},\quad N\text{ nilpotent}. $$

The corpus states the tie with Peirce in the split case:

$$ Q=\sum_i\bigl(\lambda_i\varepsilon_i+n_i\bigr),\qquad n_i\in\varepsilon_i\mathbb{B}\varepsilon_i,\qquad n_i^{e_i}=0 . $$

The $\varepsilon_i$ are the spectral idempotents; in the Jordan algebra literature they are the Peirce idempotents of $Q$. They are the projections onto the generalized eigenspaces. The Peirce decomposition at the frame $\{\varepsilon_i\}$ organizes the algebra by those subspaces: the diagonal block $\varepsilon_i\mathbb{B}\varepsilon_i$ is the endomorphism algebra of the $i$-th generalized eigenspace, and the off-diagonal block $\varepsilon_i\mathbb{B}\varepsilon_j$ maps the $j$-th into the $i$-th. Each $n_i$ is the nilpotent part inside the diagonal block. So:

  • Peirce is the resolution of the algebra at the frame of the semisimple part;
  • Jordan–Chevalley is the splitting of the element inside the diagonal blocks;
  • eigen is the case $n_i=0$ for every $i$.

They are three readings of one resolution, not three nested theorems. The corpus says it in two places: The Eigenvalue Decomposition in a Jordan Algebra calls the spectral idempotents "the spectral idempotents or Peirce idempotents", and Nilpotent Elements and the Jordan–Chevalley Decomposition says the $n_i$ are "the nilpotent parts inside the blocks".

In $\mathbb{B}$ the degree is two, and the Peirce corner pattern computed in a frame adapted to $Q$ is the Jordan form of $Q$. Five rows, from the general element to the degenerate elements:

Type Condition Frame Non-zero Peirce corners JC
scalar $\vec Q=0$ $\{e_t\}$ one diagonal $S=Q$, $N=0$
semisimple, unit $B^2\ne0$, $QQ^{\nu}\ne0$ spectral $\{\Pi_+,\Pi_-\}$ two diagonal $S=Q$, $N=0$
semisimple, zero divisor $B^2\ne0$, $QQ^{\nu}=0$ spectral $\{\Pi_+,\Pi_-\}$ one diagonal $S=Q$, $N=0$
Jordan $B^2=0$, $\vec Q\ne0$, $Q_t\ne0$ $\{e,f\}$ with $N\in e\mathbb{B}f$ two diagonal and one off-diagonal $S=Q_t$, $N=\vec Q$
nilpotent $B^2=0$, $Q_t=0$, $Q\ne0$ $\{e,f\}$ with $Q\in e\mathbb{B}f$ one off-diagonal $S=0$, $N=Q$

Read the table as the Jordan form in Peirce coordinates: the diagonal corners carry the semisimple part, the off-diagonal corner carries the nilpotent part, and the pattern of non-zero corners, together with the scalar part, gives the type. The corner pattern is a normal form, not a frame-independent count: the frame is chosen from $Q$. The invariant content is the JC pair, and in $\mathbb{B}$ the pair and the type determine each other.

The last three rows are the degenerate ones. In the Jordan and nilpotent rows there is no spectral frame, because the element is defective; the frame $\{e,f\}$ that exhibits $N$ off-diagonal is an extra choice and is not unique. JC is canonical, Peirce needs a frame, and in the semisimple rows the spectral frame supplies it. The spectral Peirce decomposition is a case of JC; a general Peirce decomposition needs an arbitrary idempotent and is the frame in which JC is stated.

  • Corpus: covered by Nilpotent Elements and the Jordan–Chevalley Decomposition (the generalized eigenvalue section) and The Jordan–Chevalley Decomposition of a Matrix; the biquaternion implementation is The Jordan–Chevalley Decomposition of a Biquaternion.
  • Status: covered, including the multiplicative version $Q=SU$ for units in §The Multiplicative Form of that article.
  • Why it is form-free: semisimplicity, nilpotency and the scalar part are properties of the product.

1.4 The semisimple case: the eigenvalue decomposition

For $B^2\ne0$,

$$ Q=\lambda_+\tilde\Pi_++\lambda_-\tilde\Pi_-,\qquad \lambda_\pm=Q_t\pm iB,\qquad \tilde\Pi_\pm=\tfrac12(e_t\pm\vec\Xi),\qquad \vec\Xi=-i\vec Q/B . $$

The two projectors are the spectral idempotents, complementary and primitive. In their Peirce frame, $Q$ occupies the two diagonal corners and the off-diagonal corners vanish; that is the $n=0$ row of §1.3.

  • Corpus: covered by The Biquaternion Eigenvalue Decomposition, the eigenbasis articles, and The Eigenvalue Decomposition in a Clifford Algebra for the general dictionary.
  • Status: covered.
  • Degenerate cases. $B^2=0$, $\vec Q\ne0$: no eigenvalue decomposition, and no two-projector frame; the replacement is the Jordan basis. $B^2=0$, $\vec Q=0$: the one-term decomposition is unique but the two-projector refinement is not.

1.5 The zero divisors: a single Peirce corner

$$ QQ^{\nu}=0,\qquad Q\ne0 . $$

The condition is intrinsic: the conjugate product vanishes, so $Q$ is a zero divisor. Cayley–Hamilton gives $Q^2=2Q_tQ$ on this set. The two cases are the two corners:

  • $Q_t\ne0$: $Q/(2Q_t)$ is an idempotent, $B^2=Q_t^2\ne0$, and $Q$ is supported in one diagonal corner. This is the semisimple zero-divisor row of §1.3.
  • $Q_t=0$: $Q^2=0$, and $Q$ is supported in one off-diagonal corner, by the corner product of §1.2. This is the nilpotent row.

Conversely, every non-zero element of a single corner is a zero divisor. So the zero-divisor set is exactly the set of single-corner configurations, and the two kinds of zero divisor are the two kinds of corner. In the matrix model they are the rank-one matrices, which is why the word "rank" appears in the corpus; the biquaternion statement needs no rank.

  • Corpus: covered by N-Regularity and N-Degeneracy, Square Roots of One and the Idempotents, Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents, and the $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ Rank-One Matrices article for the model.
  • Status: covered. General rank factorization is rejected: in $2\times2$ it is this case plus the identity.
  • Why it is form-free: $QQ^{\nu}=0$ is a product identity, not a pairing.

1.6 LU: rejected

$$ Q=LU,\qquad L\text{ unit lower triangular},\quad U\text{ upper triangular}. $$

  • Status: rejected.
  • Why: it needs a chosen basis, the pivots are arbitrary, and in $2\times2$ it is one division. It carries no biquaternion content and no spectral information: it reads no eigenvalue, no rank and no idempotent.
  • Placement: it is not part of the spectral layer. It is recorded here only because it is algebraic, and it is rejected on the same line.

The natural first guess is half right, and the half that is wrong is worth stating.

  • Eigen is a case of Peirce. True: Peirce at the spectral frame with the off-diagonal blocks zero.
  • A zero divisor is a case of Peirce. True: a single non-zero corner.
  • Peirce and Jordan–Chevalley: the tie is real, and it cuts both ways. Peirce at an arbitrary idempotent is not a case of JC, because the frame is extra data. So the spectral Peirce decomposition is a case of JC, while the general Peirce decomposition is the frame in which JC is stated.

A compact reading: the characteristic polynomial decides which rows exist, the Peirce frame is the coordinate system, JC is the invariant content, eigen is the semisimple row, and a zero divisor is the one-corner row.

1.9 The article ladder: general to special

Two rules fix the order.

  • Peirce first. Peirce needs only an idempotent and decomposes the algebra; it carries no spectral hypothesis on the element. 2. The forms and their algebraic norms

The decompositions of §3–§7 rest on four forms. This section holds their data once, so the family sections do not repeat it. It is a digest of the technical note's §1, §2, §11A, §11C, §12 and §13, and it adds no theorem.

2.1 The four forms are the scalar part

Determinant versus form. §1 used $\det Q$ and never a pairing. The form $N$ agrees with $\det$ on the diagonal, $N(Q)=\det Q$, but the two are different objects: $\det$ is a polynomial invariant of the product and is multiplicative, while $N(x,y)$ is a pairing of two elements. §1 needs only the first.

Write $x=x_t+\vec x$, $y=y_t+\vec y$, $\operatorname{Sc}(x)=x_t$, and $\tau(x)=x_t-\vec x$, $x^{*}=\overline{x_t}+\overline{\vec x}$. Then, with the product of $\mathbb{B}$,

$$ \begin{aligned} P(x,y)&=\operatorname{Sc}(xy) &&= x_ty_t-(\vec x,\vec y),\\ N(x,y)&=\operatorname{Sc}(x\,\tau y) &&= x_ty_t+(\vec x,\vec y),\\ K(x,y)&=\operatorname{Sc}(x\,y^{*}) &&= x_t\overline{y_t}-(\vec x,\vec y)_{\bar{}},\\ H(x,y)&=\operatorname{Sc}\bigl(x\,(\tau y)^{*}\bigr) &&= x_t\overline{y_t}+(\vec x,\vec y)_{\bar{}}. \end{aligned} $$

Read the second column as two binary choices on the second slot. The star decides bilinear versus sesquilinear; $\tau$ decides Gram $E$ versus Gram $I$.

no star: bilinear star: sesquilinear
no $\tau$ $P=\operatorname{Sc}(xy)$, Gram $E$ $K=\operatorname{Sc}(xy^{*})$, Gram $E$
$\tau$ $N=\operatorname{Sc}(x\tau y)$, Gram $I$ $H=\operatorname{Sc}(x(\tau y)^{*})$, Gram $I$

The Gram matrices and signatures:

Form Gram Signature on $\mathbb{R}^8$ Multiplicative Definite
$P$ $E$ $(4,4)$ no no
$N$ $I_4$ $(4,4)$ yes no
$K$ $E$ $(2,6)$ no no
$H$ $I_4$ $(8,0)$ no yes

On the diagonal,

$$ P(Q)=Q_t^2-\lvert\vec Q\rvert^2,\quad N(Q)=Q_t^2+\lvert\vec Q\rvert^2,\quad K(Q)=\lvert Q_t\rvert^2-\lvert\vec Q\rvert^2,\quad H(Q)=\lvert Q_t\rvert^2+\lvert\vec Q\rvert^2 . $$

Four facts that drive everything below.

  • Only $N$ is multiplicative, and $N(Q)=\det\mathsf{M}_2(Q)$.
  • Only $H$ is definite, and $H(Q)=\lVert\mathsf{M}_2(Q)\rVert_F^2/2$ on the matrix image.
  • No form is both, by the zero divisors of §2.4.
  • $P$ is the scalar part of the plain product, with no conjugation at all. That is what makes it the first form, and why a decomposition that never takes a scalar part is earlier still.

Note on the slot convention. The technical note puts $\tau$ on the first slot, $P=N\circ(\tau\otimes\mathrm{id})$. The table above puts it on the second. The two agree because $\tau$ is an anti-automorphism: $\operatorname{Sc}(\tau x\cdot\tau y)=\operatorname{Sc}(\tau(yx))=\operatorname{Sc}(yx)=\operatorname{Sc}(xy)$.

2.2 Adjoints, isometry groups, dimensions

Form Adjoint Isometry group $\dim_\mathbb{R}$ Self-adjoint means
$P$ $A^{\#_P}=EA^{\mathsf T}E$ $O_4(\mathbb{C})$ 12 $E$-symmetric
$N$ $A^{\#_N}=A^{\mathsf T}$ $O_4(\mathbb{C})$ 12 symmetric
$H$ $A^{\#_H}=A^{*}$ $U(4)$ 16 Hermitian
$K$ $A^{\#_K}=EA^{*}E$ $U(1,3)$ 16 K-self-adjoint

$\operatorname{Isom}(P)=\{A:A^{\mathsf T}EA=E\}$ and $\operatorname{Isom}(N)=\{A:A^{\mathsf T}A=I\}$ are conjugate by $S=\operatorname{diag}(1,i,i,i)$, not equal: $A\in\operatorname{Isom}(P)\iff SAS^{-1}\in\operatorname{Isom}(N)$. Both are isomorphic to $O_4(\mathbb{C})$, and the adjoints differ. An $N$-theorem and its $P$-twin have the same group type and still read differently in a fixed basis.

$K$ is an operator-level form: the element-level $K$-isometries are only the central phases $U(1)$. $H$ has the element-level group $U(\mathbb{B})\cong U(2)$. $N$ has $\{N=1\}\cong SL_2(\mathbb{C})$, whose intersection with the transpose isometries is $O(2,\mathbb{C})$.

2.3 The four involutions

Involution Fixed space Form
$A\mapsto A^{*}$ Hermitian matrices $H$
$A\mapsto EA^{*}E$ K-self-adjoint matrices $K$
$A\mapsto A^{\mathsf T}$ symmetric matrices $N$
$A\mapsto EA^{\mathsf T}E$ $E$-symmetric matrices $P$

Caution. The technical note calls these four "the real forms of $M_2(\mathbb{C})$". Strictly they are four anti-linear involutions, and their fixed spaces are real vector spaces, not subalgebras: the Hermitian matrices are not closed under multiplication. The count of four is a count of involutions, not of real forms in the algebraic sense.

2.4 The engine

definite not definite
multiplicative empty, by the zero divisors $N$: algebra structure
not multiplicative $H$: topology $K$: indefinite geometry

The empty cell is why there are four forms and not one. In a division algebra it is occupied, the two properties do not separate, and a single norm serves both the algebra and the topology. Here they separate, and each occupied cell gives one form.

Two precisions.

  • "Unique survivor of the cell" holds up to normalisation. Any positive multiple of $H$ is also definite and not multiplicative. The canonical choices are fixed by $N(Q)=\det\mathsf{M}_2(Q)$ and $H(Q)=\lVert\mathsf{M}_2(Q)\rVert_F^2/2$.
  • $P$ is outside the table because it is neither multiplicative nor definite. It is still a form, the corner of §2.1 with no conjugation at all, and it is not a fourth cell: it is the plain product on which the other three are read. Its metric decompositions are not new; they are the N-decompositions in the $S$-basis (§3).

2.5 The conjugations

$$ S=\operatorname{diag}(1,i,i,i),\qquad S^{\mathsf T}ES=I_4,\qquad S^{*}=S^{-1},\qquad E=S^2 . $$

$$ N=P\circ(\mathrm{id}\otimes\tau),\qquad K=H\circ(\tau\otimes\mathrm{id}), $$

The first identity is the definition of $N$ from the plain product: $N$ is $P$ with $\tau$ in the second slot. It also reads $P=N\circ(\tau\otimes\mathrm{id})$, because $\tau$ may be moved from one slot to the other under $\operatorname{Sc}$; the primary object is $P$, and $N$ is its $\tau$-twist.

$$ A^{\#_P}=S^{-1}\bigl(SAS^{-1}\bigr)^{\#_N}S,\qquad A^{\#_K}=S^{-1}\bigl(SAS^{-1}\bigr)^{\#_H}S . $$

Hence $A\in\operatorname{Isom}(P)\iff SAS^{-1}\in\operatorname{Isom}(N)$, and $A\in\operatorname{Isom}(K)\iff SAS^{-1}\in\operatorname{Isom}(H)$.

Three consequences.

  • Every $P$-metric item is an $N$-item conjugated by $S$. That is why §3 keeps the form but rejects its decompositions as separate articles.
  • The same conjugation sends $K$-normal to $H$-normal, whose diagonal is complex. The note's K-spectral wants a real diagonal with signs; $K$-normality does not give one, and neither does $K$-self-adjointness. The matrix $C$ of §6 is $K$-self-adjoint with eigenvalues $\pm i\sqrt3$. This is the structural reason behind the $L_{\vec e_x}$ counterexample of §6.
  • On $Q\in\mathbb{R}^4$ the coefficientwise conjugation is the identity, so $P=K$ and $N=H$: the two bilinear forms and the two sesquilinear forms coincide in pairs. Four forms collapse to two, and the four-fold theory is genuinely complex. (The note pairs them the other way, $P=N$ and $H=K$; that is wrong, since $P$ and $N$ differ by the sign of the vector part on every non-pure element.)

The four shapes are one schema, cut at different points. The polar keeps one factor symmetric; the spectral makes the middle factor diagonal and one side trivial; the SVD makes the middle diagonal and keeps both sides; the flag makes the middle triangular.

The no-form layer of §1 has its own four shapes — additive, frame, diagonal Peirce, elimination — and they do not fit this table, because they do not use a form.

The main chain is a zigzag, not a tower of inclusions:

$$ \text{eigen}\;\xrightarrow{\text{restriction}}\;\text{H-spectral}\;\xrightarrow{\text{coarsening}}\;\text{SVD}\;\xrightarrow{\text{coarsening}}\;\text{polar}. $$

Going right loses information and gains domain. Going left gains information and loses domain. No member is "more general" in both senses at once.

The composite arrow not in the chain is

$$ \text{SVD}=\text{polar}\circ\text{spectral}(P). $$

It is a composition, not an inclusion. It holds when the spectral step holds. This is why the K-SVD is blocked: the spectral step fails before the composition starts.

3. $P$ — the scalar part of the plain product

$P$ is exactly the case of §2.1 with no conjugation:

$$ P(x,y)=\operatorname{Sc}(xy),\qquad P(Q)=\operatorname{Sc}(Q^2)=Q_t^2-\lvert\vec Q\rvert^2 . $$

So $P$ is the plain product read through the scalar pairing: no conjugation enters, and $N$ plays no part in its definition. The distinction is the whole reason §1 exists: a decomposition that multiplies but never takes a scalar part is form-free, while a decomposition that evaluates $\operatorname{Sc}(xy)$ uses $P$.

3.1 Data

$$ \operatorname{Gram}(P)=E,\qquad A^{\#_P}=EA^{\mathsf T}E,\qquad \operatorname{Isom}(P)=O_4(\mathbb{C}),\qquad \dim_\mathbb{R}=12 . $$

3.2 Its decompositions

With $S=\operatorname{diag}(1,i,i,i)$ and $S^{\mathsf T}ES=I_4$,

$$ \text{N-polar}(A)=S\,\text{P-polar}(S^{-1}AS)\,S^{-1}, $$

and the same change carries P-spectral to N-spectral and P-SVD to N-SVD. The dictionary runs in this direction on purpose: $P$ is the plain product and needs no conjugation, while $N$ is $P$ with $\tau$ inserted in the second slot. $P$ is the base and $N$ is the $\tau$-twist; $P$ is not a shadow of $N$.

What the form supplies: a positive cone, a topological norm, an orthonormal eigenbasis for normal elements. What it does not supply: multiplicativity.

What the form supplies: multiplicativity, the determinant, the invertibility criterion. What it does not supply: a cone, a topology, a positive notion.

What the form supplies: an indefinite scalar product, a Krein signature, a conditional $J$-positive cone. What it does not supply: an orthonormal eigenbasis.

  • K-polar. $A=UP$ with $U\in U(1,3)$ and $P$ K-self-adjoint, for invertible $A$ with $A^{\#K}A$ having no negative real eigenvalue. Research, see §8.3.
  • K-spectral. False as stated. $L_{\vec e_x}$ is $J$-normal and has spectrum $\{i,-i\}$, each twice. Rejected.
  • K-SVD. Blocked, not difficult: it contains the spectral step that just failed. Research, negative.
  • Flag. Conditional and basis-dependent. Rejected.
  • Group level. The Krein Isometry Group and Its Cartan Decomposition covers the group statement. At element level the K-isometries are only the central phases $U(1)$, so an element-level K-theory is nearly empty. $K$ is an operator-level form.

The K-SVD counterexample. Take $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $E=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$. Then $A$ is invertible and

$$ A^{\#K}A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix} $$

has eigenvalues $(1\pm i\sqrt3)/2$. Embedded in $\mathbb{C}^4$ with $E=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$, the same non-real eigenvalues survive. So invertibility gives neither real K-singular values nor an inertia to conserve.

The hybrid identities. With the same $E$, the matrix $C=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\-2&-1\end{pmatrix}$ is K-self-adjoint, has eigenvalues $\pm i\sqrt3$, and has H-singular values $3$ and $1$. The H-singular values are not the moduli of the K-eigenvalues, and the two SVDs do not share singular subspaces.

The cell's invariant is the inertia $(2,6)$ of $K$. None of the K-decompositions of the note is stated in terms of it: the note's K-SVD claims an inertia, and the counterexample above shows that the claim fails.

8.1 The Singular Values of a Biquaternion

  • Family H + N, shape SVD. Element level, $Q\in\mathbb{B}$.
  • What is new. The matrix SVD is already in the $M_2$ polar article. The new article is the element-level reading: the two-norm formula, the rank criterion, the equality case, and the degenerate strata. It must not restate the matrix theorem.
  • Theorem. With $M=\mathsf{M}_2(Q)$, $$ \sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2=2H(Q),\qquad \sigma_1\sigma_2=|N(Q)|,\qquad \sigma_\pm^2=H(Q)\pm\sqrt{H(Q)^2-|N(Q)|^2}. $$ The factor $2$ is real: $\mathsf{M}_2$ is an algebra isomorphism but not an isometry, $\lVert M\rVert_F^2=2H(Q)$.
  • Factorisation. Unitary biquaternions $Q_U,Q_V$ and a Hermitian positive semidefinite $Q_\Sigma$ give $Q=Q_UQ_\Sigma Q_V^{\#}$.
  • Rank. $\sigma_2>0$ iff $N(Q)\ne0$. If $N(Q)=0$, then $\sigma_2=0$, and $\sigma_1>0$ exactly when $Q\ne0$.
  • Equality case. $\sigma_1=\sigma_2$ iff $H=|N|$ iff $Q=\zeta q$ with $|\zeta|=1$ and $q\in\mathbb{R}^4$.
  • Worked values. $e_t$: $(1,1)$. $e_t+e_x$: $(\sqrt2,\sqrt2)$. $e_t+i e_x$: $(2,0)$. $e_t+e_x+i e_y$: $(1+\sqrt2,\sqrt2-1)$. Nilpotent $e_x+i e_y$: $(2,0)$. $e_t+e_x+e_y$: $(\sqrt3,\sqrt3)$.
  • Risks. Say the factor $2$. Do not call $\sigma_i$ eigenvalues. The unitary factors are unique only up to phases on degenerate singular subspaces.
  • Size: 300–400 lines.

8.2 The Algebraic Polar Decomposition of an N-Regular Biquaternion

  • Family N, shape polar. Element level, $N(Q)\ne0$.
  • What is new. The corpus has only $H$-polars. Here both adjectives change: symmetric instead of positive, complex orthogonal instead of unitary.
  • Theorem. With $M=\mathsf{M}_2(Q)$, $$ M=US,\qquad U\in O(2,\mathbb{C}),\quad S^{\mathsf T}=S,\quad S^2=M^{\mathsf T}M. $$ It exists for every invertible $M$ on a chosen branch. On the right-half-plane branch it exists and is unique iff $M^{\mathsf T}M$ has no negative real eigenvalue.
  • Basis dependence. The form is fixed in the coefficient basis, and the transpose is the matrix transpose, not one of the four conjugations. In the fixed model, $S^{\mathsf T}=S$ corresponds to $Q_y=0$, which is not an invariant subspace. Say so.
  • Worked examples. $M=\operatorname{diag}(2,3)$: $S=\operatorname{diag}(2,3)$, $U=I$. $M=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$: $S=I$, $U=M$. $M=\operatorname{diag}(1,i)$: $M^{\mathsf T}M=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$, two branches.
