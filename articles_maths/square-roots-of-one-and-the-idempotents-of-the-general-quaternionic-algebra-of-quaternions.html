Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the General Quaternionic Algebra

Introduction

This article classifies the square roots of the central value $+1$ in the general quaternionic bilinear product, and then the idempotents of that product, the elements the same squaring map keeps on their own square.

Both notions name a product, and throughout this article the product is the general quaternionic bilinear product $\tilde p\star\tilde q=\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde q$ of the general quaternionic algebra, the $\mathrm{GQA}$ of The 6 Algebraic Structures over the Quaternion $\mathbb{R}$ Space; the square of the article is the square of $\star$, and an unqualified idempotent is an idempotent of $\star$. The general definition of the square and of the roots of a central value, and the general definition of an idempotent, are Definitions for the Study of the 6 Algebraic Structures, §The Element Vocabulary.

The whole problem is decided by one identity. The square of every element of this product is the central element $N(\tilde p)e_0$, so the root equation of the value $\lambda$ reduces to the single real equation $N(\tilde p)=\lambda$, and the idempotent equation collapses to a quadratic on the scalar line. The roots of $+1$ are the level set $N=1$, the unit sphere $\mathrm{Sp}(1)$ of real dimension three; the idempotents are as small as they can be, the two trivial ones $0$ and $e_0$.

The results. The roots of $+1$ in the general quaternionic product are the elements of the norm-one level set $\mathrm{Sp}(1)$, the unit sphere of the quaternion algebra, of real dimension three; they contain the central pair $\pm e_0$ and the pure unit vectors $\hat\mu$. The idempotents of the product are $0$ and $e_0$ alone, and the product has no nontrivial idempotent. The two sets are not related by the halving map that carries the roots of $+1$ onto the idempotents of the plain product: the map $\tilde\xi\mapsto\tfrac12(e_0+\tilde\xi)$ produces an idempotent of $\star$ only at $\tilde\xi=\pm e_0$, and every other root of $+1$ is sent out of the idempotent set. On the operator side the left multiplication $L_{\tilde p}$ is a projector exactly when $\tilde p$ is an idempotent of the plain product, so over $\mathbb{R}$ the only projectors of that form are the zero operator and the identity. Read on the two remarkable subspaces, the idempotent equation leaves the two trivial idempotents in the centre and only $0$ in the vector subspace. The roots of $-1$ are empty, and they are quoted in one paragraph at the end of the roots-of-one part and used nowhere below.

Boundaries. The product, the square and the algebraic norm are Introduction to the General Quaternionic Algebra of Quaternions and Norm and Invertibility in the General Quaternionic Algebra; the general root problem for an arbitrary radicand is Square Roots of a General Element in the General Quaternionic Algebra; the roots of $0$ and the nilpotents of the product are Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents of the General Quaternionic Algebra; the zero divisors are Zero Divisors of the General Quaternionic Algebra; the idempotents of the plain product of the same space and the Hermitian idempotents of the complex chapter are Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the General Plain Algebra; the projectors and the operator theory are The Left Multiplications of the Quaternionic Product and the Opposite Monoid and The Specificities of the General Quaternionic Algebra; the structure-level comparison of the six root sets is Square Roots of a General Element in the 6 Algebraic Structures over the Quaternion $\mathbb{R}$ Space, and that of the six idempotent sets is Square Roots of One and the Idempotents in the 6 Algebraic Structures over the Quaternion $\mathbb{R}$ Space.

Conventions. $\mathbb{H}$ is the real quaternion algebra with basis $e_0=1,e_1,e_2,e_3$, the rules $e_k^2=-e_0$ and $e_1e_2=e_3$. Juxtaposition is the plain product and $\star$ the general quaternionic bilinear product $\tilde p\star\tilde q=\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde q$, with the conjugation $\tilde p^{\natural}=p_0-\mathbf p$ and $\tilde p=p_0e_0+\mathbf p$, $\mathbf p=p_1e_1+p_2e_2+p_3e_3$. The algebraic norm is

$$ N(\tilde p)=\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde p=p_0^2+\lvert\mathbf p\rvert^2=\sum_{\mu=0}^{3}p_\mu^2 , $$

positive definite, with polar form $\beta(\tilde p,\tilde q)=p_0q_0+\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf q$. A central value is an element $\lambda e_0$ with $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$, and $\hat\mu$ denotes a real unit vector of $\mathbb{R}^3$. The centre is the scalar line $\mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{H}}=\mathbb{R}e_0$ and the vector subspace is $\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})=\{\mathbf p\}$; the two are supplementary and orthogonal for the norm. A root of a central value carries the lower-case xi $\tilde\xi$ of the quaternion layer, and an idempotent the lower-case pi $\tilde\pi$.

The Root Equation and Its Reduction

Definition. Let $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$. A square root of the central value $\lambda$ in the general quaternionic product is an element $\tilde\xi$ with

$$ \tilde\xi\star\tilde\xi=\lambda e_0 . $$

The root is relative to the product, and this article uses the one of $\star$ throughout; the general definition of the square and of the roots of a central value is Definitions for the Study of the 6 Algebraic Structures, §The Element Vocabulary.

Theorem (the reduction). For every $\tilde\xi\in\mathbb{H}$,

$$ \tilde\xi\star\tilde\xi=\lambda e_0 \iff N(\tilde\xi)=\lambda . $$

Proof. The square is $N(\tilde\xi)e_0$ by Norm and Invertibility in the General Quaternionic Algebra, and $e_0\neq0$.

Corollary (the level set). The roots of the central value $\lambda$ are the level set $\{\tilde\xi:N(\tilde\xi)=\lambda\}$.

Remark (no vector equation). The reduction is a single real equation in the four coordinates, and it holds for every $\lambda$ at once. The plain product reduces the same problem to the pair $\xi_0^2-\lvert\boldsymbol\xi\rvert^2=\lambda$ and $2\xi_0\boldsymbol\xi=0$, whose second equation forces $\xi_0=0$ or $\boldsymbol\xi=0$ (Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the General Plain Algebra); the quaternionic square is central for every element, so the second equation does not arise and the root set is the level set itself. The general value is Square Roots of a General Element in the General Quaternionic Algebra, and the value $0$ is Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents of the General Quaternionic Algebra.

The Roots of Plus One

The roots of $+1$ are exactly the solutions of $N(\tilde\xi)=\xi_0^2+\lvert\boldsymbol\xi\rvert^2=1$, the unit sphere $\mathrm{Sp}(1)$ of the quaternion algebra, of real dimension three, containing the central pair $\pm e_0$ and the pure unit vectors $\hat\mu$.

Statement and Dimension

Theorem. The roots of $+1$ in the general quaternionic product are the solutions of

$$ N(\tilde\xi)=\xi_0^2+\lvert\boldsymbol\xi\rvert^2=1 , $$

the unit sphere $\mathrm{Sp}(1)$ of real dimension three. It contains the two central roots $\pm e_0$ and the pure unit vectors $\hat\mu$.

Proof. The criterion is the reduction. The norm is positive definite and nondegenerate, and its level set at the value $1$ is a nondegenerate quadric in the four real coordinates, of real dimension $4-1=3$. The elements $\pm e_0$ have $N=1$; a pure unit vector $\hat\mu$ has $\xi_0=0$ and $\lvert\boldsymbol\xi\rvert^2=1$, and satisfies the equation.

Corollary (the count). The root set of $+1$ is never a finite set; its cardinality is that of the continuum, and the article states the count by dimension rather than by cardinality, as the corpus does.

The Comparison with the Plain Product

The value $+1$ is read side by side with the plain product, and the exchange of the two signed values is the whole of the difference.

value $\lambda$ quaternionic $\tilde\xi\star\tilde\xi$ plain $\tilde\xi^2$
$-1$ no root the pure elements with $\lvert\boldsymbol\xi\rvert=1$, of real dimension two
$+1$ the norm-one level set $\mathrm{Sp}(1)$, of real dimension three $\{\pm e_0\}$ alone

Proposition. The two root sets of $-1$ are as different as they can be: the plain equation has a two-dimensional family of roots, the pure elements of norm one, and the quaternionic equation has none. The two root sets of $+1$ are exchanged in the same way: the plain equation has two roots, $\pm e_0$, and the quaternionic equation has the whole three-dimensional unit sphere.

Proof. The plain square is $(\xi_0^2-\lvert\boldsymbol\xi\rvert^2)e_0+2\xi_0\boldsymbol\xi$; the equation $\tilde\xi^2=\lambda e_0$ is the pair $\xi_0^2-\lvert\boldsymbol\xi\rvert^2=\lambda$, $2\xi_0\boldsymbol\xi=0$. For $\lambda=-1$ the second equation forces a pure root, and then $-\lvert\boldsymbol\xi\rvert^2=-1$ gives $\lvert\boldsymbol\xi\rvert=1$; for $\lambda=+1$ it forces $\xi_0=0$, which gives $-\lvert\boldsymbol\xi\rvert^2=1$, impossible, or $\boldsymbol\xi=0$, which gives $\xi_0=\pm1$. The quaternionic column is §Statement and Dimension and the emptiness at $-1$; the plain column is Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the General Plain Algebra.

Remark (the roots of $+1$ and the units). The quaternionic root set of $+1$ is exactly the norm-one shell $N=1$, a subgroup of the group of units of the plain algebra; the units of the plain algebra are the elements of nonzero norm, the shell is their norm-one stratum, and the two readings meet at the norm.

The roots of $-1$. The roots of $-1$ are empty, and this paragraph is all the article spends on them. A multiplication by a central scalar $c$ rescales the square by $c^2$, because the product is $\mathbb{R}$-bilinear and the scalar is central,

$$ (c\tilde\xi)\star(c\tilde\xi)=c^{2}\bigl(\tilde\xi\star\tilde\xi\bigr), $$

so no central multiple turns a root of $+1$ into a root of $-1$: over $\mathbb{R}$ the centre is the field $\mathbb{R}$, with no element of negative square and no central imaginary unit. The equation $N(\tilde\xi)=-1$ has no solution at all, the norm being positive definite, so the root set of $-1$ is the empty set. It is quoted here and used nowhere below.

The Idempotents of the Product

The idempotents of the general quaternionic product are $0$ and $e_0$ alone; the product has no nontrivial idempotent.

Definition and the Square

Definition. Let $\star$ be a product on $\mathbb{H}$ and let $\tilde\pi\in\mathbb{H}$. The element $\tilde\pi$ is idempotent of $\star$ when $\tilde\pi\star\tilde\pi=\tilde\pi$; the trivial idempotents are $0$ and the left unit $e_0$, and an idempotent different from both is nontrivial. The definition is Definitions for the Study of the 6 Algebraic Structures, §The Element Vocabulary, and it names the product. Throughout this part the product is $\star$.

Lemma (the square is central). For every $\tilde p\in\mathbb{H}$,

$$ \tilde p\star\tilde p=\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde p=N(\tilde p)\,e_0 , \qquad N(\tilde p)=p_0^2+p_1^2+p_2^2+p_3^2 . $$

Proof. With $\tilde p=p_0e_0+\mathbf p$ the product has scalar part $p_0^2+\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf p$ and vector part $p_0\mathbf p-p_0\mathbf p-\mathbf p\times\mathbf p=0$ at equal arguments; the square is $N(\tilde p)e_0$ (Introduction to the General Quaternionic Algebra of Quaternions).

The lemma is the reason the idempotent equation is trivial here and nontrivial for the plain product. The plain square of the same element is $p_0^2e_0+2p_0\mathbf p+\mathbf p^2$, whose vector part is not obliged to vanish; the quaternionic square carries no vector part at all, and the equation is a quadratic in one real variable.

The Classification

Theorem (idempotents). The solutions of $\tilde\pi\star\tilde\pi=\tilde\pi$ are $\tilde\pi=0$ and $\tilde\pi=e_0$; the general quaternionic product has no nontrivial idempotent.

Proof. By the lemma the left-hand side is $N(\tilde\pi)e_0$, an element of the centre $\mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{H}}$, so an idempotent is central, $\tilde\pi=Ae_0$ with $A\in\mathbb{R}$. For a central element the equation reads $A^2e_0=Ae_0$, that is $A^2=A$, or $A(A-1)=0$; the field $\mathbb{R}$ gives $A\in\{0,1\}$. Both values satisfy the equation.

Remark (the equation is weaker than centrality). The single scalar equation $N(\tilde\pi)=\pi_0$ is not idempotence; it must be read with centrality attached. Every pure element $\mathbf p\neq0$ has $N(\mathbf p)=\lvert\mathbf p\rvert^2\neq0$ and is not central, so it fails the equation and is not an idempotent; and the central element $-e_0$, of norm $1$, satisfies $N=1\neq\pi_0=-1$ and is not idempotent either. The scalar equation alone does not decide idempotence.

The Halving Map and the Roots of One

The halving map $\tilde\xi\mapsto\tfrac12(e_0+\tilde\xi)$ carries the plain roots of $+1$ onto the plain idempotents, but it is not a bijection of the roots of $+1$ of $\star$ onto the idempotents of $\star$: it yields an idempotent of $\star$ only at $\tilde\xi=\pm e_0$.

Proposition (the plain halving map). For the plain product the map $\tilde\xi\mapsto\tfrac12(e_0+\tilde\xi)$ is a bijection of the plain roots of $+1$ onto the plain idempotents, with inverse $\tilde\pi\mapsto2\tilde\pi-e_0$. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the plain roots of $+1$ are $\pm e_0$ and the plain idempotents are $0$ and $e_0$, and the map is the pair $\tilde\xi=e_0\mapsto\tilde\pi=e_0$ and $\tilde\xi=-e_0\mapsto\tilde\pi=0$ (Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the General Plain Algebra).

Proof. If $\tilde\xi\tilde\xi=e_0$ then $\bigl(\tfrac12(e_0+\tilde\xi)\bigr)^2=\tfrac14(e_0+2\tilde\xi+e_0)=\tfrac12(e_0+\tilde\xi)$; conversely from $\tilde\pi^2=\tilde\pi$ the element $\tilde\xi=2\tilde\pi-e_0$ has $\tilde\xi^2=4\tilde\pi-4\tilde\pi+e_0=e_0$. The two maps are inverse to each other.

Proposition (the map misses here). Let $\tilde\xi$ be a root of $+1$ of $\star$ and put $\tilde\pi=\tfrac12(e_0+\tilde\xi)$. Then

$$ \tilde\pi\star\tilde\pi-\tilde\pi=\tfrac14\bigl(\tilde\xi^{\natural}-\tilde\xi\bigr)=-\tfrac12\boldsymbol\xi , $$

so $\tilde\pi$ is an idempotent of $\star$ exactly when $\boldsymbol\xi=0$, that is exactly for $\tilde\xi=\pm e_0$.

Proof. The product is $\mathbb{R}$-bilinear, so

$$ (e_0+\tilde\xi)\star(e_0+\tilde\xi) = e_0\star e_0+e_0\star\tilde\xi+\tilde\xi\star e_0+\tilde\xi\star\tilde\xi = e_0+\tilde\xi+\tilde\xi^{\natural}+N(\tilde\xi)e_0 , $$

and $N(\tilde\xi)=1$; dividing by four and subtracting $\tilde\pi=\tfrac12(e_0+\tilde\xi)$ leaves $\tfrac14(\tilde\xi^{\natural}-\tilde\xi)$. Since $\tilde\xi^{\natural}-\tilde\xi=-2\boldsymbol\xi$, the difference vanishes exactly when the vector part of $\tilde\xi$ does, and the roots of $+1$ with vanishing vector part are $\pm e_0$.

The two propositions are the whole of the relation between the roots of one and the idempotents. The halving map sends the two central roots onto $e_0$ and $0$, and every other root of the three-dimensional sphere out of the idempotent set; the idempotents are read from the squaring map directly, in §The Classification.

Remark (the absent complex correspondence). In the complex chapter the plain idempotents are the Hermitian family $\tfrac12(e_0+i\hat\mu)$ over the real unit vectors $\hat\mu$, and there the halving map is a bijection of the plain roots of $+1$, the elements $i\hat\mu$ with $(i\hat\mu)^2=e_0$, onto them. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the centre is the field $\mathbb{R}$ itself, with no central imaginary unit, so the imaginary unit vectors do not exist, the Hermitian family has no real counterpart, and the map $\tilde\pi\mapsto2\tilde\pi-e_0$ followed by the division by the central imaginary has no analogue (Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the General Plain Algebra, the plain chapter over $\mathbb{C}$, for the Hermitian family).

The Trivial Family and Orthogonality

Definition. Two idempotents $\tilde\pi,\tilde\rho$ are orthogonal when $\tilde\pi\star\tilde\rho=0$; a family of pairwise orthogonal idempotents is complete when its members sum to $e_0$.

Proposition (the family). The idempotent set is $\{0,e_0\}$; the two are orthogonal, the origin being orthogonal to every element; there is no nontrivial idempotent and no pair of nontrivial orthogonal idempotents. The only complete orthogonal family of nonzero idempotents is $\{e_0\}$.

Proof. The classification gives the set. For orthogonality, $0\star e_0=0$ and $0\star0=0$, while $e_0\star e_0=e_0\neq0$; a nontrivial idempotent does not exist, so no pair of them can be orthogonal. A complete family of nonzero orthogonal idempotents summing to $e_0$ is available only as $\{e_0\}$, since any member other than $e_0$ would be a nontrivial idempotent, and $e_0$ cannot be split, $e_0$ being of norm one and the only idempotent of norm one.

Idempotents as Projections

Theorem (the projector criterion). The left multiplication $L_{\tilde p}(\tilde x)=\tilde p\star\tilde x$ is a projector, $L_{\tilde p}^{2}=L_{\tilde p}$, if and only if $\tilde p$ is an idempotent of the plain product. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the plain idempotents are $0$ and $e_0$ alone, so the only projectors of the form $L_{\tilde p}$ are the zero operator and the identity.

Proof. $L_{\tilde p}=L^{\mathrm{plain}}_{\tilde p^{\natural}}$ by the isotope identity, and the plain left multiplications are faithful; so $L_{\tilde p}^{2}=L_{\tilde p}$ exactly when $(\tilde p^{\natural})^2=\tilde p^{\natural}$, that is when $\tilde p$ is a plain idempotent. The plain idempotents are $0$ and $e_0$, and $L_{e_0}\tilde x=e_0^{\natural}\tilde x=\tilde x$ is the identity while $L_0$ is zero. The theorem and the monoid law $L_{\tilde p}L_{\tilde q}=L_{\tilde q\tilde p}$ are The Left Multiplications of the Quaternionic Product and the Opposite Monoid.

Remark. The complex sibling carries a two-parameter family of projectors, indexed by the plain idempotents, whose elements are not idempotents of the product. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the indexing set is the two-point set $\{0,e_0\}$, so the projector family is the trivial pair, and the displacement it records in the complex block has no real content.

The Idempotent Equation on the Remarkable Subspaces

Proposition (the idempotent equation on the two subspaces). On the centre the idempotents are $0$ and $e_0$; on the vector subspace the only idempotent is $0$.

subspace element $\tilde\pi\star\tilde\pi$ idempotents
$\mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{H}}$, the centre $Ae_0$ $A^2e_0$ $0$, $e_0$
$\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})$, the vector subspace $\mathbf p$ $\lvert\mathbf p\rvert^2e_0$ $0$

Proof. Each entry is the algebraic norm on the subspace of the same row, by the lemma. In the centre the equation leaves $A^2=A$, hence $A\in\{0,1\}$. In the vector subspace a central element equals a vector only at $0$; a nonzero pure element has square $\lvert\mathbf p\rvert^2e_0$, a nonzero central element, which differs from the pure $\mathbf p$, so the origin alone is idempotent.

Remark. The centre carries the two trivial idempotents and the vector subspace only $0$; neither remarkable subspace carries a nontrivial idempotent. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the quaternion algebra has exactly these two distinguished subspaces and no third, the fixed and the anti-fixed space of the natural sign (Introduction to the Remarkable Subspaces of the Quaternions); the Hermitian subspace of the complex chapter, which carries the nontrivial Hermitian idempotents there, therefore has no real counterpart, and the real idempotent set does not refine further.

The Peirce Decomposition It Does Not Carry

A nonzero idempotent of an associative unital algebra carries the Peirce decomposition $A=A_1\oplus A_{1/2}\oplus A_0$ with $A_\lambda=\{x:\Pi x=\lambda x\}$, and the decomposition is the source of the minimal ideals and the matrix units. The construction uses associativity twice, in reading the two maps as projections and in verifying that their images add.

For the general quaternionic product there is nothing to attach it to. The only nonzero idempotent is the left unit $e_0$, and $L_{e_0}$ is the identity, so the only Peirce piece is the whole algebra. The nontrivial decomposition, the frame of two orthogonal idempotents and its Peirce lines, is a property of the plain algebra alone, and over $\mathbb{R}$ the plain algebra is a division algebra, whose only idempotents are $0$ and $e_0$ and which carries no nontrivial frame either. What the product has in place of the decomposition is the centrality of the square and the trivial projectors of §Idempotents as Projections.

The Idempotents and the Nilpotents

Theorem (disjoint classes). No element of the block is both an idempotent and a nilpotent; the only idempotent whose square vanishes is the origin.

Proof. The idempotents of $\star$ are $0$ and $e_0$, and the nilpotents are the nonzero square-zero elements; the square-zero set of the block is the origin alone (Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents of the General Quaternionic Algebra), so no nonzero idempotent is square-zero and the nilpotent class is empty. Of the two idempotents, $e_0\star e_0=e_0\neq0$ and $0\star0=0$, so the origin is the only idempotent whose square vanishes. The two classes meet in no element.

Summary

In the general quaternionic bilinear product the square of every element is the central element $N(\tilde p)e_0$, so the root equation of a central value reduces to the single real equation $N(\tilde p)=\lambda$ and the idempotent equation forces centrality. The roots of $+1$ are the solutions of $N=1$, the unit sphere $\mathrm{Sp}(1)$ of real dimension three; they contain the central pair $\pm e_0$ and the pure unit vectors $\hat\mu$, and they are exactly the units of the plain algebra. The roots of $-1$ are empty, because no central scalar can flip the sign of a square over $\mathbb{R}$ and the norm is definite; they are quoted in one paragraph and used nowhere. The idempotents of the product are $0$ and $e_0$ alone, and the product has no nontrivial idempotent; the two form a family of orthogonal idempotents whose only complete nonzero subfamily is $\{e_0\}$. The halving map that carries the plain roots of $+1$ onto the plain idempotents produces an idempotent of $\star$ only at $\pm e_0$, so the sphere of roots of one and the two-point idempotent set do not correspond, and the Hermitian family of the complex chapter has no real counterpart. On the operator side $L_{\tilde p}$ is a projector exactly when $\tilde p$ is a plain idempotent, so over $\mathbb{R}$ the only projectors of the form $L_{\tilde p}$ are the zero operator and the identity. Read on the two remarkable subspaces, the idempotent equation leaves the two trivial idempotents in the centre and only $0$ in the vector subspace, no element is both an idempotent and a nilpotent, and the origin is the only idempotent whose square vanishes.
