The Specificities of the Complex Algebra

Introduction

This article states what is peculiar to the complex field among the twelve algebraic structures of the corpus. The four general products, with their symmetrisations and antisymmetrisations, make the twelve; on a commutative base ten of the twelve coincide two-and-two with the two that survive, and the four antisymmetric ones vanish. The specificities of $\mathbb{C}$ are the reasons: the field laws, the absence of zero divisors, the absence of nilpotents and of nontrivial idempotents, the irreducibility of the polynomial that defines it, the algebraic closure, and its place among the finite-dimensional real division algebras.

The article closes the specific theory of the complex algebra: it is the verdict, not a catalogue. It does not prove the classes of element — they are The Classes of Element of the Complex Algebra, above this article — and it does not restate the products or the forms, which are The Two Products of the Complex Space and The Two Forms and the Two Algebraic Norms of the Complex Space. The multiplication of the field, the definition of $\mathbb{C}$ as $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^{2}+1)$, the fundamental theorem of algebra, the algebraic closure of $\mathbb{R}$ and the non-orderability of $\mathbb{C}$ are Complex Algebra. The norm, the invertibility criterion and the group of units are Complex Norm and Invertibility; completeness is a topological property and belongs to the same place, and the article only points to it.

Interpretation. The element is $A=a+ia'$; the base involution is the bar $\bar A=a-ia'$; ${}^{*}=\bar{\cdot}$. The unit of the field is written $1$. The four forms are $B=N=AC$ and $H=K=A\bar C$, and the two algebraic norms are $A^{2}$ and $A\bar A$; the letters $B$, $N$, $H$, $K$ are never elements.

What the Twelve Structures Collapse To

The two slots of the general product are its two arguments, the first carrying the conjugation of the coefficient ring and the second the identity or the Hermitian conjugation. On $\mathbb{C}$ the first slot carries the identity, so the four settings of the general theory collapse two-and-two onto two objects: the field product $AC$, with the identity in the second slot, and the sesquilinear product $A\bar C$, with the bar. The bilinear settings name the multiplication of the field and the sesquilinear settings name the derived product with the bar, and the four antisymmetrised products vanish, each being the antisymmetric part of a product whose two slots commute in the class-preserving exchange. The two surviving structures are the field under $AC$ and the sesquilinear structure under $A\bar C$, and they are not the same structure: the first is associative with a two-sided unit, the second is non-associative with a right unit alone. The statements are The Two Products of the Complex Space; the two surviving products are the subject of The Sesquilinear Product of the Complex Space, below this article in this category.

The Field Laws

Proposition. The complex algebra is associative, commutative, unital, and has no zero divisor; every nonzero element is invertible.

Proof. Associativity and commutativity are read on the product $AC=(ac-a'c')+i(ac'+a'c)$, which is the product of the field; the unit is $1$; the absence of zero divisors and the invertibility of every nonzero element are The Classes of Element of the Complex Algebra, §The Zero Divisors and the Annihilators.

These are the laws that the general non-commutative algebra fails one by one. The consequence for the classes is that the zero divisors, the annihilators, the nilpotents, the non-trivial idempotents and the proper ideals all vanish at once, and the article names that consequence once and does not repeat it.

The Nilpotents and the Idempotents

Proposition. In either product, $0$ and $1$ are the only idempotents of $\mathbb{C}$, and $0$ is the only nilpotent.

Proof. This is The Classes of Element of the Complex Algebra, §The Idempotents and §The Nilpotents.

The two idempotents are forced by the ring axioms: the additive and the multiplicative identity are idempotent in every unital ring. The proposition says that $\mathbb{C}$ has no others, that is, no nontrivial idempotent: no family filling a curve, no minimal left ideal to generate, no nontrivial Peirce decomposition. The nilpotent-free property is what an integral domain is.

The Irreducible Polynomial and the Algebraic Closure

The field is presented as the quotient $\mathbb{C}=\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^{2}+1)$: the polynomial $x^{2}+1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$, of degree two, and the quotient is a field. This is the algebraic specificity of the base, and it has the following reading in the corpus. $\mathbb{C}$ is the quadratic extension of the ordered field $\mathbb{R}$ by a root of $-1$, so that the two roots of the defining polynomial are the two roots of $-1$, $i$ and $-i$, and they are exchanged by the base involution. The polynomial and the extension are Complex Algebra; the two roots are The Classes of Element of the Complex Algebra, §The Roots of the Distinguished Values.

The algebraic closure of $\mathbb{C}$ is the second specificity: every non-constant polynomial with coefficients in $\mathbb{C}$ has a root in $\mathbb{C}$ and splits into linear factors there. The statement is the fundamental theorem of algebra, owned by Complex Algebra; the specificities article records only its consequence for the root classes. In a field a quadratic equation has at most two roots, and over an algebraically closed field it has exactly two counted with multiplicity, so that every square-root problem $Z^{2}=W$ of the plain product is solvable, with two opposite solutions for $W\neq0$ and the single solution $0$ for $W=0$, as in the classes article.

The Finite-Dimensional Real Division Algebras

Theorem (Frobenius). The finite-dimensional associative real division algebras are $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$ and $\mathbb{H}$, up to isomorphism.

Corollary. Among the finite-dimensional associative real division algebras, $\mathbb{C}$ is the only one that is commutative and cannot be ordered.

Proof. Of $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$ and $\mathbb{H}$, the algebra $\mathbb{H}$ is not commutative, and $\mathbb{R}$ is ordered by its usual order, which is compatible with its multiplication; the field $\mathbb{C}$ is commutative and carries no order compatible with its multiplication, since a compatible order would force $-1=i^{2}>0$ from $i\neq0$. Hence $\mathbb{C}$ is the only algebra in the list with both properties.

Frobenius's theorem is cited as standard, as it is in Complex Algebra; the non-orderability of $\mathbb{C}$ is Complex Algebra and Galois Theory of $\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}$. The octonions are omitted, the theorem being stated for the associative algebras, and the split system $\mathbb{D}$ is excluded because it is not a division algebra. The place of $\mathbb{C}$ in the list is the reason the chapter is short: the complex field is the largest of the three that is commutative, so that non-commutativity, which is the source of ten of the twelve structures, has no instance here.

The Consequences

Three consequences close the article.

On the classes. Every class of element that depends on a zero divisor, a nilpotent or a nontrivial idempotent is empty or two-point, and the classes are The Classes of Element of the Complex Algebra, above this article.

On the two products. The field product is associative and has a two-sided unit; the sesquilinear product keeps a right unit and no left unit, since $A\bar 1=A$ but $1\bar A=\bar A$, which equals $A$ only for real $A$. The two products therefore differ on exactly the laws the slot analysis predicts, and the comparison of the two is The Two Products and Their Two Slots, below this article in this category.

On the two forms. The two bilinear forms coincide with the two sesquilinear ones two-and-two, $B=N=AC$ and $H=K=A\bar C$, and the two algebraic norms are $A^{2}$ and $A\bar A$. The forms are The Two Forms and the Two Algebraic Norms of the Complex Space; the definiteness of $H$, its cone and its modules are The Hermitian Form and the Positivity of the Complex Algebra, below this article in this category.

Summary

The complex algebra is the field: associative, commutative, unital, without zero divisors, with $0$ and $1$ as the only idempotents and $0$ as the only nilpotent. Of the twelve structures, ten collapse two-and-two onto the field product $AC$ and the sesquilinear product $A\bar C$ — the four bilinear ones coincide with the multiplication of the field, the four sesquilinear ones with the derived product — and the four antisymmetric ones vanish. The base is the quadratic extension $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^{2}+1)$ by a root of $-1$, algebraically closed, and it is the only finite-dimensional real division algebra that is both commutative and non-orderable. Every emptiness of the class catalogue and every coincidence of the structures follows from the field laws, which is why the chapter is selective and not mechanical.
