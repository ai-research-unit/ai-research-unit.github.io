Eigenvalue Decomposition and Eigenbasis of a Biquaternion

Introduction

The biquaternion algebra $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$ is simple and four-dimensional over $\mathbb{C}$. This article reads the eigenvalue decomposition of a biquaternion $\tilde Q=Q_te_t+\vec Q$ in the distinct case $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2\neq0$: the writing

$$ \tilde Q=\lambda_+\tilde\Pi_++\lambda_-\tilde\Pi_-, \qquad \lambda_\pm=Q_t\pm i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}, \quad \tilde\Pi_\pm=\tfrac12(e_t\pm\vec\Xi), \qquad \vec\Xi=-\frac{i\vec Q}{\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}}. $$

Here the coefficients $\lambda_\pm$ are the eigenvalues and the idempotents $\tilde\Pi_\pm$ are the eigenprojections. The eigenvalues are distinct in this regime. The article is the noncommutative counterpart of Eigenvalue Decomposition and Eigenbasis of a Split-Complex Number and Eigenvalue Decomposition and Eigenbasis of a Reduced Biquaternion. The general definition and the existence theorem are those of The Eigenvalue Decomposition of an Element; the biquaternion content is that, in the regime of distinct eigenvalues, the pair is the pair of idempotents of a pure root of $+1$, and the decomposition is the Peirce decomposition attached to that root in Ideals and Peirce Decomposition of the Biquaternion Algebra.

The results. The spectrum of $\tilde Q=Q_te_t+\vec Q$ is $\{Q_t+i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2},Q_t-i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}\}$, and in the distinct case $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2\neq0$ the eigenprojections are the two idempotents of the pure root $\vec\Xi=-i\vec Q/\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}$. They are noncentral and vary with $\tilde Q$, the eigenvalue decomposition is the Peirce decomposition relative to that root read on the element, and the diagonal part is the commutative plane $\mathbb{C}[\tilde Q]$. The polynomial calculus is the calculus on $\mathbb{C}[\tilde Q]$, the unit group is the invertible group with a surjective but non-injective eigenvalue map, and the Jordan and nilpotent regimes are the cases with no eigenvalue decomposition. The article also constructs the Q-adapted Peirce basis, the two nilpotents of the mixed corners, and the eigenbasis of left multiplication; it separates the eigenspaces of left multiplication, which are the two right ideals, from the left ideals, which are the spinor spaces. The two minimal left ideals $S_\pm=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_\pm$ are the spinor spaces; each is a complex plane and a spinor is a pair of complex coordinates.

Conventions. The notation is fixed in Conventions in Mathematics: the base is $e_t,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z$, a general element is $\tilde Q=Q_te_t+\vec Q$ with $\vec Q=Q_x\vec e_x+Q_y\vec e_y+Q_z\vec e_z$ and complex coefficients, an idempotent is $\tilde\Pi$, a root of $+1$ is $\tilde\Xi$ and a pure root is $\vec\Xi$, and a pure nilpotent is $\vec\Upsilon$. Put $B=\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}$. In the distinct case the Q-adapted basis is $(\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_-,\vec\Upsilon_+,\tilde\Pi_-)$, the general element in it is $\tilde Q_{\mathrm{sp}}=Q_{\mathrm{sp},11}\tilde\Pi_++Q_{\mathrm{sp},12}\vec\Upsilon_-+Q_{\mathrm{sp},21}\vec\Upsilon_++Q_{\mathrm{sp},22}\tilde\Pi_-$, and the two spinor spaces are $S_\pm=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_\pm$, with coordinates $(Q_{\mathrm{sp},11},Q_{\mathrm{sp},21})$ on $S_+$ and $(Q_{\mathrm{sp},12},Q_{\mathrm{sp},22})$ on $S_-$. Boundaries. The split-complex and reduced biquaternion cases are those of Eigenvalue Decomposition and Eigenbasis of a Split-Complex Number and Eigenvalue Decomposition and Eigenbasis of a Reduced Biquaternion; the quaternion case, with its left and right eigenvalues, is the subject of Quaternion Spectral Theory; the exponential and the logarithm built on the decomposition are those of Biquaternion Elementary Functions; and the spectral theorem in its general form is that of Biquaternion Spectral Theory. The Q-adapted construction, the nilpotents and the spinor spaces are developed here; the standard Peirce decomposition is that of Ideals and Peirce Decomposition of the Biquaternion Algebra. The article cites these and does not restate them. The other products of the biquaternion space carry nilpotents and idempotents relative to the product, and are outside its scope.

The Spectrum and the Characteristic Polynomial

Definition. Let $\tilde Q\in\mathbb{B}$. A complex scalar $\mu$ is an eigenvalue of $\tilde Q$ if $\tilde Q-\mu e_t$ is not a unit in $\mathbb{B}$; the set of eigenvalues is the spectrum $\sigma(\tilde Q)$. This is the definition of The Eigenvalue Decomposition of an Element, §The Eigenvalues of an Element.

Notation. The two eigenvalues are written $\lambda_\pm=Q_t\pm i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}$ when their values are exhibited, the radical being one of the two square roots. The test scalar of the definition is $\mu$, so the letter $\lambda$ is reserved for the two eigenvalues. The pair is the analogue of $\lambda_\pm=q\pm q'$ of Eigenvalue Decomposition and Eigenbasis of a Split-Complex Number and of $\lambda_\pm=Q_{\blacktriangle}\pm Q_{\blacktriangledown}$ of Eigenvalue Decomposition and Eigenbasis of a Reduced Biquaternion.

The pure root of the vector part. The square of the vector part is central, $$ \vec Q^2=-(Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2)e_t, $$ as in Biquaternion Spectral Theory, §The Biquaternion Spectrum.

Assume $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2\neq0$. Put $$ \vec U=\frac{\vec Q}{\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}},\qquad \vec\Xi=-i\vec U=-\frac{i\vec Q}{\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}}. $$ Then $\vec U$ is a pure unit element of the biquaternion layer, $\vec U^2=-e_t$, and $\vec\Xi$ is a pure root of $+1$: $$ \vec\Xi^2=(-i)^2\vec U^2=e_t. $$ Conversely $\vec Q=\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}\,\vec U=i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}\,\vec\Xi$. The root is pure, so it carries the arrow and not the tilde (§Pure Elements and their Coefficients of Conventions in Mathematics), and it belongs to the roots classified in Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the Biquaternion Algebra, §The Roots of One. For a real vector part, the square root may be taken real and positive, $\vec U$ is then the real unit vector $\vec u$, and $\vec\Xi=-i\vec u$ lies in the family $i\vec u$ of the pure roots. More generally, for $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2\neq0$, $\vec U$ is real exactly when $\vec Q$ is a complex multiple of a real vector, equivalently when $\tilde Q$ is normal (§The Hermitian Case); otherwise $\vec U$ and $\vec\Xi$ have nonzero imaginary parts.

The choice is two-fold: the negative of a square root is the other square root, and it replaces $\vec U$ and $\vec\Xi$ by their opposites. Only $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2$ is determined by $\tilde Q$, and with it the unordered pair $\{\vec\Xi,-\vec\Xi\}$; in the real case the convention that the square root be positive removes the choice.

Theorem. The spectrum of $\tilde Q=Q_te_t+\vec Q$ is $$ \sigma(\tilde Q)=\{\lambda_+,\lambda_-\}, \qquad \lambda_\pm=Q_t\pm i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}. $$ The radical is one of the two square roots, and the other choice exchanges $\lambda_+$ and $\lambda_-$. In the real case the positive root is taken, $\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}=|\vec Q|>0$.

Proof. Since $\vec Q=i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}\,\vec\Xi$, $$ (\tilde Q-Q_te_t)^2=\vec Q^2=-(Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2)e_t, $$ hence $$ \tilde Q^2-2Q_t\tilde Q+\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}e_t=0,\qquad \langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=Q_t^2+Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2. $$ This is the quadratic identity of Biquaternion Spectral Theory, §The Biquaternion Spectrum. The reduced norm of $\tilde Q-\mu e_t$ is $$ (\tilde Q-\mu e_t)(\tilde Q-\mu e_t)^\natural=(Q_t-\mu)^2+Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2, $$ so by Introduction to the Biquaternion Algebra, §The Reduced Norm and Invertibility, $\tilde Q-\mu e_t$ is not a unit exactly when $(Q_t-\mu)^2=-(Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2)$, that is exactly when $\mu=\lambda_\pm$.

Characteristic polynomial. The characteristic polynomial of $\tilde Q$ is $$ p_{\tilde Q}(X)=X^2-2Q_tX+\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=(X-Q_t)^2+Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2. $$ Its discriminant is $$ (\lambda_+-\lambda_-)^2=\left(2i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}\right)^2=-4(Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2), $$ so the eigenvalues are distinct exactly when $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2\neq0$.

Minimal polynomial. For $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2\neq0$ the minimal polynomial is $p_{\tilde Q}$, which is squarefree. For $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2=0$, $\vec Q\neq0$, it is $(X-Q_t)^2$; for $\vec Q=0$, it is $X-Q_t$.

The Eigenprojections

The eigenprojections of a biquaternion with $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2\neq0$ are the two primitive idempotents $\tilde\Pi_\pm$ of the pure root $\vec\Xi$.

Put $$ \tilde\Pi_+=\frac12(e_t+\vec\Xi),\qquad \tilde\Pi_-=\frac12(e_t-\vec\Xi), $$ the two idempotents of the root $\vec\Xi$ under the halving map of Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the Biquaternion Algebra, §The Idempotents. By that article the pair is complete and orthogonal, $\tilde\Pi_++\tilde\Pi_-=e_t$ and $\tilde\Pi_+\tilde\Pi_-=0$, and the root is their difference, $\vec\Xi=\tilde\Pi_+-\tilde\Pi_-$; by Ideals and Peirce Decomposition of the Biquaternion Algebra, §Idempotents and Orthogonal Idempotents, each member is primitive.

They are the eigenprojections of $\tilde Q$: $$ \tilde Q\tilde\Pi_+=\lambda_+\tilde\Pi_+,\qquad \tilde Q\tilde\Pi_-=\lambda_-\tilde\Pi_-. $$ Indeed, since $\vec Q=i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}\,\vec\Xi$ and $\vec\Xi^2=e_t$, $$ \tilde Q\tilde\Pi_+=(Q_te_t+i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}\,\vec\Xi)\frac12(e_t+\vec\Xi)=\frac12(Q_t+i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2})(e_t+\vec\Xi)=\lambda_+\tilde\Pi_+, $$ and, with $Q_t-i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}$ in place of $Q_t+i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}$ and $e_t-\vec\Xi$ in place of $e_t+\vec\Xi$, $$ \tilde Q\tilde\Pi_-=\lambda_-\tilde\Pi_-. $$

Equivalently, by the eigenprojector formula of The Lagrange–Sylvester Eigenprojector Formula, §The Eigenprojector Formula, $$ \tilde\Pi_+=\frac{\tilde Q-\lambda_-e_t}{\lambda_+-\lambda_-},\qquad \tilde\Pi_-=\frac{\tilde Q-\lambda_+e_t}{\lambda_--\lambda_+},\qquad \lambda_+-\lambda_-=2i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}. $$ Hence $\tilde\Pi_\pm$ are polynomials in $\tilde Q$, and in particular commute with $\tilde Q$.

Dependence on the element. The eigenprojections $\tilde\Pi_\pm$ vary with $\tilde Q$: they are the two idempotents of the pure root $\vec\Xi=-i\vec Q/\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}$, and the root is recovered from them as $\vec\Xi=\tilde\Pi_+-\tilde\Pi_-$. This is the structural difference from the split-complex and reduced biquaternion cases, where the eigenprojections are the fixed central idempotents $\Pi_\pm$ (Eigenvalue Decomposition and Eigenbasis of a Split-Complex Number, §The Eigenprojections; Eigenvalue Decomposition and Eigenbasis of a Reduced Biquaternion, §The Eigenprojections). Here the dependence on the element lies in both the eigenvalues and the idempotents.

The Eigenvalue Decomposition

Every biquaternion with distinct eigenvalues is the complex linear combination of $\tilde\Pi_+,\tilde\Pi_-$ with its eigenvalues as coefficients.

Summing the two eigenvalue relations, $$ \boxed{\ \tilde Q=\lambda_+\tilde\Pi_++\lambda_-\tilde\Pi_-\ } =(Q_t+i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2})\tilde\Pi_++(Q_t-i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2})\tilde\Pi_-. $$

Separating scalar and vector parts, $$ \tilde Q=\frac{\lambda_++\lambda_-}{2}e_t+\frac{\lambda_+-\lambda_-}{2}\vec\Xi=Q_te_t+i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}\,\vec\Xi=Q_te_t+\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}\,\vec U. $$ So the decomposition writes $\tilde Q$ as a complex linear combination of the two primitive idempotents of $\vec\Xi$, with the eigenvalues as coefficients.

This is an eigenvalue decomposition in the sense of The Eigenvalue Decomposition of an Element, §The Eigenvalue Decomposition: the coefficients $\lambda_\pm$ are distinct, the idempotents $\tilde\Pi_\pm$ are nonzero, orthogonal and complete, and they are the eigenprojections (the eigenprojectors of that article).

Example. For $\tilde Q=(1+i)e_t+\vec e_x+i\vec e_y+\vec e_z$, $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2=1$, $\vec U=\vec e_x+i\vec e_y+\vec e_z$ and $\vec\Xi=-i\vec e_x+\vec e_y-i\vec e_z$, so $\tilde\Pi_\pm=\frac12(e_t\pm\vec\Xi)$, and $$ (1+i)e_t+\vec e_x+i\vec e_y+\vec e_z=(1+2i)\tilde\Pi_++\tilde\Pi_-. $$ The scalar-vector form confirms it: $\frac{\lambda_++\lambda_-}{2}e_t+\frac{\lambda_+-\lambda_-}{2}\vec\Xi=(1+i)e_t+i\vec\Xi=\tilde Q$.

Uniqueness. By the uniqueness theorem of The Eigenvalue Decomposition of an Element, §Uniqueness, an element with distinct eigenvalues has a unique eigenprojection attached to each eigenvalue. Here the two eigenprojections are the two idempotents of the root $\vec\Xi$, and the root is read back from them as $\vec\Xi=\tilde\Pi_+-\tilde\Pi_-$. The unordered pair $\{\tilde\Pi_+,\tilde\Pi_-\}$ is determined by $\tilde Q$; the ordered pair is determined only up to the exchange that sends the square root to its negative and $\vec\Xi$ to $-\vec\Xi$. The labelling is canonical when the square root is chosen real and positive, which is possible exactly when $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2$ is a positive real number; otherwise the choice of square root is a convention.

Semisimplicity. Since $\lambda_+\neq\lambda_-$, the minimal polynomial $p_{\tilde Q}$ is squarefree, and $\tilde Q$ is semisimple (Nilpotent Elements and the Jordan–Chevalley Decomposition, §Semisimple Elements). The decomposition is the diagonalisation of $\tilde Q$ with respect to the noncentral idempotents $\tilde\Pi_\pm$.

The Peirce Decomposition Relative to the Eigenprojection

The Peirce decomposition relative to $\tilde\Pi_+$ has two diagonal lines and two square-zero mixed lines, and its diagonal part is the centraliser of $\tilde Q$.

Let $$ \mathbb{B}_{\sigma\tau}=\tilde\Pi_\sigma\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_\tau,\qquad \sigma,\tau\in\{+,-\}, $$ be the Peirce corners of the pair, in the notation of Ideals and Peirce Decomposition of the Biquaternion Algebra, §The Biquaternion Case. The Peirce decomposition is $$ \mathbb{B}=\mathbb{B}_{++}\oplus\mathbb{B}_{+-}\oplus\mathbb{B}_{-+}\oplus\mathbb{B}_{--}. $$ Each corner is one complex line, the diagonal corners are $\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_+$ and $\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_-$, and the two mixed corners are square-zero. This is the decomposition attached to the root $\vec\Xi=-i\vec Q/\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}$ in §The Peirce Decomposition of that article.

The diagonal part is $$ \mathbb{B}_{++}\oplus\mathbb{B}_{--}=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_+\oplus\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_-=\mathbb{C}[\tilde Q]=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{e_t,\tilde Q\}. $$ It is a commutative subalgebra isomorphic to $\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}$. By The Centraliser and the Diagonal Peirce Part, §The theorem, applied to the eigenvalue decomposition, it is the centraliser of $\tilde Q$; by Off-Diagonal Peirce Components and Noncentral Idempotents, §The Commutator Interpretation, the mixed part is $[\tilde\Pi_+,\mathbb{B}]$, so an element commutes with $\tilde Q$ exactly when its two mixed Peirce components vanish.

On $\tilde Q$ the Peirce decomposition reads $$ \tilde Q=\lambda_+\tilde\Pi_++\lambda_-\tilde\Pi_-, $$ with zero mixed components. The eigenvalue decomposition is therefore the Peirce decomposition read on the element: the element lies in the diagonal part, and its two diagonal Peirce coordinates are its eigenvalues $Q_t\pm i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}$. The mixed corners are the directions that fail to commute with $\tilde Q$.

The two signs of the pair are the two eigenvalues of multiplication by the root: an element of $\mathbb{B}_{\sigma\tau}$ is multiplied by $\sigma$ on the left and by $\tau$ on the right by $\vec\Xi$ (§The Biquaternion Case). The diagonal corners are the components on which the two signs agree; $\tilde Q$ acts on them by $\lambda_+$ and $\lambda_-$ respectively. The construction of the mixed generators $\vec\Upsilon_\pm$ is the subject of §The Peirce Corners and the Nilpotents; the Q-adapted basis and the eigenbasis of left multiplication are the subject of §The Q-Adapted Peirce Basis and §The Eigenbasis of Left Multiplication.

The Q-Adapted Peirce Basis, the Nilpotents, and the Eigenbasis

Fix $\tilde Q$ with $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2\neq0$. Its own root carries a Peirce pair, the two mixed corners carry a pair of nilpotents, and the four elements form a basis in which left multiplication by $\tilde Q$ is diagonal. The basis is an eigenbasis of left multiplication; the two minimal left ideals are the eigenspaces of right multiplication, not of left multiplication. This is the Q-dependent specialisation of the Peirce decomposition of Ideals and Peirce Decomposition of the Biquaternion Algebra, §The Peirce Decomposition: the four corner lines are the same construction, read relative to the pair of $\tilde Q$'s own root.

The Root, the Idempotents, and the Eigenvalues

Let $$ \tilde Q=Q_te_t+\vec Q,\qquad \vec Q=Q_x\vec e_x+Q_y\vec e_y+Q_z\vec e_z,\qquad Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2\neq0, $$ and fix a square root of $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2$. Define $$ \vec U=\frac{\vec Q}{\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}},\qquad \vec\Xi=-i\vec U=-\frac{i\vec Q}{\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}},\qquad \tilde\Pi_\pm=\frac{e_t\pm\vec\Xi}{2},\qquad \lambda_\pm=Q_t\pm i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}. $$ Then $$ \vec\Xi^2=e_t,\qquad \tilde\Pi_\pm^2=\tilde\Pi_\pm,\qquad \tilde\Pi_+\tilde\Pi_-=\tilde\Pi_-\tilde\Pi_+=0,\qquad \tilde\Pi_++\tilde\Pi_-=e_t, $$ and $$ \tilde Q=\lambda_+\tilde\Pi_++\lambda_-\tilde\Pi_-. $$ The pair $(\tilde\Pi_+,\tilde\Pi_-)$ is attached to $\tilde Q$: it is the pair of the root $\vec\Xi$, not an arbitrary pair of idempotents. The signs are fixed by $$ \vec\Xi\tilde\Pi_\pm=\tilde\Pi_\pm\vec\Xi=\pm\tilde\Pi_\pm,\qquad \tilde Q\tilde\Pi_\pm=\lambda_\pm\tilde\Pi_\pm. $$ Choosing the negative square root sends $\vec\Xi$ to $-\vec\Xi$, exchanges $\tilde\Pi_+$ and $\tilde\Pi_-$, and exchanges $\lambda_+$ and $\lambda_-$; the unordered pair and the unordered spectrum are unchanged.

The Peirce Corners and the Nilpotents

Define the four Peirce corners $$ \mathbb{B}_{st}=\tilde\Pi_s\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_t,\qquad s,t\in\{+,-\}. $$ Each is one complex line. The two diagonal corners are the lines of the idempotents, and the two mixed corners are square-zero: an element $x\in\mathbb{B}_{st}$ with $s\neq t$ has zero scalar part, because its trace is zero, and its square is zero, because $$ x^2\in\tilde\Pi_s\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_t\tilde\Pi_s\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_t=0 \qquad(s\neq t). $$

Construction of the two nilpotents. Choose a nonzero $$ \vec\Upsilon_+\in\mathbb{B}_{-+}=\tilde\Pi_-\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+. $$ Consider the map $$ \mathbb{B}_{+-}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_-,\qquad x\longmapsto\vec\Upsilon_+x. $$ It is linear between complex lines, and it is nonzero. Otherwise $\vec\Upsilon_+x=0$ for every $x\in\mathbb{B}_{+-}$, hence $\vec\Upsilon_+\tilde\Pi_-\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-=0$, and then $\mathbb{B}\vec\Upsilon_+\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-=0$. But $\vec\Upsilon_+\neq0$, so the two-sided ideal $\mathbb{B}\vec\Upsilon_+\mathbb{B}$ is the whole simple algebra $\mathbb{B}$, and $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-\neq0$, a contradiction. A nonzero linear map between complex lines is an isomorphism. Hence there is a unique $$ \vec\Upsilon_-\in\mathbb{B}_{+-} \qquad\text{with}\qquad \vec\Upsilon_+\vec\Upsilon_-=\tilde\Pi_-. $$ The other product is then forced. It lies in $\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_+$, say $\vec\Upsilon_-\vec\Upsilon_+=\alpha\tilde\Pi_+$, and $$ \vec\Upsilon_+=\tilde\Pi_-\vec\Upsilon_+=\vec\Upsilon_+(\vec\Upsilon_-\vec\Upsilon_+)=\alpha\vec\Upsilon_+\tilde\Pi_+=\alpha\vec\Upsilon_+, $$ so $\alpha=1$. Therefore $$ \boxed{\ \vec\Upsilon_+\vec\Upsilon_-=\tilde\Pi_-,\qquad \vec\Upsilon_-\vec\Upsilon_+=\tilde\Pi_+,\qquad \vec\Upsilon_+^2=\vec\Upsilon_-^2=0.\ } $$ The two-line normalisation. If one starts from arbitrary nonzero $u\in\mathbb{B}_{-+}$ and $v\in\mathbb{B}_{+-}$, then $uv=c\tilde\Pi_-$ for some $c\neq0$. Choose $\alpha$ with $\alpha^2=c$ and set $\vec\Upsilon_+=u/\alpha$, $\vec\Upsilon_-=v/\alpha$; the argument above applied to $u/\alpha$ and $v/\alpha$ gives $vu=c\tilde\Pi_+$, and the boxed identities hold. This is the same construction read from two arbitrary generators instead of one, and the residual freedom is the reciprocal rescaling of the pair.

The construction from $\vec\Upsilon_+$ alone makes one choice, the nonzero element $\vec\Upsilon_+$ of the line $\mathbb{B}_{-+}$. That choice determines $\vec\Upsilon_-$ uniquely. In that construction no intermediate scalar and no square root enter. A square root appears when the pair is parametrised by two arbitrary generators, as in the two-line normalisation above, or by a transverse vector, as in the closed forms below.

Uniqueness up to rescaling. The pair is determined by the products and squares only up to $$ \vec\Upsilon_+\longmapsto\mu\,\vec\Upsilon_+,\qquad \vec\Upsilon_-\longmapsto\mu^{-1}\vec\Upsilon_-,\qquad \mu\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}, $$ which preserves both products and both squares. Indeed, $\mu^{-1}\vec\Upsilon_-$ is the unique element of $\mathbb{B}_{+-}$ whose product with $\mu\vec\Upsilon_+$ is $\tilde\Pi_-$, so the construction forces $\vec\Upsilon_-'=\mu^{-1}\vec\Upsilon_-$. Conversely every pair with the same products and squares arises this way, because the mixed corners are lines. The construction therefore defines the pair up to this one rescaling, and no further choice enters.

The corner, without a preferred axis. For every pure biquaternion $\vec X$, $$ \tilde\Pi_-\vec X\tilde\Pi_+=\frac12\left(\vec X_\perp+i\,\vec U\times\vec X\right) =\frac12\left(\vec X-\frac{\vec X\cdot\vec Q}{B^2}\vec Q\right)+\frac{i}{4B}\,[\vec Q,\vec X], $$ $$ \tilde\Pi_+\vec X\tilde\Pi_-=\frac12\left(\vec X_\perp-i\,\vec U\times\vec X\right) =\frac12\left(\vec X-\frac{\vec X\cdot\vec Q}{B^2}\vec Q\right)-\frac{i}{4B}\,[\vec Q,\vec X], $$ where $\vec X_\perp=\vec X-\dfrac{\vec X\cdot\vec Q}{B^2}\vec Q$. No axis is singled out. When $\vec X_\perp^2\neq0$ both projections are nonzero.

The two nilpotents from a transverse vector. Let $\vec X$ be a pure vector with $\vec X_\perp^2\neq0$, and put $$ u_+=\tilde\Pi_-\vec X\tilde\Pi_+,\qquad u_-=\tilde\Pi_+\vec X\tilde\Pi_-. $$ Then $$ u_+u_-=\vec X_\perp^2\,\tilde\Pi_-,\qquad u_-u_+=\vec X_\perp^2\,\tilde\Pi_+,\qquad u_\pm^2=0, $$ as the expansion of the two projections shows: the cross terms cancel because $\vec X_\perp\cdot(\vec U\times\vec X_\perp)=0$. Then $$ \vec\Upsilon_\pm=\frac{u_\pm}{\sqrt{\vec X_\perp^2}} $$ satisfy $\vec\Upsilon_+\vec\Upsilon_-=\tilde\Pi_-$, $\vec\Upsilon_-\vec\Upsilon_+=\tilde\Pi_+$, $\vec\Upsilon_\pm^2=0$. Every admissible pair arises this way, and two choices of $\vec X$ give pairs differing by the reciprocal rescaling. The scalar is $$ \vec X_\perp^2=\vec X^2+\frac{(\vec X\cdot\vec Q)^2}{B^2}. $$

The transverse unit vector. If $\vec V$ is a pure unit vector with $\vec V\cdot\vec U=0$, then $\vec V\tilde\Pi_+\in\mathbb{B}_{-+}$ and $\vec V\tilde\Pi_-\in\mathbb{B}_{+-}$, and, up to the reciprocal rescaling, $$ \vec\Upsilon_+=i\,\vec V\tilde\Pi_+,\qquad \vec\Upsilon_-=i\,\vec V\tilde\Pi_-. $$ For $\vec V=\vec e_x$ and $\vec U=\vec e_z$ one has $\vec V\tilde\Pi_+=(\vec e_x+i\vec e_y)/2$ and $\vec V\tilde\Pi_-=(\vec e_x-i\vec e_y)/2$, and direct multiplication, using $\vec V^2=-e_t$, gives $$ (\vec V\tilde\Pi_+)(\vec V\tilde\Pi_-)=-\tilde\Pi_-, \qquad (\vec V\tilde\Pi_-)(\vec V\tilde\Pi_+)=-\tilde\Pi_+. $$ A general transverse unit is $\vec V=\lambda\vec e_x+\mu\vec e_y$ with $\lambda^2+\mu^2=1$; the factor $\lambda-i\mu$ cancels in the normalised pair, so the same products hold. The factor $i$ on both generators restores the plus signs. This is the transverse-vector construction with $\vec X=\vec V$ and $\sqrt{\vec V_\perp^2}=i$.

The symmetric closed form. The vector $\vec w=\vec e_x+\vec e_y+\vec e_z$ is the only nonzero vector fixed by every permutation of the axes. Put $\sigma=\vec w\cdot\vec Q=Q_x+Q_y+Q_z$. Then $$ \vec w_\perp=\vec w-\frac{\Sigma}{B^2}\vec Q,\qquad \vec w_\perp^2=\vec w^2+\frac{\Sigma^2}{B^2}=-3+\frac{\Sigma^2}{B^2}=\frac{\Sigma^2-3B^2}{B^2}. $$ Therefore $$ \boxed{\ \vec\Upsilon_\pm=\frac{B\,\vec w-\dfrac{\Sigma}{B}\,\vec Q\pm\dfrac{i}{2}\,[\vec Q,\vec w]}{2\sqrt{\Sigma^2-3B^2}}\ } $$ with $\vec w=\vec e_x+\vec e_y+\vec e_z$ and $\Sigma=Q_x+Q_y+Q_z$. In full coordinates, $$ \vec\Upsilon_\pm=\frac{\Bigl(B-\dfrac{\Sigma Q_x}{B}\pm i(Q_y-Q_z)\Bigr)\vec e_x+\Bigl(B-\dfrac{\Sigma Q_y}{B}\pm i(Q_z-Q_x)\Bigr)\vec e_y+\Bigl(B-\dfrac{\Sigma Q_z}{B}\pm i(Q_x-Q_y)\Bigr)\vec e_z}{2\sqrt{\Sigma^2-3B^2}}. $$ A permutation of the axes permutes the three components and leaves $B$ and $\Sigma$ unchanged, so the formula is symmetric in $x,y,z$. Expanding the brackets gives $$ \vec\Upsilon_+\vec\Upsilon_-=\tilde\Pi_-,\qquad \vec\Upsilon_-\vec\Upsilon_+=\tilde\Pi_+,\qquad \vec\Upsilon_\pm^2=0, $$ and the denominator is $$ 2\sqrt{\Sigma^2-3B^2}=2\sqrt{-\bigl((Q_x-Q_y)^2+(Q_y-Q_z)^2+(Q_z-Q_x)^2\bigr)}. $$

The exceptional cone and the chart. The symmetric formula degenerates exactly on the cone $$ \Sigma^2=3B^2, \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad (Q_x-Q_y)^2+(Q_y-Q_z)^2+(Q_z-Q_x)^2=0, $$ which for real $\vec Q$ is the line $Q_x=Q_y=Q_z$. On that cone use the axis chart: for $\mu\in\{x,y,z\}$, $$ \vec\Upsilon_\pm=\frac{B\,\vec e_\mu-\dfrac{Q_\mu}{B}\,\vec Q\pm\dfrac{i}{2}\,[\vec Q,\vec e_\mu]}{2\sqrt{Q_\mu^2-B^2}}, $$ which is the transverse-vector construction with $\vec X=\vec e_\mu$. The chart with axis $\mu$ is defined when $Q_\mu^2\neq B^2$, that is, when $Q_\nu^2+Q_\rho^2\neq0$ for the two other coordinates. At least one of the three is defined whenever $B\neq0$: if all three failed, then $Q_x^2+Q_y^2=Q_y^2+Q_z^2=Q_z^2+Q_x^2=0$, hence $Q_x^2=Q_y^2=Q_z^2$ and $2Q_x^2=0$, so $B=0$. The charts are the one formula read with $\mu=x,y,z$, and the ones that are defined give pairs differing by the reciprocal rescaling. The symmetric formula off the cone and the axis charts on it cover every $\vec Q$ with $B\neq0$.

Why no formula is axis-free everywhere. A closed form with no auxiliary vector would be a map $F$ with $F(R\vec Q)=R\,F(\vec Q)$ for every rotation $R$, rational in $\vec Q$ and $B$. By invariant theory the only such maps are $F(\vec Q)=f(B^2)\vec Q$: the invariant tensors are $\delta_{ij}$ and $\epsilon_{ijk}$, and $\epsilon$ cannot build a vector from a single vector. But the corner is transverse to $\vec Q$, so $F$ would vanish. Every explicit generator therefore uses an auxiliary vector: the symmetric formula uses the permutation-invariant $\vec w$, the axis charts use $\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z$. The corner line is a spinor line, and a spinor is not a function of $\vec Q$ alone.

The standard pair. For the opposite root, $\tilde\Pi_\pm=\tfrac12(e_t\pm i\vec e_z)$, the corner generators of Ideals and Peirce Decomposition of the Biquaternion Algebra, §The Biquaternion Case, are $$ \vec\Upsilon_x=\frac{i\vec e_x-\vec e_y}{2}\in\mathbb{B}_{+-},\qquad \vec\Upsilon_y=\frac{i\vec e_x+\vec e_y}{2}\in\mathbb{B}_{-+}, $$ with $\vec\Upsilon_x\vec\Upsilon_y=\tilde\Pi_+$ and $\vec\Upsilon_y\vec\Upsilon_x=\tilde\Pi_-$. These are the $\vec\Upsilon_1,\vec\Upsilon_2$ of Conventions in Mathematics, §The Nilpotent Convention, indexed by direction. The present root is $\vec\Xi=-i\vec e_z$ when $Q_z>0$ with the choice $B=Q_z$, which is the negative of that one; the two corner labels and the two idempotent labels are therefore exchanged. In the present indexing $$ \vec\Upsilon_+=\vec\Upsilon_x,\qquad \vec\Upsilon_-=\vec\Upsilon_y, $$ and the products are $\vec\Upsilon_+\vec\Upsilon_-=\tilde\Pi_-$, $\vec\Upsilon_-\vec\Upsilon_+=\tilde\Pi_+$, as required.

The Q-Adapted Peirce Basis

The four elements $$ \tilde\Pi_+,\qquad \vec\Upsilon_-,\qquad \vec\Upsilon_+,\qquad \tilde\Pi_- $$ form a $\mathbb{C}$-basis of $\mathbb{B}$. It is the Q-adapted Peirce basis. In this basis the multiplication by $\tilde Q$ is diagonal: $$ \tilde Q\tilde\Pi_+=\lambda_+\tilde\Pi_+,\qquad \tilde Q\vec\Upsilon_-=\lambda_+\vec\Upsilon_-,\qquad \tilde Q\vec\Upsilon_+=\lambda_-\vec\Upsilon_+,\qquad \tilde Q\tilde\Pi_-=\lambda_-\tilde\Pi_-. $$ The diagonal values are $$ \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_+,\lambda_+,\lambda_-,\lambda_-). $$ The reason is the corner rule: an element of $\mathbb{B}_{st}$ has left sign $s$ and right sign $t$ under multiplication by the root $\vec\Xi$, and $\tilde Q=Q_te_t+i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}\,\vec\Xi$ acts on it on the left by $Q_t+i\,s\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}=\lambda_s$ and on the right by $Q_t+i\,t\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}=\lambda_t$. This is the precise sense in which the Peirce coordinates of $\tilde Q$ are the eigenvalues: $Q_{\mathrm{sp},12}=Q_{\mathrm{sp},21}=0$ is the condition that both actions be diagonal, and $\tilde Q$ is the member with $Q_{\mathrm{sp},11}=\lambda_+$ and $Q_{\mathrm{sp},22}=\lambda_-$.

The four elements of the fixed basis. The coordinates of $e_t,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z$ in $\mathcal{B}$ are read from the expansions. In the standard case, $Q_x=Q_y=0$ with the standard root $i\vec e_z$, that is $B=-Q_z$ and $\lambda_\pm=Q_t\mp iQ_z$, the nilpotents are $$ \vec\Upsilon_-=\frac{i\vec e_x-\vec e_y}{2},\qquad \vec\Upsilon_+=\frac{i\vec e_x+\vec e_y}{2}, $$ and $$ e_t=\tilde\Pi_++\tilde\Pi_-,\qquad \vec e_x=-i(\vec\Upsilon_-+\vec\Upsilon_+),\qquad \vec e_y=\vec\Upsilon_+-\vec\Upsilon_-,\qquad \vec e_z=-i\tilde\Pi_++i\tilde\Pi_-. $$ Their coordinates are $$ e_t=(1,0,0,1),\qquad \vec e_x=(0,-i,-i,0),\qquad \vec e_y=(0,-1,1,0),\qquad \vec e_z=(-i,0,0,i), $$ and the left action of the four on the basis is read from the table. In particular $\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z$ satisfy $\vec e_x^2=\vec e_y^2=\vec e_z^2=-e_t$ and $\vec e_x\vec e_y=\vec e_z$, as the fixed basis does, so the same identities hold for their left actions.

A general pure element. Off the axis the coordinates of $\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z$ depend on $\vec Q$. The Eigenbasis of Left Multiplication

A basis of $\mathbb{B}$ is an eigenbasis of $L_{\tilde Q}$ if each of its vectors $b$ satisfies $\tilde Qb=\mu b$ for some scalar $\mu$. The Q-adapted Peirce basis is one; because each eigenspace is two-dimensional, it is not unique.

Write $$ L_{\tilde Q}(\tilde Q_{\mathrm{sp}})=\tilde Q\tilde Q_{\mathrm{sp}},\qquad R_{\tilde Q}(\tilde Q_{\mathrm{sp}})=\tilde Q_{\mathrm{sp}}\tilde Q. $$ An eigenvector of $L_{\tilde Q}$ with eigenvalue $\mu$ is a nonzero element $\tilde Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ with $\tilde Q\tilde Q_{\mathrm{sp}}=\mu\tilde Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$. The diagonal property of the Q-adapted basis reads $$ \tilde Q\tilde\Pi_+=\lambda_+\tilde\Pi_+,\qquad \tilde Q\vec\Upsilon_-=\lambda_+\vec\Upsilon_-,\qquad \tilde Q\vec\Upsilon_+=\lambda_-\vec\Upsilon_+,\qquad \tilde Q\tilde\Pi_-=\lambda_-\tilde\Pi_-, $$ so the basis is an eigenbasis; the left action in it is diagonal with the values $\lambda_+,\lambda_+,\lambda_-,\lambda_-$.

The two eigenspaces. The eigenspaces are two-dimensional: $$ \ker(L_{\tilde Q}-\lambda_+)=\tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_+\oplus\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_-, \qquad \ker(L_{\tilde Q}-\lambda_-)=\tilde\Pi_-\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_+\oplus\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_-. $$ These are the two minimal right ideals. The basis $(\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_-)$ is a basis of the first, $(\vec\Upsilon_+,\tilde\Pi_-)$ a basis of the second.

Uniqueness. An eigenbasis of $L_{\tilde Q}$ is any pair of bases of these two eigenspaces. It is therefore unique only up to an invertible change of basis on each eigenspace. The Q-adapted basis is the choice singled out by the Peirce structure: it orders each eigenspace as $(\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_-)$ and $(\vec\Upsilon_+,\tilde\Pi_-)$, the diagonal idempotent of the Peirce pair first.

The left ideals are eigenspaces of right multiplication. The two minimal left ideals $$ S_+=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_+\oplus\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_+, \qquad S_-=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-=\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_-\oplus\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_- $$ are not eigenspaces of $L_{\tilde Q}$: each contains one eigenvector for $\lambda_+$ and one for $\lambda_-$. They are the eigenspaces of $R_{\tilde Q}$, $$ \ker(R_{\tilde Q}-\lambda_+)=S_+,\qquad \ker(R_{\tilde Q}-\lambda_-)=S_-, $$ and the right action in the same basis is diagonal with the values $\lambda_+,\lambda_-,\lambda_+,\lambda_-$. The reason is the side of the multiplication: $L_{\tilde Q}$ multiplies on the left and preserves each right ideal, $R_{\tilde Q}$ multiplies on the right and preserves each left ideal.

The eigenprojection and the projector. The element $\tilde\Pi_+$ is the eigenprojection of the eigenvalue decomposition. The operator $L_{\tilde\Pi_+}$ is the spectral projector onto the $\lambda_+$-eigenspace of $L_{\tilde Q}$: it sends $\tilde Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$ to $\tilde\Pi_+\tilde Q_{\mathrm{sp}}$, the first Peirce component, and its image is the two-dimensional right ideal $\tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}$. The two objects must not be confused: $L_{\tilde\Pi_+}$ is an operator on the four-dimensional algebra, while the spectral projector of the multiplication map is defined on the algebra itself.

The Spinor Spaces and their Coordinates

A spinor space of $\mathbb{B}$ is a minimal nonzero left ideal. The two spinor spaces are $S_+=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+$ and $S_-=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$, each a complex plane, and the algebra is their direct sum.

The two spaces are $$ S_+=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_+\oplus\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_+, \qquad S_-=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-=\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_-\oplus\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_-, $$ each of complex dimension two, and $$ \mathbb{B}=S_+\oplus S_-. $$ A spinor of first chirality is an element of $S_+$; a spinor of second chirality is an element of $S_-$.

The module structure. Left multiplication makes $S_+$ and $S_-$ left $\mathbb{B}$-modules, and each is a complex plane: a spinor has two complex coordinates. The two are isomorphic as left modules: right multiplication by $\vec\Upsilon_-$ maps $S_+$ onto $S_-$, with inverse right multiplication by $\vec\Upsilon_+$, because $$ \tilde\Pi_+\vec\Upsilon_-=\vec\Upsilon_-,\qquad \vec\Upsilon_-\vec\Upsilon_+=\tilde\Pi_+, \qquad \vec\Upsilon_+\vec\Upsilon_-=\tilde\Pi_-, \qquad \tilde\Pi_-\vec\Upsilon_+=\vec\Upsilon_+. $$ They are nevertheless distinct subspaces of $\mathbb{B}$, and the direct sum is the whole algebra.

In the eigenbasis $(\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_+)$ of $S_+$, a spinor of first chirality is $$ \xi_+=Q_{\mathrm{sp},11}\tilde\Pi_++Q_{\mathrm{sp},21}\vec\Upsilon_+,\qquad Q_{\mathrm{sp},11},Q_{\mathrm{sp},21}\in\mathbb{C}, $$ with the coordinate pair $(Q_{\mathrm{sp},11},Q_{\mathrm{sp},21})$, the first column of the matrix model of §Matrices and the Column Correspondence. Under left multiplication by $\tilde Q$, $$ \tilde Q\xi_+=\lambda_+Q_{\mathrm{sp},11}\tilde\Pi_++\lambda_-Q_{\mathrm{sp},21}\vec\Upsilon_+, $$ so the coordinate pair is sent to $$ (Q_{\mathrm{sp},11},Q_{\mathrm{sp},21})\longmapsto(\lambda_+Q_{\mathrm{sp},11},\;\lambda_-Q_{\mathrm{sp},21}). $$ A spinor of second chirality is an element of $S_-$, $$ \xi_-=Q_{\mathrm{sp},12}\vec\Upsilon_-+Q_{\mathrm{sp},22}\tilde\Pi_-,\qquad Q_{\mathrm{sp},12},Q_{\mathrm{sp},22}\in\mathbb{C}. $$ The order of the coefficients follows the eigenbasis $(\vec\Upsilon_-,\tilde\Pi_-)$ of $S_-$, in which the eigenvalues are $(\lambda_+,\lambda_-)$, so the coordinate pair is $(Q_{\mathrm{sp},12},Q_{\mathrm{sp},22})$, the second column of the matrix model of §Matrices and the Column Correspondence, and $$ \tilde Q\xi_-=\lambda_+Q_{\mathrm{sp},12}\vec\Upsilon_-+\lambda_-Q_{\mathrm{sp},22}\tilde\Pi_- \longmapsto (\lambda_+Q_{\mathrm{sp},12},\;\lambda_-Q_{\mathrm{sp},22}). $$ Left versus right multiplication. The diagonal action displayed above is the action of $L_{\tilde Q}$ on each spinor space, and it carries both eigenvalues. The space $S_+$ is not an eigenspace of $L_{\tilde Q}$; it is the $\lambda_+$-eigenspace of the right multiplication $R_{\tilde Q}$, and $S_-$ is the $\lambda_-$-eigenspace. The distinction is the side on which $\tilde Q$ multiplies: $R_{\tilde Q}$ is scalar on each $S_\pm$, while $L_{\tilde Q}$ carries the two eigenvalues on each. This is the content of §The Eigenbasis of Left Multiplication.

The basis $(\vec\Upsilon_-,\tilde\Pi_-)$ is part of the definition: writing the two vectors in the other order would exchange the two diagonal values.

Matrices and the Column Correspondence

Fix the assignment $$ \tilde\Pi_+\mapsto E_{11},\qquad \vec\Upsilon_-\mapsto E_{12},\qquad \vec\Upsilon_+\mapsto E_{21},\qquad \tilde\Pi_-\mapsto E_{22}, $$ where $E_{ij}$ is the matrix unit with $1$ in row $i$ and column $j$. Then every $P\in\mathbb{B}$ has a unique expansion $$ P=a_{11}\tilde\Pi_++a_{12}\vec\Upsilon_-+a_{21}\vec\Upsilon_++a_{22}\tilde\Pi_-, \qquad a_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}, $$ and is represented by $$ M_2^Q(P)=\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\a_{21}&a_{22}\end{pmatrix}. $$ In the notation of the spinor coordinates, $a_{11}=Q_{\mathrm{sp},11}$, $a_{12}=Q_{\mathrm{sp},12}$, $a_{21}=Q_{\mathrm{sp},21}$ and $a_{22}=Q_{\mathrm{sp},22}$. The element $\tilde Q$ itself has $a_{11}=\lambda_+$, $a_{22}=\lambda_-$ and $a_{12}=a_{21}=0$, so $$ M_2^Q(\tilde Q)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_+&0\\0&\lambda_-\end{pmatrix}. $$

The two columns are the two spinor spaces: $$ S_+=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+\longleftrightarrow\operatorname{span}\{E_{11},E_{21}\}=\text{column }1, \qquad S_-=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-\longleftrightarrow\operatorname{span}\{E_{12},E_{22}\}=\text{column }2. $$ The first column of $M_2^Q(P)$ is the coordinate vector of $P\tilde\Pi_+$, the second is the coordinate vector of $P\tilde\Pi_-$.

A spinor of first chirality $$ \xi_+=\alpha\tilde\Pi_++\beta\vec\Upsilon_+ \longleftrightarrow \begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix} $$ is represented by the matrix $$ \begin{pmatrix}\alpha&0\\\beta&0\end{pmatrix}, $$ whose first column is $\xi_+$ and whose second column is zero. A spinor of second chirality $$ \xi_-=\gamma\vec\Upsilon_-+\delta\tilde\Pi_- \longleftrightarrow \begin{pmatrix}\gamma\\\delta\end{pmatrix} $$ is represented by the matrix $$ \begin{pmatrix}0&\gamma\\0&\delta\end{pmatrix}, $$ whose first column is zero and whose second column is $\xi_-$. The order $(\vec\Upsilon_-,\tilde\Pi_-)$ is the eigenbasis of $S_-$ used above; it is the order in which the diagonal values are $(\lambda_+,\lambda_-)$.

Multiplication by $\tilde Q$ acts on the first column by $M_2^Q(\tilde Q)$, $$ \tilde Q\xi_+\longleftrightarrow \begin{pmatrix}\lambda_+&0\\0&\lambda_-\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\lambda_+\alpha\\\lambda_-\beta\end{pmatrix}, $$ and on the second column by the same matrix, $$ \tilde Q\xi_-\longleftrightarrow \begin{pmatrix}\lambda_+&0\\0&\lambda_-\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\gamma\\\delta\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\lambda_+\gamma\\\lambda_-\delta\end{pmatrix}. $$ This is the precise statement that the two spinor spaces are the two columns and that multiplication by $\tilde Q$ acts on both by the same diagonal matrix. A general element of $\mathbb{B}$ is not a spinor: its matrix has two nonzero columns, one in each spinor space.

The Degenerate Case $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2=0$

The construction uses $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2\neq0$. When $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2=0$ and $\vec Q\neq0$: - the root $\vec\Xi=-i\vec Q/\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}$ is undefined; - no Peirce pair is attached to $\tilde Q$; - there is no Q-adapted Peirce basis; - the nilpotents $\vec\Upsilon_\pm$ adapted to $\tilde Q$ do not exist; - there is no eigenbasis of $L_{\tilde Q}$.

The standard Peirce decomposition of the root $i\vec e_z$ still exists, with its two nilpotents, but it is not attached to $\tilde Q$. In any Peirce basis the Peirce coordinates of $\tilde Q$ have trace $2Q_t$ and determinant $Q_t^2$, because the characteristic polynomial is $(X-Q_t)^2$. They are not diagonalisable, since the minimal polynomial is $(X-Q_t)^2$ when $\vec Q\neq0$. In the Jordan regime $Q_t\neq0$ the Peirce matrix of $\tilde Q$ is a single $2\times2$ Jordan block with eigenvalue $Q_t$, so $L_{\tilde Q}$ is not diagonalisable; in the nilpotent regime $Q_t=0$ it is a nonzero nilpotent. The nilpotent construction of the standard pair remains available, but it is not the construction of the present section: the present construction takes the nilpotents from the mixed corners of the pair of $\tilde Q$'s own root, and that root does not exist when $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2=0$.

For $\vec Q=0$ the element is scalar, $\tilde Q=Q_te_t$, and $L_{\tilde Q}=Q_t\,\mathrm{id}$ on the whole algebra: every basis is an eigenbasis, the Q-adapted construction is absent, and there is no Jordan or nilpotent regime.

Summary Block

Given $\tilde Q$ with $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2\neq0$, the following data are attached to $\tilde Q$: $$ \vec\Xi=-\frac{i\vec Q}{\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}},\qquad \tilde\Pi_\pm=\frac{e_t\pm\vec\Xi}{2},\qquad \lambda_\pm=Q_t\pm i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}, $$ $$ \vec\Upsilon_+\in\mathbb{B}_{-+},\qquad \vec\Upsilon_-\in\mathbb{B}_{+-},\qquad \vec\Upsilon_+\vec\Upsilon_-=\tilde\Pi_-,\qquad \vec\Upsilon_-\vec\Upsilon_+=\tilde\Pi_+,\qquad \vec\Upsilon_\pm^2=0, $$ with the symmetric closed form $$ \vec\Upsilon_\pm=\frac{B\,\vec w-\dfrac{\Sigma}{B}\,\vec Q\pm\dfrac{i}{2}\,[\vec Q,\vec w]}{2\sqrt{\Sigma^2-3B^2}}, \qquad \vec w=\vec e_x+\vec e_y+\vec e_z,\quad \Sigma=Q_x+Q_y+Q_z, $$ valid off the cone $\Sigma^2=3B^2$; on that cone the axis charts $$ \vec\Upsilon_\pm=\frac{B\,\vec e_\mu-\dfrac{Q_\mu}{B}\,\vec Q\pm\dfrac{i}{2}\,[\vec Q,\vec e_\mu]}{2\sqrt{Q_\mu^2-B^2}}, \qquad \mu=x,y,z, $$ cover the pair, at least one of the three being defined, and the defined readings differ by the reciprocal rescaling. $$ \mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_+\oplus\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_-\oplus\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_+\oplus\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_-, $$ $$ S_+=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_+\oplus\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_+,\qquad S_-=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-=\mathbb{C}\vec\Upsilon_-\oplus\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_-, $$ and every $\tilde Q_{\mathrm{sp}}\in\mathbb{B}$ has the unique expansion $$ \tilde Q_{\mathrm{sp}}=Q_{\mathrm{sp},11}\tilde\Pi_++Q_{\mathrm{sp},12}\vec\Upsilon_-+Q_{\mathrm{sp},21}\vec\Upsilon_++Q_{\mathrm{sp},22}\tilde\Pi_-, \qquad Q_{\mathrm{sp},ij}\in\mathbb{C}, $$ with $\tilde Q$ itself having $Q_{\mathrm{sp},11}=\lambda_+$, $Q_{\mathrm{sp},22}=\lambda_-$ and $Q_{\mathrm{sp},12}=Q_{\mathrm{sp},21}=0$.

Diagonal property. $$ \tilde Q\tilde\Pi_+=\lambda_+\tilde\Pi_+,\qquad \tilde Q\vec\Upsilon_-=\lambda_+\vec\Upsilon_-,\qquad \tilde Q\vec\Upsilon_+=\lambda_-\vec\Upsilon_+,\qquad \tilde Q\tilde\Pi_-=\lambda_-\tilde\Pi_-. $$ The Q-adapted Peirce basis is an eigenbasis of $L_{\tilde Q}$ with eigenvalues $$ (\lambda_+,\lambda_+,\lambda_-,\lambda_-). $$ The $\lambda_+$-eigenspace of $L_{\tilde Q}$ is the right ideal $\tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}$, with basis $(\tilde\Pi_+,\vec\Upsilon_-)$; the $\lambda_-$-eigenspace is $\tilde\Pi_-\mathbb{B}$, with basis $(\vec\Upsilon_+,\tilde\Pi_-)$. The two left ideals $S_+,S_-$ are the eigenspaces of $R_{\tilde Q}$, with eigenvalues $\lambda_+,\lambda_-$ respectively. On each left ideal the restriction of $L_{\tilde Q}$ has the distinct eigenvalues $(\lambda_+,\lambda_-)$, so the eigenbasis of that restriction is unique up to scale.

Spinors. A spinor of first chirality is an element of $S_+$, $$ \xi_+=Q_{\mathrm{sp},11}\tilde\Pi_++Q_{\mathrm{sp},21}\vec\Upsilon_+, \qquad (Q_{\mathrm{sp},11},Q_{\mathrm{sp},21})\in\mathbb{C}^2, $$ and under left multiplication $$ \tilde Q\xi_+=\lambda_+Q_{\mathrm{sp},11}\tilde\Pi_++\lambda_-Q_{\mathrm{sp},21}\vec\Upsilon_+, \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad (Q_{\mathrm{sp},11},Q_{\mathrm{sp},21})\longmapsto(\lambda_+Q_{\mathrm{sp},11},\;\lambda_-Q_{\mathrm{sp},21}). $$ A spinor of second chirality is an element of $S_-$, $$ \xi_-=Q_{\mathrm{sp},12}\vec\Upsilon_-+Q_{\mathrm{sp},22}\tilde\Pi_-, \qquad (Q_{\mathrm{sp},12},Q_{\mathrm{sp},22})\in\mathbb{C}^2, $$ and under left multiplication $$ \tilde Q\xi_-=\lambda_+Q_{\mathrm{sp},12}\vec\Upsilon_-+\lambda_-Q_{\mathrm{sp},22}\tilde\Pi_-, \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad (Q_{\mathrm{sp},12},Q_{\mathrm{sp},22})\longmapsto(\lambda_+Q_{\mathrm{sp},12},\;\lambda_-Q_{\mathrm{sp},22}). $$ The two spinor spaces are the two minimal left ideals; under left multiplication each is a complex plane carrying the two eigenvalues $\lambda_+,\lambda_-$.

Matrices. With $$ \tilde\Pi_+\mapsto E_{11},\qquad \vec\Upsilon_-\mapsto E_{12},\qquad \vec\Upsilon_+\mapsto E_{21},\qquad \tilde\Pi_-\mapsto E_{22}, $$ a general element has the matrix $$ M_2^Q(P)=\begin{pmatrix}Q_{\mathrm{sp},11}&Q_{\mathrm{sp},12}\\Q_{\mathrm{sp},21}&Q_{\mathrm{sp},22}\end{pmatrix}, \qquad M_2^Q(\tilde Q)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_+&0\\0&\lambda_-\end{pmatrix}. $$ The first column is $S_+$, the second is $S_-$, and multiplication by $\tilde Q$ acts on both by $\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_+,\lambda_-)$.

Degenerate case. $$ Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2=0:\quad \text{no Q-adapted Peirce basis and no Q-adapted nilpotents.} $$ For $\vec Q\neq0$ there is no eigenbasis of $L_{\tilde Q}$: in any Peirce basis the Peirce matrix of $\tilde Q$ is a single $2\times2$ Jordan block when $Q_t\neq0$ and a nonzero nilpotent when $Q_t=0$, so it is not diagonal. For $\vec Q=0$ the element is scalar, $L_{\tilde Q}=Q_t\,\mathrm{id}$, and every basis is an eigenbasis; the Q-adapted construction is then absent, but an eigenbasis is not.

Powers, Polynomial Calculus, and the Group of Units

The eigenvalue decomposition carries the polynomial calculus and the inverse, and it describes the eigenvalue map of the unit group.

By Sylvester's formula of The Lagrange–Sylvester Eigenprojector Formula, §Sylvester's Formula, every polynomial $f\in\mathbb{C}[X]$ acts as $$ f(\tilde Q)=f(\lambda_+)\tilde\Pi_++f(\lambda_-)\tilde\Pi_-. $$ In particular $$ \tilde Q^n=\lambda_+^n\tilde\Pi_++\lambda_-^n\tilde\Pi_-,\qquad n\geq0, $$ with $\tilde\Pi_++\tilde\Pi_-=e_t$ for $n=0$. In this regime, when $\tilde Q$ is a unit, the two eigenvalues are nonzero, since $\lambda_+\lambda_-=\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}\neq0$ (Introduction to the Biquaternion Algebra, §The Reduced Norm and Invertibility), and the formula extends to negative powers: $$ \tilde Q^{-1}=\lambda_+^{-1}\tilde\Pi_++\lambda_-^{-1}\tilde\Pi_-. $$ The result agrees with the inverse formula $\tilde Q^{-1}=\tilde Q^{\natural}/\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}$ of that article, since $\lambda_+\lambda_-=\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}$ and $\lambda_-\tilde\Pi_++\lambda_+\tilde\Pi_-=Q_te_t-\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}\,\vec U=\tilde Q^{\natural}$.

The group of units. The eigenvalue map $$ \mathbb{B}^{\times}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}, \qquad \tilde Q\longmapsto(\lambda_+,\lambda_-) $$ is surjective, because $\lambda_+\lambda_-=\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}$ and every pair of nonzero complex numbers occurs. It is not injective: two units with the same eigenvalues differ by an element of the mixed Peirce part $\mathbb{B}_{+-}\oplus\mathbb{B}_{-+}$. It is not a group homomorphism either, because the eigenvalues of a product are not determined by the eigenvalues of the factors. The polynomial calculus is a statement about the diagonal part $\mathbb{C}[\tilde Q]$, where the eigenvalues are coordinates; it does not extend as a coordinatewise calculus to the whole unit group.

The Algebraic Fibre of the Decomposition

The eigenvalue coordinates and the basis coordinates are two linear coordinate systems on the same diagonal part.

Fix the ordered pair $(\tilde\Pi_+,\tilde\Pi_-)$, equivalently the root $\vec\Xi=\tilde\Pi_+-\tilde\Pi_-$. The fibre of the map that sends an element with distinct eigenvalues to its ordered pair of eigenprojections is the set of elements $\mu_+\tilde\Pi_++\mu_-\tilde\Pi_-$ with $\mu_+\neq\mu_-$. Every element of the plane $\mathbb{C}[\tilde Q]$ is $$ \tilde Q_4=\mu_+\tilde\Pi_++\mu_-\tilde\Pi_-,\qquad \mu_\pm\in\mathbb{C}, $$ and the map $(\mu_+,\mu_-)\mapsto\tilde Q_4$ is a $\mathbb{C}$-linear isomorphism $\mathbb{C}^2\to\mathbb{C}[\tilde Q]$.

For $\mu_+\neq\mu_-$ this is the eigenvalue decomposition of $\tilde Q_4$, with the eigenprojections $\tilde\Pi_\pm$ and the eigenvalue data $(\mu_+,\mu_-)$. For $\mu_+=\mu_-=a$ the element is $\tilde Q_4=ae_t$, scalar; the writing is still valid but every complete orthogonal pair gives it, so the two-projector pair is not determined by $\tilde Q_4$, although the eigenprojection $e_t$ of the one-term decomposition is. The eigenvalue decomposition is then the one-term writing $\tilde Q_4=ae_t$ of §The Scalar Regime.

For $\mu_+\neq\mu_-$ the unordered pair $\{\mu_+,\mu_-\}$ is the spectrum, and with the pair of idempotents fixed the coordinate map to the unordered spectrum is two-to-one: the two preimages of $\{\mu_+,\mu_-\}$ are $\mu_+\tilde\Pi_++\mu_-\tilde\Pi_-$ and $\mu_-\tilde\Pi_++\mu_+\tilde\Pi_-$, which differ by $(\mu_+-\mu_-)(\tilde\Pi_+-\tilde\Pi_-)\neq0$. For eigenvalues constrained to subsets $S_\pm\subseteq\mathbb{C}$, the corresponding elements are $$ \{\mu_+\tilde\Pi_++\mu_-\tilde\Pi_-:\ \mu_+\in S_+,\ \mu_-\in S_-\}. $$

The Lattice of Eigenvalue Data

The eigenvalue coordinates in $2\pi i\mathbb{Z}^2$ give $$ L=\bigl\{2\pi i k_+\tilde\Pi_++2\pi i k_-\tilde\Pi_-:\ k_\pm\in\mathbb{Z}\bigr\}. $$ Using $\tilde\Pi_\pm=\tfrac12(e_t\pm\vec\Xi)$, $$ 2\pi i k_+\tilde\Pi_++2\pi i k_-\tilde\Pi_-=\pi i(k_++k_-)e_t+\pi i(k_+-k_-)\vec\Xi. $$ With $m=k_++k_-$ and $n=k_+-k_-$ one has $m-n=2k_-$, so $m\equiv n\pmod2$; conversely every pair $(m,n)$ of the same parity comes from the integers $k_\pm=(m\pm n)/2$. Hence $$ L=\bigl\{\pi i m\,e_t+\pi i n\,\vec\Xi:\ m,n\in\mathbb{Z},\ m\equiv n\pmod2\bigr\} =\bigl\{\pi i m\,e_t+\pi n\,\vec U:\ m,n\in\mathbb{Z},\ m\equiv n\pmod2\bigr\}. $$ This is the parity-matched lattice. In this regime it is the kernel of the exponential on the diagonal part, and Biquaternion Elementary Functions, §The Eigenvalue Formula, uses it for the logarithm. It is not the rectangular lattice $2\pi i\mathbb{Z}e_t\oplus2\pi\mathbb{Z}\vec U$: the rectangular lattice is the sublattice of index two in $L$ on which $m$ and $n$ are both even, the other coset is $\pi i e_t+\pi\vec U$, and the scalar and the vector coordinate cannot be chosen independently, because the idempotents $\tilde\Pi_\pm$ mix the two directions.

The Hermitian Case

The eigenprojections are Hermitian exactly when $\vec U$ is real, and the decomposition is then the spectral theorem.

Two further notions enter. Hermitian conjugation is the star, and normality is the condition $\tilde Q\tilde Q^{*}=\tilde Q^{*}\tilde Q$, as in Biquaternion Spectral Theory, §Hermitian and Normal Elements (§The Conjugations of Conventions in Mathematics). The proposition below identifies the case in which the pair is Hermitian.

Proposition. Let $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2\neq0$. The following are equivalent: (i) $\tilde\Pi_\pm$ are Hermitian; (ii) $\vec U$ is real; (iii) $\vec\Xi$ is purely imaginary; (iv) $\tilde Q$ is normal.

Proof. Since $\vec\Xi=-i\vec U$ and $\vec\Xi^{*}=-\overline{\vec\Xi}$, $$ \tilde\Pi_\pm^{*}=\frac12\bigl(e_t\pm\vec\Xi^{*}\bigr)=\frac12\bigl(e_t\mp i\overline{\vec U}\bigr), $$ so (i) holds exactly when $\overline{\vec U}=\vec U$, which is (ii); and (ii) and (iii) are the same condition, since $\vec U=i\vec\Xi$. If (ii) holds, then $\tilde Q^{*}=\overline{Q_t}e_t-\overline{\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}}\,\vec U$ lies in $\mathbb{C}[\tilde Q]$, so $\tilde Q$ commutes with $\tilde Q^{*}$ and (iv) holds. Conversely, if (iv) holds, Theorem 11.2 of Biquaternion Spectral Theory, §The Spectral Theorem, writes $\tilde Q=\lambda_1\tilde\Pi_1+\lambda_2\tilde\Pi_2$ with Hermitian idempotents; since $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2\neq0$ the eigenvalues are distinct, and the formulas of that theorem identify $\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Pi_2$ with $\tilde\Pi_\pm$, so (i) holds.

The pair is then the Hermitian pair of the root $\vec\Xi=-i\vec u$ over a real unit vector $\vec u$ (§The Families of Idempotents of Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the Biquaternion Algebra), and the decomposition is the spectral theorem of Biquaternion Spectral Theory, §The Spectral Theorem, read in the biquaternion algebra. When in addition $\tilde Q$ is Hermitian, $Q_t\in\mathbb{R}$ and $\vec Q=i\vec q'$ with $\vec q'$ real, and the square root of $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2$ is $i|\vec q'|$, so that $\lambda_\pm=Q_t\mp|\vec q'|$ are real.

Degenerate Regimes

The eigenvalue decomposition exists exactly when the minimal polynomial is split and squarefree (The Eigenvalue Decomposition of an Element, §Existence and Uniqueness). The spectrum separates the cases as follows. For $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2\neq0$ the minimal polynomial is $p_{\tilde Q}=(X-Q_t)^2+Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2$, which is squarefree: the element is semisimple, with distinct eigenvalues, and the decomposition exists. When in addition $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=0$, one eigenvalue vanishes: this is the zero-divisor regime. For $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2=0$ the characteristic polynomial is $(X-Q_t)^2$; the minimal polynomial is $(X-Q_t)^2$ when $\vec Q\neq0$, and $X-Q_t$ when $\vec Q=0$. The first is the nilpotent regime, named for the vector part, with no eigenvalue decomposition; the element is a unit in the Jordan subcase $Q_t\neq0$ and a pure nilpotent in the subcase $Q_t=0$. The second is the scalar regime, with the one-term decomposition.

The Scalar Regime

A scalar element has a repeated eigenvalue, the one-term eigenprojection $e_t$, and a non-unique two-projector refinement.

Let $\vec Q=0$, so $\tilde Q=Q_te_t$. The element is central and semisimple, with the single eigenvalue $Q_t$ and minimal polynomial $X-Q_t$; its centraliser is all of $\mathbb{B}$.

In the sense of the general definition the eigenvalue decomposition has one term, $\tilde Q=Q_te_t$, with the single eigenprojection $e_t$, and it is unique. A decomposition into two primitive idempotents with the repeated coefficient $Q_t$ is a different object: every complete orthogonal pair $\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Pi_2$ gives $$ \tilde Q=Q_t\tilde\Pi_1+Q_t\tilde\Pi_2, $$ and the pair is not determined by $\tilde Q$. The eigenvalue data therefore determine nothing beyond the scalar, and the two-projector writing is non-unique.

The Zero-Divisor Regime

A zero divisor with distinct eigenvalues has one eigenvalue equal to zero and still admits an eigenvalue decomposition.

Suppose $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2\neq0$ and $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=Q_t^2+Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2=0$. Then $Q_t=\pm i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}$ and $Q_t\neq0$, and the eigenvalues are $$ \lambda_\sigma=Q_t+\sigma i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}=2Q_t,\qquad \lambda_{-\sigma}=Q_t-\sigma i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}=0, $$ where $\sigma\in\{+,-\}$ is the sign for which $Q_t=\sigma i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}$. They are distinct because $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2\neq0$. The minimal polynomial is $$ p_{\tilde Q}(X)=X^2-2Q_tX=X(X-2Q_t), $$ which is squarefree, so $\tilde Q$ is semisimple. The eigenvalue decomposition is $$ \tilde Q=2Q_t\tilde\Pi_\sigma+0\cdot\tilde\Pi_{-\sigma}, $$ and $\tilde Q=2Q_t\tilde\Pi_\sigma$ is a nonzero scalar multiple of the idempotent $\tilde\Pi_\sigma$. The element is a zero divisor, since $\tilde Q\tilde\Pi_{-\sigma}=0$ with $\tilde\Pi_{-\sigma}\neq0$, and it is not a unit; the decomposition survives because one eigenvalue vanishes, exactly as in the split-complex and reduced biquaternion cases. The nilpotent regime of §The Nilpotent Regime is different: there the minimal polynomial has a repeated root.

The Nilpotent Regime

A nonzero nilpotent vector part makes the element non-semisimple, and no eigenvalue decomposition exists. The regime is named for the vector part: the element itself is nilpotent exactly when $Q_t=0$, in which case it is a nonzero zero divisor.

Let $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2=0$ and $\vec Q\neq0$. Then $\vec Q^2=0$, so $\vec\Upsilon=\vec Q$ is a pure nilpotent, written with the arrow and without the tilde by §The Nilpotent Convention of Conventions in Mathematics, and $$ \tilde Q=Q_te_t+\vec\Upsilon,\qquad \vec\Upsilon^2=0. $$ The element is a unit exactly when $Q_t\neq0$, since $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=Q_t^2$ (Introduction to the Biquaternion Algebra, §The Reduced Norm and Invertibility), and its minimal polynomial is $$ (X-Q_t)^2, $$ which is not squarefree. By the theorem of The Eigenvalue Decomposition of an Element, §Existence and Uniqueness, $\tilde Q$ has no eigenvalue decomposition. The Peirce matrix of $\tilde Q$ is a single $2\times2$ Jordan block with eigenvalue $Q_t$.

The centraliser of $\tilde Q$ is $$ \{ae_t+b\vec\Upsilon:\ a,b\in\mathbb{C}\}. $$ Its only idempotents are $0$ and $e_t$: if $(ae_t+b\vec\Upsilon)^2=ae_t+b\vec\Upsilon$, then $a^2=a$ and $2ab=b$, so $(a,b)=(0,0)$ or $(1,0)$. Hence no pair of nonzero orthogonal idempotents summing to $e_t$ lies in it, and there is no Peirce decomposition adapted to $\tilde Q$ into two primitive idempotents.

Remarks

  1. Relation to the Lagrange–Sylvester formula. The formulas for $\tilde\Pi_\pm$ are the Lagrange–Sylvester formulas applied to the quadratic $p_{\tilde Q}$: they are the eigenprojections of the multiplication $L_{\tilde Q}$ on the commutative plane $\mathbb{C}[\tilde Q]$. The biquaternion-specific content is the identification of the eigenprojections with the idempotents of a pure root of $+1$, and of the eigenvalue data with the scalar part and the complex angle along $\vec U$.

  2. Centrality and the Peirce decomposition. The idempotents $\tilde\Pi_\pm$ are noncentral: they commute with $\tilde Q$ and with its centraliser, but not with all of $\mathbb{B}$. There is no nontrivial central idempotent in $\mathbb{B}$, since the algebra is simple (§The Two-Sided Ideals of Ideals and Peirce Decomposition of the Biquaternion Algebra). The decomposition is therefore a Peirce decomposition relative to an element-dependent idempotent, not a decomposition of the algebra into ideals.

  3. Uniqueness and labelling. The decomposition is canonical only as the unordered set of pairs $\{(\lambda_+,\tilde\Pi_+),(\lambda_-,\tilde\Pi_-)\}$. The labelling is interchanged by replacing the square root by its negative and sending $\vec\Xi$ to $-\vec\Xi$; it is fixed by the convention of §The Eigenvalue Decomposition exactly when the square root can be taken real and positive.

  4. Degenerate regimes. The eigenvalue decomposition is a property of semisimple elements; the number of its terms is the number of distinct eigenvalues. The zero-divisor regime is semisimple with one eigenvalue zero and keeps a two-term decomposition. The nilpotent regime is non-semisimple and admits none. The scalar regime is semisimple with a repeated eigenvalue: the general decomposition has one term, and any two-projector refinement is non-unique.

  5. The commutative cases. The split-complex and reduced biquaternion decompositions are the two commutative shadows of the present one: their eigenprojections are central and fixed, the Peirce corners reduce to the two diagonal lines, and the unit group is a direct product. The biquaternion-specific phenomena are the noncentrality of $\tilde\Pi_\pm$, the two square-zero mixed corners, and the failure of the eigenvalue map to be injective or a homomorphism.

Summary

For a biquaternion with $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2\neq0$, the eigenvalue decomposition is the writing $$ \tilde Q=\lambda_+\tilde\Pi_++\lambda_-\tilde\Pi_-, \qquad \lambda_\pm=Q_t\pm i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}, \qquad \tilde\Pi_\pm=\frac12(e_t\pm\vec\Xi). $$ Here the radical is $\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}$, equal to $|\vec Q|$ in the real case, and $\vec\Xi=-i\vec Q/\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}$ is the pure root of $+1$ of the vector part. The eigenvalues are distinct, the idempotents are primitive, orthogonal and complete, and they are the eigenprojections. The decomposition is unique up to the exchange of the two labels.
