Square Roots of a General Element in the Antisymmetric Plain Sesqualgebra

Introduction

This article solves the square-root problem in the antisymmetric plain sesqualgebra: given $\tilde Q\in\mathbb{B}$, find every $\tilde P\in\mathbb{B}$ with

$$ \tilde P\diamond\tilde P=\tilde Q, $$

for the product $\tilde P\diamond\tilde Q=\operatorname{Vect}(\tilde P\tilde Q^{*})$ of Introduction to the Antisymmetric Plain Sesqualgebra of Biquaternions.

The method is the scalar–vector split, carried by the real structure of the coefficients. The split writes each element as a real quaternion plus $i$ times a real quaternion, and the diagonal of the block then closes in a single expression in the eight real data of the root; the expression exhibits the diagonal as $2i$ times a real vector, and that is the whole classification. Every value of the diagonal is of that form, and the square-root equation is solvable exactly for the radicands of that form: the block has no root of a radicand outside the Hermitian pure vectors, and for a radicand inside them the roots form a real five-parameter family. The squaring map is thus a map whose image is a real three-dimensional space and each of whose fibres is a real five-dimensional family, and the discrepancy is the non-associativity and the non-injectivity of the projection.

The article is algebraic. No norm enters and no form is needed beyond the description of the image. The three central values $\tilde P\diamond\tilde P=-e_0,0,+e_0$ are the degenerate data of the classification and are Square Roots of Minus One, Zero and Plus One in the Antisymmetric Plain Sesqualgebra; the criterion for the diagonal to vanish and the quadric it traces are The Vector Part of the Square and the Jacobi Failure of the Antisymmetric Plain Sesqualgebra; the square-zero class is Nilpotents of the Antisymmetric Plain Sesqualgebra. The general definition of the square root is Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures, §Powers, the Square and the Roots; the model of the method is Square Roots of a General Element in the General Plain Algebra; and the two models of the block, which read the same classification through the matrix and the regular representation, are The Antisymmetric Plain Sesqualgebra in the $2\times2$ Matrix Element Representation $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ and The Antisymmetric Plain Sesqualgebra in the $4\times4$ Matrix Element Representation $M_4(\mathbb{C})_L$.

Throughout, the square is the square of $\diamond$ and the root is the root in $\diamond$, unqualified. $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$ has basis $e_0=1,e_1,e_2,e_3$ with $e_1e_2=e_3$, central scalar imaginary $i$ and coefficients in $\mathbb{C}$; the projection $\operatorname{Vect}$ removes the scalar part, its kernel is the centre $\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{B}}$, the Hermitian subspace is $\mathbb{M}_+$ and the vector subspace $\operatorname{Vect}(\mathbb{B})$. The conjugations are ${}^{\natural}$, the coefficientwise $\bar{\cdot}$ and the star ${}^{*}=\bar{\cdot}\circ{}^{\natural}$.

The Reduction

The Real Form of an Element

Every coefficient $P_\mu$ of a biquaternion is complex, and the element is written as a real quaternion plus $i$ times a real quaternion,

$$ \tilde P=\tilde p+i\tilde a,\qquad \tilde p=p_0e_0+\mathbf p,\quad \tilde a=a_0e_0+\mathbf a,\qquad p_0,a_0\in\mathbb{R},\quad \mathbf p,\mathbf a\in\mathbb{R}^{3}, $$

where $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ reads the three real coefficients of the vector part. The scalar part of $\tilde P$ is the complex number $P_0=p_0+ia_0$ and the vector part is the complex vector $\mathbf{P}=\mathbf p+i\mathbf a$; the splitting is a real-linear isomorphism $\mathbb{B}\cong\mathbb{R}^{4}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{4}$ and it carries no choice beyond the central imaginary.

The Diagonal in Coordinates

Proposition (the diagonal). For every $\tilde P=\tilde p+i\tilde a$,

$$ \tilde P\diamond\tilde P=2i\,\bigl(p_0\mathbf a-a_0\mathbf p-\mathbf a\times\mathbf p\bigr), $$

so the diagonal is $2i$ times a real vector.

Proof. Expand the parent product in the real form, $\tilde P\tilde P^{*}=\tilde p\tilde p^{\natural}+\tilde a\tilde a^{\natural}+i(\tilde a\tilde p^{\natural}-\tilde p\tilde a^{\natural})$. The first two terms are real scalars, so they contribute nothing to the vector part; the vector part of $\tilde a\tilde p^{\natural}$ is $-a_0\mathbf p+p_0\mathbf a-\mathbf a\times\mathbf p$, and the vector part of $\tilde p\tilde a^{\natural}$ is $-p_0\mathbf a+a_0\mathbf p+\mathbf a\times\mathbf p$, so their difference is $2(p_0\mathbf a-a_0\mathbf p-\mathbf a\times\mathbf p)$. Multiplying by $i$ and applying $\operatorname{Vect}$ gives the display.

The expression is the reason for everything that follows. It says three things at once: the diagonal is real-vector-valued, $2i$ times a vector of $\mathbb{R}^{3}$; it depends on the eight real data of the root through only three real components, so it cannot be injective; and it is the map of the real eight-dimensional space of roots onto the real three-dimensional target.

The Existence

The Necessary Condition

Theorem (the image of the diagonal). The diagonal of the block takes exactly the values

$$ \tilde P\diamond\tilde P\in i\mathbb{R}^{3}=\mathbb{M}_+\cap\operatorname{Vect}(\mathbb{B}), $$

the Hermitian pure vectors, and every such value is attained. Equivalently, the equation $\tilde P\diamond\tilde P=\tilde Q$ has no solution unless $\tilde Q$ is a pure vector with purely imaginary coefficients,

$$ Q_0=0,\qquad \overline{Q_k}=-Q_k\quad(k=1,2,3), $$

and for every such $\tilde Q$ it has a solution.

Proof. The diagonal is $2i\mathbf h$ with $\mathbf h\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$, so it is a pure vector with purely imaginary coefficients; conversely a pure vector with purely imaginary coefficients is $i\mathbf g$ with $\mathbf g\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$, and $\tilde P=e_0+\tfrac12\tilde Q$ has $p_0=1$, $a_0=0$, $\mathbf p=0$, $\mathbf a=\tfrac12\mathbf g$, so its diagonal is $i\mathbf g=\tilde Q$; the identification with $\mathbb{M}_+\cap\operatorname{Vect}(\mathbb{B})$ is the statement that a pure vector is Hermitian exactly when its coefficients are purely imaginary.

Corollary (the rootless radicands). The elements outside $i\mathbb{R}^{3}$ have no square root in the block. The real vector $e_1$, whose coefficient is real and not purely imaginary, is rootless:

$$ \nexists\,\tilde P\ \text{with}\ \tilde P\diamond\tilde P=e_1 , $$

although $e_1$ is itself square-zero, $e_1\diamond e_1=0$.

A Canonical Root

The proof of the theorem delivers a root and it is worth naming, because it is a section of the diagonal map.

Corollary (the canonical root). For every Hermitian pure vector $\tilde Q$ the element

$$ \tilde P=e_0+\tfrac12\tilde Q $$

is a square root of $\tilde Q$.

Proof. Write $\tilde Q=2i\mathbf g$ with $\mathbf g\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$; then $\tfrac12\tilde Q=i\mathbf g$ and $\tilde P=e_0+i\mathbf g$ has $p_0=1$, $a_0=0$, $\mathbf p=0$, $\mathbf a=\mathbf g$, so its diagonal is $2i(1\cdot\mathbf g-0-0)=2i\mathbf g=\tilde Q$.

The map $\tilde Q\mapsto e_0+\tfrac12\tilde Q$ is a real-linear section of the diagonal: it is injective, and its image, the roots with $p_0=1$, $a_0=\mathbf p=0$ and $\mathbf a\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$, is a real three-dimensional part of the root set. It is the analogue, for the block, of the two isolated roots $\pm\sqrt{Q_0}e_0$ of a complex scalar in the plain algebra, and it plays the same role: it names one root where the generic case has a family.

The Root Set

The Equation in Real Coordinates

Let $\tilde Q$ be a Hermitian pure vector and write $\tilde Q=2i\mathbf g$ with $\mathbf g\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$. The equation $\tilde P\diamond\tilde P=\tilde Q$ reads, in the real coordinates of the root,

$$ p_0\mathbf a-a_0\mathbf p-\mathbf a\times\mathbf p=\mathbf g . $$

Proposition (the equation is solved for the imaginary vector part). Fix $a_0\in\mathbb{R}$ and let $(p_0,\mathbf p)\neq(0,0)$. Then the equation has exactly one solution $\mathbf a$, the linear map $\mathbf a\mapsto p_0\mathbf a-\mathbf a\times\mathbf p$ being invertible with eigenvalues $p_0$ and $p_0\pm i|\mathbf p|$, all nonzero.

Proof. Rewrite the equation as $p_0\mathbf a+\mathbf p\times\mathbf a=\mathbf g+a_0\mathbf p$, that is $(p_0I+[\mathbf p]_\times)\mathbf a=\mathbf h$ with $\mathbf h=\mathbf g+a_0\mathbf p$ and $[\mathbf p]_\times$ the cross-product operator. The operator $[\mathbf p]_\times$ satisfies $[\mathbf p]_\times^{3}=-|\mathbf p|^{2}[\mathbf p]_\times$, and $p_0I+[\mathbf p]_\times$ has the eigenvalues $p_0$ and $p_0\pm i|\mathbf p|$, nonzero exactly when $(p_0,\mathbf p)\neq(0,0)$; it is therefore invertible, and the solution is unique.

Theorem (the root set). Let $\tilde Q$ be a Hermitian pure vector.

  • If $\tilde Q\neq0$, the root set of $\tilde Q$ is in bijection with $(\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus\{0\})\times\mathbb{R}$: the parameters are $(p_0,\mathbf p)\neq0$ and $a_0$, and $\mathbf a$ is determined by the proposition. The roots form a real five-parameter family.
  • If $\tilde Q=0$, the root set is the square-zero cone, the criterion $\tilde P\tilde P^{*}$ central with $\tilde P$ nonzero, of real dimension five (Nilpotents of the Antisymmetric Plain Sesqualgebra).

Proof. The equation is the proposition, and a root with $(p_0,\mathbf p)=(0,0)$ satisfies it only when $\mathbf g=0$; hence for $\tilde Q\neq0$ every root has $(p_0,\mathbf p)\neq0$ and the parametrisation is a bijection, of real dimension $4+1=5$. For $\tilde Q=0$ the equation is $\tilde P\diamond\tilde P=0$, which is the square-zero condition, and its set is the cone of The Vector Part of the Square and the Jacobi Failure of the Antisymmetric Plain Sesqualgebra.

The dimension is the count of the free parameters: the root has eight real components, the four real components of $\tilde p$ and the four of $\tilde a$, and the diagonal imposes the three real equations $\mathbf h=\mathbf g$ on them, so the root set has real dimension $8-3=5$.

The Classification

Collecting the two steps, every radicand falls into exactly one row.

condition on $\tilde Q$ number of roots
$\tilde Q\notin i\mathbb{R}^{3}$ none
$\tilde Q\in i\mathbb{R}^{3}$, $\tilde Q\neq0$ a real five-parameter family
$\tilde Q=0$ the square-zero cone, a real five-dimensional cone

The generic radicand with a root is a nonzero Hermitian pure vector, and its root set is a five-parameter family of which the canonical root $e_0+\tfrac12\tilde Q$ is one member. The radicands without a root are the elements with a nonzero scalar part or a non-imaginary vector coefficient, and they are the majority of the space.

The Central Values

The classification specialises to the three central values of Square Roots of Minus One, Zero and Plus One in the Antisymmetric Plain Sesqualgebra, and the agreement is the check that the two methods are the same.

A central value $\lambda e_0$ is a Hermitian pure vector exactly when $\lambda=0$, since $i\mathbb{R}^{3}$ has vanishing scalar part. Hence the row of the classification is the third for $\lambda=0$ and the first for $\lambda=\pm1$: the block has the roots of $0$ and no root of $-1$ or $+1$. This is the parity obstruction of the companion article, recovered here as the statement that $\pm e_0\notin i\mathbb{R}^{3}$, and it is a special case of the necessary condition and not a new computation.

Worked Examples

The examples are computed with the classification and verified by squaring.

A nonzero Hermitian pure vector. For $\tilde Q=2ie_3$ the parameter is $\mathbf g=e_3$, and the canonical root is $\tilde P=e_0+ie_3$. A second root is obtained from the parameters $p_0=1$, $\mathbf p=e_1$, $a_0=1$: the proposition solves $\mathbf a+(\mathbf e_1\times\mathbf a)=e_3+e_1$, that is $\mathbf a=e_1+\tfrac12e_2+\tfrac12e_3$, and the element $\tilde P=e_0+e_1+i(e_0+e_1+\tfrac12e_2+\tfrac12e_3)$ has the same diagonal $2ie_3$, which is checked by $p_0\mathbf a-a_0\mathbf p-\mathbf a\times\mathbf p=\mathbf a-e_1-\mathbf a\times e_1=e_3$.

A rootless element. For $\tilde Q=e_2$ the vector coefficient is real and not purely imaginary, so $\tilde Q\notin i\mathbb{R}^{3}$ and there is no root, though $e_2$ is square-zero.

A square-zero radicand. For $\tilde Q=0$ the root set is the cone; the member $\tilde P=e_1+ie_1$, a complex multiple of the unit real quaternion $e_1$, is a root, since $p_0\mathbf a-a_0\mathbf p-\mathbf a\times\mathbf p=0-e_1\times e_1=0$; the element $e_1+ie_2$, by contrast, has the diagonal $2ie_3\neq0$ and is not a root of $0$.

Summary

The square-root problem of the block is solved by the real splitting $\tilde P=\tilde p+i\tilde a$ of the element into two real quaternions. The diagonal is

$$ \tilde P\diamond\tilde P=2i\,\bigl(p_0\mathbf a-a_0\mathbf p-\mathbf a\times\mathbf p\bigr), $$

$2i$ times a real vector, so the image of the diagonal is the space $i\mathbb{R}^{3}=\mathbb{M}_+\cap\operatorname{Vect}(\mathbb{B})$ of the Hermitian pure vectors, of real dimension three, and no radicand outside it has a root.

For a nonzero Hermitian pure vector $\tilde Q=2i\mathbf g$ the root set is in bijection with $(\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus\{0\})\times\mathbb{R}$, the parameter $\mathbf a$ being the unique solution of $(p_0I+[\mathbf p]_\times)\mathbf a=\mathbf g+a_0\mathbf p$; the roots form a real five-parameter family, and $\tilde P=e_0+\tfrac12\tilde Q$ is a canonical member. For $\tilde Q=0$ the root set is the square-zero cone, of real dimension five.

The three central values are the degenerate data: $\pm e_0$ are outside $i\mathbb{R}^{3}$ and are rootless, and $0$ is the cone. The classification, the two models and the rank of a value are the articles named; this article is the Common Theory account of the same classification.
