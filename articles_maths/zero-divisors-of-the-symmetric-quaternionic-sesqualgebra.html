Zero Divisors of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra

Introduction

This article characterizes the zero divisors of the block, the symmetric quaternionic sesquilinear product $\tilde P\star\tilde Q=\tfrac12(\tilde P^{\natural}\tilde Q^{*}+\tilde Q^{*}\tilde P^{\natural})$ of Introduction to the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra of Biquaternions. It follows Norm and Invertibility in the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra, which recorded that the block has no unit and no inverse, and it opens the element theory of the block: the two families it separates are read in full in Idempotents of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra and Nilpotents of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra.

A divisor is relative to a product. The definition is stated once for a general product in Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures, §The Zero Divisor and the Annihilator, and read on the products of the space; the unqualified word means the zero divisor of the block, and every other product is named. The four general products of the space share one zero-divisor set, $\{\tilde Q\neq0: C(\tilde Q)=0\}$ with $C(\tilde Q)=\sum_\mu Q_\mu^2$, a coincidence of the algebra proved in Zero Divisors in the 12 Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space; the block is a symmetric part of one of them and its set is larger, and the enlargement is the point of §Comparison with the Four General Products.

The product itself, its two slot involutions and its two one-sided actions are Introduction to the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra of Biquaternions; the general construction of the sesqualgebra parts is The Symmetric and Antisymmetric Parts of a Sesqualgebra Product; the form $K$ is The Krein Form as a Product on the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra; the diagonal and the two halves are The Non-Central Algebraic Norm and the Two Halves of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra; the operators are The Multiplication Operators of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra; and the zero divisors of the plain product, whose criterion is the same central square, are Zero Divisors of the General Plain Algebra.

Conventions. $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$ with basis $e_0,e_1,e_2,e_3$, $e_1^2=e_2^2=e_3^2=-e_0$ and $e_1e_2=e_3$; $\tilde Q=\sum_\mu Q_\mu e_\mu$ with $Q_\mu\in\mathbb{C}$ and central scalar imaginary $i$; $\vec Q=\sum_kQ_ke_k$; $(\vec P,\vec Q)=\sum_kP_kQ_k$ is the complex bilinear dot product; ${}^{\natural}$ fixes $e_0$ and negates $e_1,e_2,e_3$ with the coefficients fixed, $\bar{\cdot}$ conjugates the coefficients, and ${}^{*}={}^{\natural}\circ\bar{\cdot}$; and $\varepsilon=(1,-1,-1,-1)$. The central square of an element is $C(\tilde Q)=\tilde Q\tilde Q^{\natural}=\sum_\mu Q_\mu^2=Q_0^2+(\vec Q,\vec Q)$, the product of the plain bilinear product with the natural conjugation, as in Zero Divisors of the General Plain Algebra. Throughout this article the product is the block, written $\star$.

The results. A nonzero biquaternion is a zero divisor of the block exactly when $Q_0=0$ or $C(\tilde Q)=Q_0^2+(\vec Q,\vec Q)=0$, so the zero-divisor set is the enlarged set $(\{Q_0=0\}\cup\{C(\tilde Q)=0\})\setminus\{0\}$. It splits into the pure family $\{Q_0=0\}$, the nonzero elements of the vector subspace, and the non-pure family $\{Q_0\neq0,\ C(\tilde Q)=0\}$, whose members have the central square of the plain zero divisors. The block's set strictly contains the single zero-divisor set $\{\tilde Q\neq0:C(\tilde Q)=0\}$ of the four general products, the difference being the pure non-isotropic vectors such as $e_1$. The left and the right zero divisors coincide, and the regular elements are those with $Q_0\neq0$ and $C(\tilde Q)\neq0$.

Definition

The word names a product, so the definition is stated for a general product and then read on the block; it is stated once and for all in Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures, §The Zero Divisor and the Annihilator.

Definition (zero divisor of a product). Let $\star$ be a product on $\mathbb{B}$ and let $\tilde Q\in\mathbb{B}$. The element $\tilde Q$ is a left zero divisor of $\star$ when $\tilde Q\neq0$ and there is a nonzero $\tilde R$ with $\tilde Q\star\tilde R=0$; a right zero divisor of $\star$ when $\tilde Q\neq0$ and there is a nonzero $\tilde R$ with $\tilde R\star\tilde Q=0$; and a zero divisor of $\star$ when it is one or the other. An element that is both is a two-sided zero divisor of $\star$.

Both elements are required to be nonzero, so the origin is not a zero divisor of any product. The annihilators of $\tilde Q$ in $\star$ are $A_\star(\tilde Q)=\{\tilde X:\tilde Q\star\tilde X=0\}$ and $B_\star(\tilde Q)=\{\tilde X:\tilde X\star\tilde Q=0\}$.

On the block. Substituting $\star$ for the block, the two defining equations are $\tilde Q\star\tilde R=0$ and $\tilde R\star\tilde Q=0$. On a general pair the scalar–vector form of Introduction to the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra of Biquaternions reads

$$ \tilde Q\star\tilde R=\bigl[Q_0\overline{R_0}-(\vec Q,\overline{\vec R})\bigr] -Q_0\overline{\vec R}-\overline{R_0}\,\vec Q , $$

so the equation $\tilde Q\star\tilde R=0$ is the pair

$$ Q_0\overline{R_0}=(\vec Q,\overline{\vec R}), \qquad Q_0\overline{\vec R}+\overline{R_0}\,\vec Q=\mathbf 0 . $$

Unqualified, the product is the block for the rest of the article.

The Criterion

A nonzero biquaternion $\tilde Q$ is a zero divisor of the block exactly when $Q_0=0$ or $C(\tilde Q)=Q_0^2+(\vec Q,\vec Q)=0$, so the zero-divisor set is the disjoint union of the pure family $\{Q_0=0,\ \tilde Q\neq0\}$ and the non-pure family $\{Q_0\neq0,\ C(\tilde Q)=0\}$.

Theorem (the criterion of the block). A nonzero biquaternion $\tilde Q$ is a zero divisor of the block if and only if

$$ Q_0=0 \qquad\text{or}\qquad C(\tilde Q)=Q_0^2+(\vec Q,\vec Q)=0 . $$

Proof. Write the two defining equations for the unknown $\tilde R$.

The case $Q_0=0$. The vector equation $Q_0\overline{\vec R}+\overline{R_0}\vec Q=\mathbf 0$ becomes $\overline{R_0}\vec Q=\mathbf 0$, and since $\tilde Q\neq0$ has $\vec Q\neq0$, this forces $R_0=0$. The scalar equation becomes $0=(\vec Q,\overline{\vec R})$, that is $\sum_{k=1}^{3}Q_k\overline{R_k}=0$, a single complex equation in the three complex unknowns $R_1,R_2,R_3$; it has a nonzero solution, and with $R_0=0$ that solution gives a nonzero $\tilde R$ with $\tilde Q\star\tilde R=0$. Hence every element of vanishing scalar part is a left zero divisor.

The case $Q_0\neq0$. The vector equation gives $\overline{R_k}=-(\overline{R_0}/Q_0)Q_k$ for each $k$. Substituting into the scalar equation,

$$ Q_0\overline{R_0}=\sum_kQ_k\overline{R_k}=-\frac{\overline{R_0}}{Q_0}\sum_kQ_k^2=-\frac{\overline{R_0}}{Q_0}\bigl(C(\tilde Q)-Q_0^2\bigr), $$

which after multiplication by $Q_0$ reads $\overline{R_0}\,C(\tilde Q)=0$. If $C(\tilde Q)\neq0$ then $\overline{R_0}=0$, and the vector equation forces $\overline{\vec R}=\mathbf 0$, hence $\tilde R=0$: there is no zero divisor. If $C(\tilde Q)=0$ then the scalar equation holds for every $R_0$, the vector equation determines $\vec R$ from $R_0$, and any $R_0\neq0$ gives a nonzero $\tilde R$ with $\tilde Q\star\tilde R=0$. Hence a non-pure element is a left zero divisor exactly when $C(\tilde Q)=0$.

The converse. The two cases above cover every $\tilde Q\neq0$, and in each the existence of the nonzero $\tilde R$ is exactly the displayed condition. So the left zero divisors are the set stated.

Corollary (the two families). The zero-divisor set of the block is the disjoint union of the pure family $\{Q_0=0,\ \tilde Q\neq0\}$, the nonzero elements of the vector subspace, and the non-pure family $\{Q_0\neq0,\ C(\tilde Q)=0\}$, whose members have the same central square as the non-pure zero divisors of the plain product.

Proof. The two conditions $Q_0=0$ and $Q_0\neq0$ are exclusive; on the first the criterion is $Q_0=0$ alone, on the second it is $C(\tilde Q)=0$ alone. In the second family $\vec Q\neq0$, since $C(\tilde Q)=Q_0^2+(\vec Q,\vec Q)=0$ would force $(\vec Q,\vec Q)=-Q_0^2\neq0$.

The Two Sides and the Annihilators

The block is conjugate-commutative, so the left and the right zero divisors coincide and the two annihilators of an element are one set; the annihilator is a complex plane in the pure case and a complex line in the non-pure case.

The annihilator of an element is the kernel of the left or the right multiplication, the two coinciding because the block is conjugate-commutative; it is a complex plane in the pure case and a complex line in the non-pure case, so it separates the two families the criterion joins. The annihilators, the rank table and the reading on the remarkable subspaces are the companion article Annihilators of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra, below this article in this category.

Comparison with the Four General Products

Every zero divisor of a general product is a zero divisor of the block, and the inclusion is strict: the block's set adds the pure non-isotropic vectors, such as $e_1$, whose central square is $1$.

The four general products of $\mathbb{B}$ have one zero-divisor set, $\{\tilde Q\neq0:C(\tilde Q)=0\}$, where $C$ is the central square (Zero Divisors in the 12 Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space). The block's set contains it, and it contains more.

Theorem (the block's set contains the general one). Every zero divisor of a general product is a zero divisor of the block, and the inclusion is strict: the union of the block's two families is

$$ \{\tilde Q\neq0:Q_0=0\}\ \cup\ \{\tilde Q\neq0:C(\tilde Q)=0\} \;\supsetneq\; \{\tilde Q\neq0:C(\tilde Q)=0\} . $$

Proof. The second family of the block is exactly the general set, so the general set is contained. The enlargement is the set $\{Q_0=0,\ C(\tilde Q)\neq0\}$, nonempty: it contains $e_1$, whose scalar part vanishes and whose central square is $c(e_1)=1$.

The difference is therefore the pure non-isotropic vectors: the elements of the vector subspace whose central square does not vanish. On $e_1$ the general products give $e_1e_1=-e_0\neq0$, while the block gives $e_1\star e_2=0$ at $e_2$; the general criterion is blind to $e_1$ and the block's is not.

Remark (why the symmetric part enlarges the set). A general product of the row assigns to the pair $(\tilde Q,\tilde R)$ a value whose first slot carries the natural conjugation; the block is the half-sum of the two orders of its generating product, and the half-sum cancels the cross product that the general products leave in the value (Introduction to the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra of Biquaternions). The cancellation removes one complex condition from the vanishing equations of a pure pair, and one condition is the whole difference between the two rows.

The Complement: the Regular Elements

An element of the block is the origin, a zero divisor, or regular, and $\tilde Q$ is regular exactly when $Q_0\neq0$ and $C(\tilde Q)\neq0$; the regular elements replace the invertible ones that the absence of a unit leaves empty.

Definition. A nonzero element of the block that is neither a left nor a right zero divisor is regular. The origin is the only element excluded from both classes: an element is either the origin, a zero divisor (on both sides), or regular.

Theorem (the regular elements). $\tilde Q$ is regular exactly when

$$ Q_0\neq0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad C(\tilde Q)\neq0 . $$

Proof. The complement of the block's two families is the negation of the criterion of §The Criterion: $Q_0\neq0$ and $C(\tilde Q)\neq0$. By §The Two Sides and the Annihilators the left and the right predicates coincide, so being neither is the same as being one of them is false.

The regular elements are exactly the elements whose left multiplication is injective, by the corollary of §The Two Sides and the Annihilators; they are the largest class on which the operator calculus of the block is faithful. They are not invertible: the block has no unit (Norm and Invertibility in the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra), so regularity is the substitute for invertibility that the absence of a unit leaves.

Remark (the Krein form does not detect the zero divisors). The scalar part of the block's square is the Krein form $K(\tilde Q,\tilde Q)=|Q_0|^2-\sum_k|Q_k|^2$. On $e_0+e_1$ it vanishes, $K(e_0+e_1,e_0+e_1)=1-1=0$, while the element is regular, since $c(e_0+e_1)=1+1=2\neq0$; and on $e_1$ it does not vanish, $K(e_1,e_1)=-1$, while $e_1$ is a zero divisor. So neither implication $K(\tilde Q,\tilde Q)=0\Rightarrow$ zero divisor nor its converse holds, in contrast with the plain product where the vanishing of the central square is the criterion. The form $K$ and its isotropic cone are The Krein Form as a Product on the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra.

Worked Examples

A pure zero divisor. $\tilde Q=e_1$: $Q_0=0$, so it is a zero divisor, and $e_1\star e_2=0$ exhibits a nonzero annihilating partner. Its central square is $c(e_1)=1\neq0$, so it is not in the general set.

A regular element, not a zero divisor. $\tilde Q=e_0$: $Q_0=1\neq0$ and $c(e_0)=1\neq0$, so it is regular; it is the unit candidate and it is not a zero divisor, though it is not a unit.

A non-pure zero divisor. $\tilde Q=e_0+ie_1$: $C(\tilde Q)=1+i^2=1-1=0$ and $Q_0=1$, so it is a zero divisor, and indeed $(e_0+ie_1)\star(e_0+ie_1)=0$, so it annihilates itself.

A regular non-central element. $\tilde Q=e_0+e_1$: $Q_0=1$ and $C(\tilde Q)=2$, so it is regular; it is the witness that the Krein form does not detect the zero divisors.

The annihilating partner of a non-pure zero divisor. $\tilde Q=e_0+ie_1$, so $Q_0=1$, $Q_1=i$ and $C(\tilde Q)=0$. Choose $R_0=i$; the vector equation gives $\overline{R_1}=-(\overline{R_0}/Q_0)Q_1=-((-i)/1)(i)=-1$, that is $R_1=-1$, and the partner is $\tilde R=ie_0-e_1$; one checks $(e_0+ie_1)\star(ie_0-e_1)=0$.

Summary

The zero divisor of the block is defined by an equation in the product, and the block's set is

$$ \{\tilde Q\neq0:Q_0=0\}\ \cup\ \{\tilde Q\neq0:C(\tilde Q)=0\}, \qquad C(\tilde Q)=Q_0^2+(\vec Q,\vec Q), $$

the disjoint union of the pure family, the nonzero elements of the vector subspace, and the non-pure family, the elements with nonzero scalar part and vanishing central square. The block is conjugate-commutative, so the left and the right zero divisors coincide, the two annihilators of an element are one set, and every zero divisor is two-sided. The annihilator is a complex plane in the pure case and a complex line in the non-pure case, and the left multiplication of an element is injective exactly on the regular elements $Q_0\neq0$, $C(\tilde Q)\neq0$, with rank eight, six and four according to the class and rank zero at the origin.

The block's set strictly contains the single zero-divisor set $\mathcal{C}$ of the four general products, the difference being the pure non-isotropic vectors: $e_1$ is a zero divisor of the block with $c(e_1)=1$. The Krein form does not detect the zero divisors, vanishing on the regular $e_0+e_1$ and not on the zero divisor $e_1$; the block has no unit, so the regular elements are the substitute for the invertible ones.
