# __Matrix Representations : The Centraliser and the Double Centraliser__

## Introduction

A matrix has a commutant: the set of matrices that commute with it. The commutant is small for a non-scalar matrix and large for a scalar one. The double commutant returns the algebra generated by the matrix in every case. In the regular model there are two further objects: the commutant of the single regular matrix, which contains the mixing of the two spinor planes, and the commutant of the whole left image, which is the right image. The three objects have different dimensions, and the differences are the subject of this article.

This article computes the commutant and the double commutant in the $2\times2$ model and in the $4\times4$ regular model, the relative commutant inside the image, and the double centraliser of the image. It is the article in which the multiplicity of the regular model is separated from the algebra of the element.

**Conventions.** The algebra and the displays are those of *Matrix Representations : The Models and the Change of Frame*. The element is $\tilde Q=Q_te_t+\vec Q$, its $2\times2$ matrix is $A=\mathsf M_2(\tilde Q)$, and its regular matrix is $M=\mathsf M_4^{L}(\tilde Q)=I_2\otimes A$ in the Peirce frame. The commutant of $A$ is
$$
C(A)=\{X\in M_2(\mathbb{C}):XA=AX\},
$$
and the algebra generated by $A$ is $\mathbb{C}[A]$. The left and right images are $M_4(\mathbb{C})_{\mathbb{B}_L}$ and $M_4(\mathbb{C})_{\mathbb{B}_R}$.

**Boundaries.** The module-theoretic commutant is *The Centraliser*; the eigenbasis is *Matrix Representations : The Eigenbasis and the Diagonalising Matrix*; the Jordan case is *Matrix Representations : The Jordan Basis and the Jordanising Matrix*; the right regular operators are *Matrix Representations : The Right Regular Operators*; the Sylvester operator is *The Sylvester Operator and the Kronecker Sum of $M_4(\mathbb{C})$*.

## Common Aspects

A matrix has a commutant: the set of matrices that commute with it. The commutant is small for a non-scalar matrix and large for a scalar one, and the double commutant returns the algebra generated by the matrix in every case. In the regular model there are two further objects: the commutant of the single regular matrix, which contains the mixing of the two spinor planes, and the commutant of the whole left image, which is the right image. The three objects have different dimensions, and the differences are the subject of this article.

**The commutant of an element.** For $X\in M_2(\mathbb{C})$,
$$
C(X)=\{Y\in M_2(\mathbb{C}):XY=YX\},
\qquad
C(X)=
\begin{cases}
M_2(\mathbb{C}), & X=\lambda\mathrm I_2,\\
\mathbb{C}[X]=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{\mathrm I_2,X\}, & X\neq\lambda\mathrm I_2,
\end{cases}
\qquad
C\bigl(C(X)\bigr)=\mathbb{C}[X].
$$
The dimension is $4$ for a scalar and $2$ for a non-scalar. The minimal polynomial classifies the non-scalar case:

| Type of $X$ | $m_X(t)$ | $\mathbb{C}[X]$ | Dimension |
|---|---|---|---|
| scalar | $t-\lambda$ | $\mathbb{C}$ | $1$ |
| distinct eigenvalues | $(t-\lambda_+)(t-\lambda_-)$ | $\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}$ | $2$ |
| repeated eigenvalue | $(t-\lambda)^2$ | $\mathbb{C}[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$ | $2$ |

In the distinct case the two spectral projectors are a basis of idempotents. In the repeated case the algebra is the dual numbers, with $\varepsilon=N$ the nilpotent part and $N^2=0$. The scalar case has a one-dimensional polynomial algebra inside a four-dimensional commutant, because every matrix commutes with a scalar.

**The proof.** If the eigenvalues are distinct, $X$ is conjugate to $\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_+,\lambda_-)$; a commuting matrix is diagonal, and every diagonal matrix is the interpolation of its two entries at the two eigenvalues. If the eigenvalue is repeated, $X$ is conjugate to $\lambda\mathrm I_2+N$ with $N\neq0$ and $N^2=0$; a matrix commuting with $N=E_{12}$ is upper triangular Toeplitz, $a\mathrm I_2+bN$. In both cases the commutant is commutative and two-dimensional, hence equal to $\mathbb{C}[X]$. For a scalar, $C(X)=M_2(\mathbb{C})$ and $C(M_2(\mathbb{C}))=\mathbb{C}\mathrm I_2=\mathbb{C}[X]$, so the double commutant never enlarges the algebra.

**The double centraliser of the image.** If $T$ commutes with every left multiplication, then
$$
T(\tilde X)=T(\tilde Xe_t)=\tilde X\,T(e_t),
$$
so $T$ is right multiplication by $T(e_t)$. Hence
$$
\{T:T\mathsf M_4^{L}(\tilde Q)=\mathsf M_4^{L}(\tilde Q)T\ \text{for all }\tilde Q\}
=M_4(\mathbb{C})_{\mathbb{B}_R},
$$
and the same argument with the sides exchanged gives the converse. Both images have complex dimension four and their intersection is the scalar line $\mathbb{C}\mathrm I_4$. The double centraliser of the image is therefore the image, not the algebra generated by one element:
$$
C\bigl(M_4(\mathbb{C})_{\mathbb{B}_L}\bigr)=M_4(\mathbb{C})_{\mathbb{B}_R},
\qquad
C\bigl(M_4(\mathbb{C})_{\mathbb{B}_R}\bigr)=M_4(\mathbb{C})_{\mathbb{B}_L}.
$$

| Object | Commutant | Double commutant |
|---|---|---|
| the element $M$ | dimension $8$ or $16$ | $\mathbb{C}[M]$, dimension $2$ or $1$ |
| the left image | the right image, dimension $4$ | the left image |
| the right image | the left image, dimension $4$ | the right image |

**The eigenbasis ambiguity.** Two matrices diagonalise $M$ exactly when they differ by an element of the commutant of the diagonal form:
$$
C'=CG,\qquad G\in C\bigl(\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_+,\lambda_+,\lambda_-,\lambda_-)\bigr).
$$
For distinct eigenvalues this commutant is $GL_2(\mathbb{C})\times GL_2(\mathbb{C})$, of dimension eight; the two factors change the basis inside the two eigenspaces. In the entry model the same eigenvalues have multiplicity one and the ambiguity is the invertible diagonal group, of dimension two. The extra six parameters are exactly the mixing of the two planes, the same factor that appears in the commutant of $M$.

**The adjugate and the dagger.** By Cayley–Hamilton in the entry model, $\operatorname{adj}A=(\operatorname{Tr}A)\mathrm I_2-A$, so the adjugate lies in $\mathbb{C}[A]\subseteq C(A)$ for every $A$. A matrix commutes with its adjugate, and the inverse of an invertible matrix lies in the commutant as well. The Hermitian conjugate lies in the commutant exactly when the matrix is normal:
$$
A^{\dagger}\in C(A)\iff AA^{\dagger}=A^{\dagger}A.
$$
For a normal matrix the spectral theorem makes $A^{\dagger}$ a polynomial in $A$; for a non-normal matrix the two do not commute. In the regular model the adjugate is $NM^{\natural}$, and since $M^{\natural}=2Q_t\mathrm I_4-M$, it is a polynomial in $M$ and lies in the commutant. The dagger lies in $C(M)$ exactly on the normal locus.

## The Coefficient Frame

**The right matrix.** In the coefficient frame the right regular matrix is
$$
\mathsf M_{4,\mathrm{coef}}^{R}(\tilde Q)
=C_{\Pi}\bigl(\mathsf M_2(\tilde Q)^{\mathsf T}\otimes\mathrm I_2\bigr)C_{\Pi}^{-1}
=D\,\mathsf M_{4,\mathrm{coef}}^{L}(\tilde Q)^{\mathsf T}D
=D\,\mathsf M_{4,\mathrm{coef}}^{L}(\tilde Q^{\natural})D,
$$
with $D=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$. In the coefficient frame transposition is quaternion conjugation, and the two factors $D$ are the coefficient form of the exchange.

**The relative commutant.** The part of the commutant of $M$ that lies in the left image is
$$
C_L(M)=\{X\in M_4(\mathbb{C})_{\mathbb{B}_L}:XM=MX\}=L\bigl(C(\tilde Q)\bigr).
$$
Its dimension is $2$ for a non-scalar element and $4$ for a scalar. The gap between the full commutant and the relative one is six for a non-scalar element and twelve for a scalar; it measures the mixing of the two planes, which is invisible inside the image.

**The images and the scalars.** The left and right images are distinct four-dimensional subspaces meeting in $\mathbb{C}\mathrm I_4$, and the double centraliser of either is the other. In the coefficient frame the right matrix is not block diagonal in the same way as the left matrix, because the coefficient basis does not split the two spinor planes; the block form is a property of the Peirce frame. The scalar case is the same in both frames: $C(M)=M_4(\mathbb{C})$, of dimension sixteen, while the relative commutant is the four-dimensional image.

## The Entry Model

**The commutant.** The entry model gives
$$
C(A)=
\begin{cases}
M_2(\mathbb{C}), & A=\lambda\mathrm I_2,\\
\mathbb{C}[A], & A\neq\lambda\mathrm I_2,
\end{cases}
\qquad
C\bigl(C(A)\bigr)=\mathbb{C}[A].
$$
The dimension is $4$ for a scalar and $2$ for a non-scalar. In the distinct case $\mathbb{C}[A]\cong\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}$ and the spectral projectors are a basis of idempotents; in the repeated case $\mathbb{C}[A]\cong\mathbb{C}[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$ with $\varepsilon$ the nilpotent part.

**The adjugate and the dagger.** The adjugate is $(\operatorname{Tr}A)\mathrm I_2-A$, a polynomial in $A$, so it lies in the commutant. The dagger lies in the commutant exactly when $A$ is normal. The inverse, when it exists, is the adjugate divided by the determinant and also lies in the commutant.

**The eigenbasis ambiguity.** For distinct eigenvalues the diagonalising matrix is defined up to right multiplication by the invertible diagonal group, of dimension two. The two parameters rescale the two eigenvectors independently.

## The Peirce Frame

**The commutant of the matrix.** In the Peirce frame,
$$
C(M)=\{B\in M_4(\mathbb{C}):B(\mathrm I_2\otimes A)=(\mathrm I_2\otimes A)B\}
=M_2(\mathbb{C})\otimes C(A).
$$
The proof writes $B$ as a $2\times2$ block matrix; the condition $BM=MB$ is $B_{ij}A=AB_{ij}$ for every pair, so each block lies in $C(A)$. The dimension is
$$
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}C(M)=4\dim_{\mathbb{C}}C(A)=
\begin{cases}
16, & \tilde Q\text{ scalar},\\
8, & \tilde Q\text{ non-scalar}.
\end{cases}
$$
The factor $4$ is the dimension of the multiplicity space of the two spinor planes; it is present in $M_4(\mathbb{C})$ and absent from the entry model.

**The two value algebras.** When $A$ has distinct eigenvalues,
$$
C(M)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}
a_1\mathrm I_2&b_1\mathrm I_2\\
c_1\mathrm I_2&d_1\mathrm I_2
\end{pmatrix}:\ a_i,b_i,c_i,d_i\in\mathbb{C}\right\}
\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathbb{C}^2.
$$
When $A$ is a Jordan block, $C(A)=\mathbb{C}[N]$ and
$$
C(M)=M_2(\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathbb{C}[N]=\{a\mathrm I_4+b(\mathrm I_2\otimes N)\},
$$
again of dimension eight. The mixing survives; what changes is the value algebra.

**The double commutant.** For non-scalar $A$, $C(M)=M_2(\mathbb{C})\otimes C(A)$ with $C(A)=\mathbb{C}[A]$ commutative. The centraliser of $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ is the scalar line, and the centraliser of $C(A)$ is $C(A)$ itself, because $C(A)$ is maximal commutative. Hence
$$
C\bigl(C(M)\bigr)=\mathrm I_2\otimes C(A)=\mathbb{C}[\mathrm I_2\otimes A]=\mathbb{C}[M],
$$
of dimension two. For scalar $A$, $C(M)=M_4(\mathbb{C})$ and its centraliser is the scalar line, again $\mathbb{C}[M]$. The passage to the regular model does not change the double commutant, because the two copies are equal and the minimal polynomial does not grow; what grows is the commutant.

**The degenerate case.** When the eigenvalues coincide, a diagonalisable $M$ is scalar and the eigenbasis ambiguity is all of $GL_4(\mathbb{C})$; a non-scalar $M$ with a repeated eigenvalue has no eigenbasis, and its commutant is the eight-dimensional algebra above.

## Summary

The commutant of a non-scalar $2\times2$ matrix is the plane $\mathbb{C}[X]=\operatorname{span}\{\mathrm I_2,X\}$, of dimension two; for a scalar it is the whole algebra, of dimension four. The double commutant is $\mathbb{C}[X]$ in every case. In the regular model the commutant of the single matrix is $M_2(\mathbb{C})\otimes C(A)$, of dimension eight for a non-scalar and sixteen for a scalar, while the relative commutant inside the image has dimension two or four. The commutant of the whole left image is the right image, both of dimension four, and the double centraliser of the image returns the image. The eigenbasis ambiguity is $GL_1\times GL_1$ in $2\times2$ and $GL_2\times GL_2$ in $4\times4$; the extra six parameters are the mixing of the two spinor planes, the same multiplicity that separates the commutant of the regular matrix from the commutant of the element.
