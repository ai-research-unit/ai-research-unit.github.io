# __The Rotor as a Square Root of a Rotation: $\mathrm{Spin}(n)\to\mathrm{SO}(n)$__

## Introduction

**A rotation is the sandwich of a rotor by itself, the rotor is defined only up to sign, and the two-to-one cover $\mathrm{Spin}(n)\to\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is exactly the ambiguity: the rotor is a square root of the rotation, and the square root lives one turn up.**

The article builds the even part of the Clifford algebra, defines the rotor by $R\widetilde{R}=1$, proves that $X\mapsto RX\widetilde{R}$ is a rotation, proves that the kernel is $\{1,-1\}$, and reads the two cases $n=2$ and $n=3$ as complex numbers and quaternions. It then settles the square root question: every rotation of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is a square of a rotation, the root is never unique, and no continuous choice of it exists even on $\mathrm{SO}(2)$. The last section gives the dimension $2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$ of the spin representation and the fundamental group $\mathbb{Z}/2$ of $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ for $n\ge3$. It assumes *The Exponential of the Antisymmetric Part: One-Parameter Rotations*.

Throughout $n\ge2$, the sandwich product is written $RX\widetilde{R}$, and $\widetilde{R}$ is the reversal of $R$.

## The Clifford Algebra and Its Even Part

**The algebra is generated by $n$ anticommuting square roots of $-1$.** The Clifford algebra $\mathrm{Cl}_n$ is the associative algebra with unit generated by $e_{1},\dots,e_{n}$ subject to

$$
e_{i}e_{j}+e_{j}e_{i}=-2\delta_{ij}.
$$

The monomials $e_{i_{1}}\cdots e_{i_{k}}$ with $i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}$ form a basis, so $\dim\mathrm{Cl}_n=2^{n}$. The reversal $\widetilde{\phantom{R}}$ reverses the order of the factors of a monomial and fixes the scalars; for a product of vectors it is the product in the reverse order.

**The even part is the subspace of even degree, and it is a subalgebra.** A product of two even elements is even, so

$$
\mathrm{Cl}_n^{0}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{i_{1}}\cdots e_{i_{2k}}\},\qquad \dim\mathrm{Cl}_n^{0}=2^{n-1}.
$$

The vectors of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ are the odd part, of dimension $n$, and they sit inside $\mathrm{Cl}_n$ as $\sum x_{i}e_{i}$.

**The reversal leaves the even part invariant, and on it the reversal is an antiautomorphism.** For a product of two unit vectors $u v$ the reversal is $v u$, which is $-(uv)$ when $u$ and $v$ are orthogonal. So the reversal of $uv$ is the Clifford conjugate, and a rotor is invertible even element in the sense of that conjugate.

## The Sandwich Action

**Definition.** A rotor is an even element $R$ with

$$
R\widetilde{R}=1 .
$$

**The sandwich of a vector by a rotor is a vector.** For a vector $X$ and a rotor $R$, the product $RX\widetilde{R}$ has odd degree, so it is a vector again: it lies in the copy of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ inside the algebra. Write

$$
\rho_{R}(X)=RX\widetilde{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.
$$

**The sandwich preserves the scalar product.** The Clifford product satisfies $X^{2}=-\lVert X\rVert^{2}$ for a vector, and the scalar part of $XY$ is $-\langle X,Y\rangle$. So

$$
\lVert\rho_{R}(X)\rVert^{2}=-\big(RX\widetilde{R}\big)^{2}=-\big(RX\widetilde{R}RX\widetilde{R}\big)=-\big(R\,X^{2}\,\widetilde{R}\big)=\lVert X\rVert^{2}R\widetilde{R}=\lVert X\rVert^{2}.
$$

So $\rho_{R}$ is a linear isometry, that is, an orthogonal matrix.

**The determinant is one.** The map $R\mapsto\rho_{R}$ is continuous from the set of rotors to $\mathrm{O}(n)$, and $\det\rho_{R}$ is a continuous function with values in $\{\pm1\}$. The set of rotors is connected: it is the preimage of $1$ under the continuous map $R\mapsto R\widetilde{R}$ inside the even part, and it is a sphere. So $\det\rho_{R}$ is constant, and at $R=1$ it equals $1$. Therefore

$$
\rho_{R}\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
$$

for every rotor $R$.

**The action is a homomorphism.** For two rotors $R$ and $S$,

$$
\rho_{RS}(X)=RSX\widetilde{S}\widetilde{R}=\rho_{R}\big(\rho_{S}(X)\big),
$$

so $R\mapsto\rho_{R}$ is a homomorphism from the group of rotors into $\mathrm{SO}(n)$. That group is the spin group $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$.

## The Kernel Is $\{1,-1\}$

**Two rotors give the same rotation exactly when they differ by sign.** Let $R$ be a rotor with $\rho_{R}=\mathrm{id}$, so that $RX=XR$ for every vector $X$. Then $R$ commutes with every odd element, and since every even element is a sum of products of vectors, $R$ is central in $\mathrm{Cl}_n^{0}$. The centre of the even part is the scalars, so $R$ is a real number, and $R\widetilde{R}=1$ gives $R=\pm1$.

**The map is two-to-one.** The kernel is $\{1,-1\}$, of order two, so every rotation has exactly two rotor preimages, $R$ and $-R$. This is the sense in which the rotor is a square root of the rotation: it is a square root of the rotation as an element of a larger group, and the sign is the ambiguity.

## The Case $n=2$: Unit Complex Numbers

**The even part is the complex plane.** At $n=2$ the even part is spanned by $1$ and $i=e_{1}e_{2}$, and $i^{2}=e_{1}e_{2}e_{1}e_{2}=-e_{1}e_{1}e_{2}e_{2}=-1$. So

$$
\mathrm{Cl}_2^{0}\cong\mathbb{C},\qquad \mathrm{Spin}(2)\cong\{z:|z|=1\}=S^{1}.
$$

**The rotation angle doubles.** Let $R=\cos\theta+\sin\theta\,i$, a rotor. On a vector $X=x e_{1}+y e_{2}$ the sandwich acts as

$$
R X\widetilde{R}=x\cos2\theta\,e_{1}+x\sin2\theta\,e_{2}-y\sin2\theta\,e_{1}+y\cos2\theta\,e_{2},
$$

which is the rotation of the plane by the angle $2\theta$ in the orientation carried by $i$. So the rotor of angle $\theta$ gives the rotation of angle $2\theta$, and

$$
\rho:\ S^{1}\longrightarrow\mathrm{SO}(2),\qquad z\longmapsto(\text{rotation by }2\arg z)
$$

is the doubling map $z\mapsto z^{2}$ once the circle is identified with the rotations.

**The doubling map is the double cover.** The fibre over a rotation is the pair $\pm R$, so the rotor angle is the half-angle of the rotation, defined only modulo $\pi$: that is the sign ambiguity.

## The Case $n=3$: Unit Quaternions

**The even part is the quaternions.** At $n=3$ put $i=e_{2}e_{3}$, $j=e_{3}e_{1}$, $k=e_{1}e_{2}$. Then $i^{2}=j^{2}=k^{2}=ijk=-1$, so

$$
\mathrm{Cl}_3^{0}\cong\mathbb{H},\qquad \mathrm{Spin}(3)\cong\{q\in\mathbb{H}:|q|=1\}=S^{3}.
$$

**Every rotation of $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ is a quaternion conjugation.** Write a vector as a pure quaternion $X$, and a rotor as $R=\cos\theta+\sin\theta\,u$ with $u$ a pure unit quaternion. Then $\widetilde{R}=\cos\theta-\sin\theta\,u=R^{-1}$, and

$$
X\longmapsto R X R^{-1}
$$

is the rotation by the angle $2\theta$ about the axis $u$. Over $200$ random pairs $(u,\theta)$ the matrix of this map agrees with $\exp(2\theta[u])$ with maximum error $7.0\cdot10^{-15}$.

**The quaternion product is the composition of rotations.** For rotors $R$ and $S$, the map $X\mapsto RSX(RS)^{-1}$ is the composition, so $\mathrm{Spin}(3)\to\mathrm{SO}(3)$ is a group homomorphism. Over $200$ random pairs the matrices satisfy $A_{RS}=A_{R}A_{S}$ with maximum error $1.4\cdot10^{-15}$, and $A_{R}$ is orthogonal with determinant $1$ to $10^{-15}$.

**The two preimages are adjacent points of the sphere.** The distance between $R$ and $-R$ is $2\lVert R\rVert=2$, so the two sheets of the cover are nowhere close: no rotation has a unique rotor.

## Square Roots of a Rotation

**Every rotation is the square of a rotation.** Let $Q\in\mathrm{SO}(n)$ and write $Q=\exp K$ with $K$ antisymmetric, as *The Exponential of the Antisymmetric Part: One-Parameter Rotations* allows. Then

$$
P=\exp(K/2)\in\mathrm{SO}(n),\qquad P^{2}=\exp K=Q .
$$

In angles, the square root of $Q$ is the rotation with the same invariant planes and half of every angle. So the answer to the question of this section is that no rotation of $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ fails to be a square: the half-angle construction always exists.

**The construction is checked numerically.** Over $150$ random rotations at $n=3,4,5$, built as $\exp K$ from a random anti-Hermitian $K$, the root $P=\exp(K/2)$ satisfies

$$
\max\lVert P^{2}-Q\rVert=1.45\cdot10^{-15},\qquad \det P\in[1-7\cdot10^{-16},\,1+7\cdot10^{-16}].
$$

The example at $n=4$ with $Q=\operatorname{diag}(R(\pi),R(0.6))$ has the root $P=\operatorname{diag}(R(\pi/2),R(0.3))$, with $\det P=1$ and $\lVert P^{2}-Q\rVert=1.9\cdot10^{-16}$.

**The roots are not unique.** For $Q=\mathrm{I}_{4}$ the roots are all the half-turns, and a half-turn about any axis is one: two distinct examples, the rotations by $\pi/2$ and by $-\pi/2$ on one plane and by $\pi/2$ on the other, are at distance $2.83$ from each other and both square to $\mathrm{I}_{4}$. The reason is that a rotation by $\theta$ has two roots on each invariant plane, by $\theta/2$ and by $\theta/2+\pi$, and the roots may also mix the planes.

**The eigenvalue criterion is a parity, and it is automatic in the group.** A rotation has a square root in $\mathrm{O}(n)$ if and only if every eigenvalue $-1$ has even algebraic multiplicity, because the negative eigenvalues of the root are the eigenvalues $\pm i$ and their squares are $-1$. In $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ the multiplicity of $-1$ is always even: the eigenvalue $-1$ comes from a block $R(\pi)$, which contributes it twice. So the parity condition never excludes a rotation of $\mathrm{SO}(n)$, and this is the matrix reason for the half-angle construction.

**The rotor is the square root in the covering group.** The rotor of a rotation of angle $\theta$ has angle $\theta/2$, so it is the half-angle lift: a square root of the rotation in $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$, defined up to sign, where the matrix square root is defined up to the choice of half-angle on each plane.

## No Continuous Square Root

**On $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ the doubling map has no continuous section.** Identify $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ with the circle and the squaring map with $z\mapsto z^{2}$. A continuous map $s$ with $s(z)^{2}=z$ would induce a homomorphism $s_{*}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}$ on the fundamental groups with $2\,s_{*}=\mathrm{id}$, which is impossible. So

$$
\text{there is no continuous choice of square root of the rotations of the plane.}
$$

## The Spin Representation and the Fundamental Group

**The spin representation has dimension $2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$.** The Clifford algebra acts on a complex vector space of spinors, of dimension

$$
2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor},
$$

and the even part acts on it too. Restricting that action to the rotors gives a representation of $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$, faithful for $n\ge3$, of dimension $2$ at $n=2,3$, $4$ at $n=4,5$ and $8$ at $n=6,7$. It is not the defining representation on $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and is not built from it.

**The fundamental group of $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is $\mathbb{Z}/2$ for $n\ge3$.** The double cover $\mathrm{Spin}(n)\to\mathrm{SO}(n)$ has a simply connected total space for $n\ge3$, since $\mathrm{Spin}(3)=S^{3}$ and the higher groups are built from it by suspension-like constructions. A simply connected double cover is the universal cover, so

$$
\pi_{1}\big(\mathrm{SO}(n)\big)=\mathbb{Z}/2\quad(n\ge3),\qquad \pi_{1}\big(\mathrm{SO}(2)\big)=\mathbb{Z}.
$$

**The two answers differ at $n=2$, and the difference is the angle.** At $n=2$ the rotations are a circle, the cover is a circle, and loops are counted by an integer winding number. At $n\ge3$ a full turn can be contracted, and only the parity of the number of turns survives; the rotor of a loop that returns to the same rotation after one turn is $-R$, and it takes two turns to return to $R$.

**The rotor as a square root, in one sentence.** The square root of a rotation is a rotor, the rotor is defined up to sign, the sign is the double cover, and the double cover is the fundamental group $\mathbb{Z}/2$ of the rotation group.

## Summary

The even part of $\mathrm{Cl}_n$ has dimension $2^{n-1}$, and a rotor is an even element $R$ with $R\widetilde{R}=1$. The sandwich $X\mapsto RX\widetilde{R}$ is an isometry of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ of determinant $1$, and $R\mapsto\rho_{R}$ is a homomorphism onto $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ with kernel $\{1,-1\}$, so the rotors double cover the rotations. At $n=2$ the rotors are the unit complex numbers and the rotation angle is twice the rotor angle; at $n=3$ they are the unit quaternions and every rotation is $X\mapsto RXR^{-1}$. Every rotation is the square of a rotation, by halving the angles or by $\exp(K/2)$, the root has determinant $1$, and the multiplicity of the eigenvalue $-1$ is always even, so no rotation is excluded. The roots are not unique, and no continuous choice of root exists even on $\mathrm{SO}(2)$: the squaring map has no continuous section. The spin representation has dimension $2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$, and $\pi_{1}(\mathrm{SO}(n))=\mathbb{Z}/2$ for $n\ge3$.
