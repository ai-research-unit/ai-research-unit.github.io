The Bounded Sesquilinear Sandwich

Introduction

The two-sided operator of a sesquialgebra is the map $S_{a,b}(x) = ax^{*}b$, obtained by putting the involution in the middle slot and a fixed element on each side; it is the operator form of the triple product $\{a,x,b\}$, and it is the smallest operator that uses the whole sesquilinear structure, one slot carrying the involution and two the multiplication. This article reads that operator with a topology on the module: it computes its norm, shows that it is the composite of the ordinary two-sided multiplication $T_{a,b}(x) = axb$ with the involution, so that the two families have the same operator norms and are exchanged by composition with the involution, reads the bounded composition table and the inverse on invertible parameters, and identifies the group that the invertible sandwiches generate.

Three facts organise the article. The sandwich is bounded whenever the two-sided multiplication is, and with the same norm, because the involution is an isometry: $\lVert S_{a,b}\rVert = \lVert T_{a,b}\rVert \leq \lVert a\rVert\lVert b\rVert$. The map $(a,b) \mapsto S_{a,b}$ is a bounded bilinear map into the space of bounded conjugate-linear operators, and its image is the set of the conjugates of the two-sided multiplications, so the sandwiches inherit the whole composition table of the algebraic layer with each entry bounded by the product of the norms of the parameters. And the invertible sandwiches are those with invertible parameters, forming a coset of the group of invertible two-sided multiplications in the semidirect product of that group with the order-two group generated by the involution.

The article defines the sandwich with its norm, treats the two families and the involution, computes the composition table and its bounds, proves the invertibility and describes the group generated, and works the examples. The algebraic operator, its composition table, its invertible elements and its reflections are The Sesquilinear Sandwich Operator; the bilinear two-sided operator is The Sandwich Operator on an Algebra; the invariance of the norm under composition with the involution is Bounded Operators on a Sesquialgebra, §The Involution as an Operator; the submultiplicative estimate and the isometric involution are Banach Sesquialgebras, §The Submultiplicative Estimate and §The Isometric Involution. Throughout $(\mathbb{K},\varsigma)$ is $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$ with its continuous involution, $A$ is a normed sesquialgebra with submultiplicative norm and isometric involution, unital where a group is named, and the product is written $\star$.

The Definition and the Norm

The Two Families

Definition. For $a,b \in A$ the sesquilinear sandwich and the ordinary two-sided multiplication are the operators

$$ S_{a,b} : A \to A, \quad S_{a,b}(x) = a\,x^{*}\,b , \qquad\qquad T_{a,b} : A \to A, \quad T_{a,b}(x) = a\,x\,b . $$

The definition is that of The Sesquilinear Sandwich Operator, §The Sandwich, and it is recalled here because the operators are the subject; in the standard example of a unital involutive algebra with the derived operation $x \star y = xy^{*}$ the sandwich is the triple product $S_{a,b}(x) = \{a,x,b\}$ of Sesquialgebras, §The Ternary Product.

Proposition (the parities). The sandwich $S_{a,b}$ is additive and $\varsigma$-semilinear, $S_{a,b}(\lambda x) = \varsigma(\lambda)S_{a,b}(x)$, so it lies in $B^{\varsigma}(A)$; the two-sided multiplication $T_{a,b}$ is additive and $\mathbb{K}$-linear, so it lies in $B(A)$.

Proof. The parities are those of The Sesquilinear Sandwich Operator, §The Sandwich, read in the operator classes of Bounded Operators on a Sesquialgebra: the variable enters through the middle slot of the triple product, which is the $\varsigma$-semilinear one, while the two-sided multiplication is linear in the variable. $\square$

The Norm and the Involution

Theorem (the sandwich and the two-sided multiplication have the same norm). Let $A$ be a normed sesquialgebra with submultiplicative norm and isometric involution. Then

$$ S_{a,b} = T_{a,b} \circ {}^{*} , \qquad \lVert S_{a,b}\rVert = \lVert T_{a,b}\rVert \leq \lVert a\rVert\lVert b\rVert , $$

and on a unital object with $\lVert 1\rVert = 1$ one has $\lVert T_{a,b}\rVert \geq \lVert ab\rVert$, hence $\lVert S_{a,b}\rVert \geq \lVert ab\rVert$.

Proof. The identity is the definition: $T_{a,b}(x^{*}) = ax^{*}b = S_{a,b}(x)$. Composition with the involution is an isometry $B(A) \to B^{\varsigma}(A)$ by Bounded Operators on a Sesquialgebra, §The Involution as an Operator, so the two operator norms agree. For the bound, $\lVert T_{a,b}x\rVert = \lVert axb\rVert \leq \lVert a\rVert\lVert x\rVert\lVert b\rVert$ by the submultiplicative estimate, and the unital lower bound is the value at the unit, $\lVert T_{a,b}(1)\rVert = \lVert ab\rVert$. $\square$

Corollary (the parametrisation is bounded and bilinear). The map

$$ A \times A \longrightarrow B^{\varsigma}(A) , \qquad (a,b) \longmapsto S_{a,b} , $$

is $\mathbb{K}$-bilinear and continuous, $\lVert S_{a,b}\rVert \leq \lVert a\rVert\lVert b\rVert$, and it factors through a linear map $A \otimes A \to B^{\varsigma}(A)$ of norm at most one for the projective norm, by the universal property of Topological Tensor Products of Sesquialgebras, §The Projective Topology.

Proof. Bilinearity is read on the parameters, $S_{\lambda a + \mu c,b} = \lambda S_{a,b} + \mu S_{c,b}$ and $S_{a,\lambda b+\mu d} = \lambda S_{a,b} + \mu S_{a,d}$, which holds because the involution is additive and scalar-fixed on the middle slot; the bound is the theorem, and continuity follows because a bilinear map bounded by a constant is continuous. The factorisation is the universal property. $\square$

Remark. The bilinear layer has the same map without the involution: $(a,b) \mapsto T_{a,b}$ with $T_{a,b}(x) = axb$ is the elementary operator of The Left and Right Multiplication Operators on a Banach Algebra, §The Multiplication Operators and their Norms, and the sandwiches are its composite with the involution on the right. The two parameter maps therefore carry the same information and the same norms; the difference is the class of the value, odd for the sandwich and even for the two-sided multiplication, and it is the whole of the difference between the sesquilinear and the bilinear two-sided operator.

The Composition and its Bounds

The Composition Table

Theorem (the bounded composition table). For all $a,b,c,d,p,q,r,s \in A$,

$$ S_{c,d}\,S_{a,b} = T_{cb^{*},\,a^{*}d} , \qquad T_{p,q}\,S_{a,b} = S_{pa,\,bq} , \qquad S_{c,d}\,T_{p,q} = S_{cq^{*},\,p^{*}d} , \qquad T_{p,q}\,T_{r,s} = T_{pr,\,sq} , $$

and every composite is bounded with norm at most the product of the norms of the factors, which are at most the products $\lVert c\rVert\lVert d\rVert\lVert a\rVert\lVert b\rVert$ and so on.

Proof. The four identities are the composition table of The Sesquilinear Sandwich Operator, §The Multiplication Table, obtained by direct computation with $(uv)^{*} = v^{*}u^{*}$ and the associativity of the involutive algebra; the bounds follow from $\lVert ST\rVert \leq \lVert S\rVert\lVert T\rVert$ and the theorem of §The Norm and the Involution applied to each factor. $\square$

Corollary (the parity of a composite and its norm). The composite of two sandwiches is a two-sided multiplication, that of a sandwich and a two-sided multiplication in either order is a sandwich, and that of two two-sided multiplications is a two-sided multiplication; the parity of the composite is the product of the parities, $\lVert S_{c,d}S_{a,b}\rVert = \lVert T_{cb^{*},a^{*}d}\rVert$, and the sandwiches are not closed under composition.

Proof. The four cases are the table; the parity statement is the grading of Bounded Operators on a Sesquialgebra, §The Parity of a Composite; and the second sentence exhibits two sandwiches composing to a two-sided multiplication, so the family is not closed. $\square$

The Square and the Reflections

Proposition (the square and the reflections). The square of a sandwich is the two-sided multiplication

$$ S_{a,b}^{2} = T_{ab^{*},\,a^{*}b} , $$

bounded with norm at most $\lVert a\rVert^{2}\lVert b\rVert^{2}$; for a unitary $u$ the sandwich $S_{u,u}$ is the reflection of The Sesquilinear Sandwich Operator, §The Reflections, an involution of $A$ with $\lVert S_{u,u}\rVert \leq 1$, and it is an isometry exactly when $\lVert ux^{*}u\rVert = \lVert x\rVert$ for all $x$, which holds when the norm is a $\mathrm{C}^{*}$-norm.

Proof. The square is the table with $c = a$ and $d = b$, giving $T_{ab^{*},a^{*}b}$, and the norm bound is $(\lVert a\rVert\lVert b\rVert)^{2}$ by the theorem. For unitary $u$ one has $\lVert S_{u,u}\rVert \leq 1$, and $S_{u,u}$ is an involution by The Sesquilinear Sandwich Operator, §The Sandwich as a Reflection; the isometry criterion is the identity $\lVert S_{u,u}x\rVert = \lVert ux^{*}u\rVert$ and the ordinary definition of an isometry. $\square$

Remark. The reflections are the bounded sandwiches that are also involutions of the space, and the square of an arbitrary sandwich is a two-sided multiplication, so the family of the reflections is the intersection of the sandwiches with the involutions, parametrised by the unitary elements. The bound $\lVert S_{u,u}\rVert \leq 1$ is not an equality in general, and the equality is the $\mathrm{C}^{*}$-condition on the norm, which is the subject of Banach Sesquialgebras, §The Function Algebras.

The Invertible Sandwiches and the Group

The Inverse

Proposition (the invertible sandwiches). Let $A$ be unital. The sandwich $S_{a,b}$ is invertible in $B^{\varsigma}(A)$ exactly when $T_{a,b}$ is invertible in $B(A)$, that is exactly when $a$ and $b$ are units; then

$$ S_{a,b}^{-1} = S_{(b^{-1})^{*},\,(a^{-1})^{*}} , $$

and $\lVert S_{a,b}^{-1}\rVert = \lVert T_{a,b}^{-1}\rVert$ is the norm of the inverse two-sided multiplication.

Proof. $S_{a,b} = T_{a,b}\circ *$ is the composite of the invertible involution with $T_{a,b}$, so it is invertible exactly when $T_{a,b}$ is; the two-sided multiplication $T_{a,b}(x) = axb$ is invertible exactly when $a$ and $b$ are units, since $T_{a,b}T_{r,s} = T_{ar,sb}$ and the unit is $T_{1,1}$. The formula is the inverse of The Sesquilinear Sandwich Operator, §The Invertible Sandwiches: $S_{a,b}S_{(b^{-1})^{*},(a^{-1})^{*}} = T_{a((a^{-1})^{*})^{*},\,((b^{-1})^{*})^{*}b} = T_{aa^{-1},\,b^{-1}b} = T_{1,1} = \mathrm{id}$. The norm equality is the isometry of composition with the involution. $\square$

Corollary (the parametrisation on the units). On the units the parametrisation has kernel the group of central units, $S_{ap,p^{-1}b} = S_{a,b}$ for $p$ central and invertible, so the set of invertible sandwiches is the quotient of the group $A^{\times} \times A^{\times}$ by that action.

Proof. The identity is that of The Sesquilinear Sandwich Operator, §The Invertible Sandwiches: for central invertible $p$ the element $p$ commutes with the middle slot's involution and $S_{ap,p^{-1}b}(x) = ap\,x^{*}p^{-1}b = a\,x^{*}b$. Conversely, if $S_{a,b} = S_{a',b'}$ then $T_{a,b} = T_{a',b'}$, and evaluating at the unit and at a general element gives $ab = a'b'$ and $a x b = a' x b'$ for all $x$, whence $a'a^{-1}$ is central, so the kernel is the central units. $\square$

The Group Generated

Theorem (the group generated by the invertible sandwiches). Let $A$ be unital. Write

$$ G_0 = \{T_{p,q} : p, q \in A^{\times}\} , \qquad \Sigma^{\times} = \{S_{a,b} : a,b \in A^{\times}\} . $$

Then $G_0$ is a subgroup of the unit group of $B(A)$, isomorphic to $A^{\times} \times A^{\times}$ under $(p,q) \mapsto T_{p,q}$ with the multiplication $(p,q)(r,s) = (pr,sq)$, the involution satisfies $*\,T_{p,q}\,* = T_{q^{*},p^{*}}$, so it normalises $G_0$, and

$$ G = G_0 \rtimes \mathbb{Z}/2 = G_0 \cup \Sigma^{\times} $$

is the group generated by the invertible sandwiches, in which $\Sigma^{\times} = G_0 \circ *$ is the nontrivial coset of $G_0$.

Proof. The multiplication of the $T_{p,q}$ is $T_{p,q}T_{r,s} = T_{pr,sq}$ with inverse $T_{p^{-1},q^{-1}}$, so $G_0$ is a subgroup and the assignment $(p,q)\mapsto T_{p,q}$ is an isomorphism onto it. The conjugation identity $(*\circ T_{p,q}\circ *)(x) = (p x^{*} q)^{*} = q^{*}xp^{*} = T_{q^{*},p^{*}}(x)$ shows that $*$ normalises $G_0$, and $*^{2} = \mathrm{id}$, so $G_0$ and $*$ generate $G_0 \rtimes \mathbb{Z}/2$. Every invertible sandwich is $T_{a,b}\circ *$ with $T_{a,b} \in G_0$, so $\Sigma^{\times} = G_0 \circ *$ is the other coset, and the union is all of $G$. $\square$

Remark. The theorem is the topological form of the statement that the sandwiches are the coset of the two-sided multiplications by the involution, and it is the reason the family is read as a group rather than as an algebra: the sandwiches are not closed under composition, their products being even, but together with the even operators they fill a group which is the semidirect product of two copies of the unit group by the order-two group of the involution. The boundedness of all the operators is what makes the group an object of the topological layer, and the norms of the elements are the operator norms computed above.

The Comparison with the Bilinear Layer

Proposition (the relation to the sandwich operator on an algebra). Let $A$ be a normed sesquialgebra with the trivial involution, $\varsigma = \mathrm{id}$ and $* = \mathrm{id}$. Then the sandwich is the two-sided multiplication, $S_{a,b} = T_{a,b}$, the two families coincide, the composition table is the classical one, and the operator layer of this article is that of The Sandwich Operator on an Algebra, with the boundedness and the bounds of the bilinear layer.

Proof. With $* = \mathrm{id}$ the middle slot carries no involution and $S_{a,b}(x) = axb = T_{a,b}(x)$; the composition table reduces to $T_{a,b}T_{r,s} = T_{ar,sb}$ and the invertibility to the units. $\square$

Remark. The difference between the two layers is the involution in the middle slot, and it is exactly one factor of the bounded conjugate-linear operator $*$. The sandwich of the sesquilinear layer is therefore never itself a two-sided multiplication unless the involution is trivial or the element is adapted to it, and it is the simplest operator that separates the two layers: the left multiplication is the sandwich $S_{a,1}$, the right multiplication is the two-sided multiplication $T_{1,a^{*}}$, and the two-sided sandwich is the general $S_{a,b}$.

Examples

The Matrices

Example (the matrices, verdict: the sandwiches span the conjugate-linear operators). Let $A = M_n(\mathbb{C})$ with the operator norm, the conjugation and the product $X \star Y = XY^{*}$. The sandwich is $S_{A,B}(X) = AX^{*}B$, bounded with $\lVert S_{A,B}\rVert \leq \lVert A\rVert\lVert B\rVert$, and $\lVert S_{A,B}\rVert = \lVert T_{A,B}\rVert$. The matrix units give $S_{E_{ij},E_{kl}}(X) = E_{ij}X^{*}E_{kl}$, and these spans exhaust the conjugate-linear maps; so the sandwiches span $B^{\varsigma}(A)$, and the group of invertible sandwiches is the general linear group acting on both sides, $\Sigma^{\times} = GL_n \times GL_n$ modulo the scalars by the parametrisation kernel.

The Field

Example (the field, verdict: one-dimensional sandwiches). Let $A = \mathbb{C}$ with the modulus, the conjugation and the product $z \star w = z\bar w$. The sandwich is $S_{a,b}(z) = a\bar z b$, a conjugate-linear map of $\mathbb{C}$; $\lVert S_{a,b}\rVert = \lvert a\rvert\lvert b\rvert$, the two-sided multiplication is $T_{p,q}(z) = pqz$ with $\lVert T_{p,q}\rVert = \lvert p\rvert\lvert q\rvert$, and $B^{\varsigma}(\mathbb{C})$ is the one-dimensional space of these maps while $B(\mathbb{C})$ is the one-dimensional space of the multiplications. The invertible sandwiches are the $S_{a,b}$ with $a,b \neq 0$, $S_{a,b}^{-1} = S_{1/\bar b, 1/\bar a}$, and the group generated is the semidirect product of $\mathbb{C}^{\times} \times \mathbb{C}^{\times}$ with $\mathbb{Z}/2$.

The Sequences

Example (the sequences, verdict: bounded sandwiches of finite norm). Let $A = \ell^{1}$ with the norm, the termwise conjugation and the termwise product, which is submultiplicative. The sandwich $S_{x,y}(z) = x\,\bar z\,y$ is the termwise map $z_k \mapsto x_ky_k\bar z_k$, bounded with $\lVert S_{x,y}\rVert \leq \lVert x\rVert_{1}\lVert y\rVert_{1}$ and with norm equal to that of $T_{x,y}$; the invertible sandwiches are those with both parameters invertible in the termwise product, that is with all terms nonzero and bounded away from zero in modulus.

Summary

The sesquilinear sandwich is the operator $S_{a,b}(x) = ax^{*}b$, conjugate-linear in its variable and the composite of the ordinary two-sided multiplication with the involution, $S_{a,b} = T_{a,b}\circ {}^{*}$, so that the two families have equal operator norms, $\lVert S_{a,b}\rVert = \lVert T_{a,b}\rVert \leq \lVert a\rVert\lVert b\rVert$, with the lower bound $\lVert ab\rVert$ on a unital object with a unit of norm one. The parametrisation $(a,b) \mapsto S_{a,b}$ is a bounded bilinear map into $B^{\varsigma}(A)$ of norm at most one, factoring through the projective tensor product, and the bounded composition table is $S_{c,d}S_{a,b} = T_{cb^{*},a^{*}d}$, $T_{p,q}S_{a,b} = S_{pa,bq}$, $S_{c,d}T_{p,q} = S_{cq^{*},p^{*}d}$ and $T_{p,q}T_{r,s} = T_{pr,sq}$, in which the parity multiplies and the composite of two sandwiches is even.

The sandwich is invertible exactly when its two parameters are units, with $S_{a,b}^{-1} = S_{(b^{-1})^{*},(a^{-1})^{*}}$, and the parametrisation on the units has the central units as kernel. The invertible sandwiches form the nontrivial coset of the group $G_0$ of invertible two-sided multiplications in the semidirect product $G_0 \rtimes \mathbb{Z}/2$, the involution normalising $G_0$ through $*\,T_{p,q}\,* = T_{q^{*},p^{*}}$; the squares are the two-sided multiplications $S_{a,b}^{2} = T_{ab^{*},a^{*}b}$, and for a unitary $u$ the sandwich $S_{u,u}$ is a reflection of norm at most one. At the trivial involution the sandwich is the two-sided multiplication and the article is the bounded side of The Sandwich Operator on an Algebra.
