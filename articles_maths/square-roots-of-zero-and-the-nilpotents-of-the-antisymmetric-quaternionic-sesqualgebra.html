Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents of the Antisymmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra

Introduction

The equations $\tilde\Xi\diamond\tilde\Xi=0$ and $\tilde\Upsilon^{k}=0$ name a product, and the product here is the conjugate cross product

$$ \tilde P\diamond\tilde Q=\tfrac12\bigl(\tilde P^{\natural}\tilde Q^{*}-\tilde Q^{*}\tilde P^{\natural}\bigr)=\mathbf{P}\times\overline{\mathbf{Q}} $$

of Introduction to the Antisymmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra of Biquaternions, the operation named $\mathrm{AQS}$ in The 12 Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space.

The article treats two subjects, and in this order. The first is the square roots of the central value $0$ in the block, the elements $\tilde\Xi$ with $\tilde\Xi\diamond\tilde\Xi=0$, that is the square-zero elements of the block. The second is the nilpotents of the block, the elements some left-nested power of which vanishes. The two subjects are one subject and a refinement of it: the roots of $0$ are exactly the square-zero elements, hence exactly the nilpotents of index two, and the block carries in addition nilpotents of index four, so that the nilpotent class of the block is strictly larger than its square-zero set. The order is the order of the two subjects, the roots of zero first and the nilpotents after; the second part begins where the first stops.

The results. The block has a root of $0$: the roots of $0$ are the elements whose vector part is a complex multiple of a real vector, a cone of real dimension six, whose scalar coordinate is free, and they are the square-zero elements of the block. The set is the sum of two families: the central family, whose elements are the pure central multiples of the unit of the parent algebra, and the non-central family, whose elements are the complex multiples of the real directions of the vector subspace. Every root is the sum of one element of each, the two families meet at the origin alone, and every nonzero root of $0$ is a two-sided zero divisor and its own annihilating partner. The nilpotents of the block form two classes and no third: the square-zero elements, of index two, and the elements of index four, whose vector part is isotropic for the complex bilinear square without being a complex multiple of a real vector. The index is therefore two or four and never three, and it depends on the bracketing of the powers: the witness $e_1+ie_2$ vanishes at the fourth left-nested power and never at any right-nested power. No nilpotent is an idempotent, every nilpotent is a zero divisor, and the nilpotent class of the block is a strict part of its zero-divisor set, as it is for nine of the twelve structures.

Boundaries. The roots of $+1$ and the idempotents are Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the Antisymmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra; the roots of the general quadratic $\tilde\Xi\diamond\tilde\Xi=\tilde Q$ are Square Roots of a General Element in the Antisymmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra; the zero-divisor set, which contains the square-zero cone, is Zero Divisors of the Antisymmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra; the pairing, the operators and the two matrix models are The Sesquilinear Pairing of the Antisymmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra, The Adjoint Operators of the Antisymmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra, The Antisymmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra in the $2\times2$ Matrix Element Representation $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ and The Antisymmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra in the $4\times4$ Matrix Element Representation $M_4(\mathbb{C})_L$; the general definitions of a root of a central value, of a power and of a nilpotent are Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures; and the law behind the orthogonality of the recurrence is The Specificities of the Antisymmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra.

Conventions. The algebra is $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$ with basis $e_0=1,e_1,e_2,e_3$, central scalar imaginary $i$ and $e_1e_2=e_3$, so that $e_k^2=-e_0$ and $e_je_k=-e_ke_j$ for $j\neq k$. A generic element is $\tilde Q=Q_0e_0+\mathbf{Q}$ with $\mathbf{Q}=\sum_{k=1}^{3}Q_ke_k$ and $Q_k=q_k+iq'_k$, the coefficients $q_k,q'_k$ real; the two operations of the algebra are written

$$ (\mathbf P,\mathbf Q)=\sum_{k=1}^{3}P_kQ_k,\qquad (\mathbf P,\overline{\mathbf Q})=\sum_{k=1}^{3}P_k\overline{Q_k}, $$

the complex bilinear dot product and the Hermitian dot product, with $\lvert\mathbf P\rvert^{2}=(\mathbf P,\overline{\mathbf P})$ a non-negative real. The natural conjugation is ${}^{\natural}$, the star is ${}^{*}={}^{\natural}\circ\bar{\cdot}$, and the bar is $\bar{\cdot}$. The product of the block is written $\diamond$; its value is $\mathbf{P}\times\overline{\mathbf{Q}}$. A root of a central value is written $\tilde\Xi$ (§The Root Convention of Conventions in Mathematics), a nilpotent $\tilde\Upsilon$ (§The Nilpotent Convention), and a generic element $\tilde P$, $\tilde Q$ or $\tilde X$.

The Roots of Zero

The roots of $0$ are exactly the square-zero elements of the block: the element $\tilde X=X_0e_0+\mathbf X$ satisfies $\tilde X\diamond\tilde X=0$ exactly when its vector part is a complex multiple of a real vector, $\mathbf X=\lambda\mathbf v$ with $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$ and $\mathbf v$ a real vector; the set is a cone of real dimension six, its scalar coordinate is free, it is the sum of the central family and the non-central family, and it is a strict part of the zero-divisor set of the block.

The roots of $0$ of the block are the elements satisfying $\tilde X\diamond\tilde X=0$. The following lemma restates the general definition for the product in force here; the vector $\mathbf X$ is the vector part of $\tilde X$ and the cross product is the complex bilinear one.

Lemma (the square-zero criterion). Let $\tilde X=X_0e_0+\mathbf X$ be a biquaternion. Then the three statements are equivalent:

  1. $\tilde X\diamond\tilde X=0$;
  2. $\mathbf X\times\overline{\mathbf X}=0$;
  3. $\mathbf X=\lambda\mathbf v$ for some $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$ and some $\mathbf v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$.

The scalar coordinate $X_0$ is free.

Proof. The value of the block is a pure vector and its scalar coordinate is zero, so $\tilde X\diamond\tilde X=0$ is the single vector equation $\mathbf X\times\overline{\mathbf X}=0$, which is the equivalence of (1) and (2). For the equivalence of (2) and (3), write $\mathbf X=\mathbf a+i\mathbf b$ with $\mathbf a,\mathbf b$ real. The conjugate cross product expands as

$$ \mathbf X\times\overline{\mathbf X}=(\mathbf a+i\mathbf b)\times(\mathbf a-i\mathbf b)=-2i\,\mathbf a\times\mathbf b , $$

so the equation is $\mathbf a\times\mathbf b=0$, that is the two real vectors are linearly dependent. If both vanish then $\mathbf X=0$, and $\lambda=0$ with any real $\mathbf v$ exhibits the form; otherwise let $\mathbf v$ be a nonzero real vector of the real line they span and write $\mathbf a=r\mathbf v$, $\mathbf b=s\mathbf v$ with real $r,s$, so that $\mathbf X=(r+is)\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v$ with $\lambda=r+is$ and $\mathbf v$ real. The converse is immediate, since $\lambda\mathbf v\times\overline{\lambda\mathbf v}=\lvert\lambda\rvert^{2}\,\mathbf v\times\mathbf v=0$. The scalar coordinate never enters, which is the last statement.

Remark (the equation read as a system). Read on the two real vectors, the equation is the system of three real equations

$$ \mathbf a\times\mathbf b=0 , $$

that is the vanishing of the three minors of the two real vectors, and its solution set is the union of the vanishing of $\mathbf a$ and of the pairs $\mathbf b=t\mathbf a$ with $t\in\mathbb{R}$; the solution set has real dimension four, and the root set of $0$ adds to it the free complex scalar coordinate, of real dimension two, giving the count of real dimension six below.

The Two Families

The root set of $0$ is the sum of two families, and every root is the sum of one element of each. The central family is the set of the complex multiples of the unit of the parent algebra, the elements $\lambda e_0$ with $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$, and it is exactly the intersection of the root set with the centre. The non-central family is the set of the complex multiples of the real directions, the elements $\lambda\mathbf v$ with $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$ and $\mathbf v$ a nonzero real vector, and it is the cone carried by the vector part. The two families meet at the origin alone, and a root with both a nonzero scalar coordinate and a nonzero vector part lies in neither: the root set is the sum of the two families and not their union.

Proposition (the two families and the planes over the real directions). The root set of $0$ of the block is the set of the sums $\lambda e_0+\mu\mathbf v$ with $\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}$ and $\mathbf v$ a real direction, that is the union, over the real directions $\mathbf v$, of the complex planes $\mathbb{C}e_0\oplus\mathbb{C}\mathbf v$; its two pieces are the central line $\mathbb{C}e_0$ and the cone $\{\mu\mathbf v:\mu\in\mathbb{C},\ \mathbf v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\}$ of the complex multiples of the real directions, and the pieces meet at the origin alone.

Proof. An element of the root set is $\tilde X=X_0e_0+\mathbf X$ with $\mathbf X=\lambda\mathbf v$ by the lemma, and its scalar coordinate is free, so it is the sum of the central element $X_0e_0$ and the non-central element $\lambda\mathbf v$: the root set is contained in the sum of the two families, and it contains that sum because every vector part of the cone is a root of $0$ on its own and adding a central element to a root of $0$ leaves a root of $0$. The sum $\lambda e_0+\mu\mathbf v$ with $\mu\mathbf v\neq0$ lies in the plane spanned by $e_0$ and $\mathbf v$, and the planes of two independent real directions meet along the central line, so the union of the planes over the real directions is the sum of the two families and nothing more. The two families meet only at the origin: a central element with $\lambda\neq0$ has a vanishing vector part and is not non-central, and a non-central element has a vanishing scalar coordinate and is not central.

Remark (the two families and the two products). The two families are the two elementary readings of the criterion: $e_1$ and $ie_1$ are roots of $0$ in the non-central family, since $e_1\times e_1=0$ and $(ie_1)\times\overline{(ie_1)}=0$, and $\lambda e_0$ is a root of $0$ in the central family for every complex $\lambda$. The element $e_1+ie_2$ is the elementary element outside the root set: its square is $-2ie_3$, nonzero. The cone of the diagonal is the fibre of the diagonal at the origin, and it is not the image of the diagonal: the image is the three real dimensional subspace $i\mathbb{R}^{3}$, whose reading is Square Roots of a General Element in the Antisymmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra.

The Parent Unit is a Root of Zero

The unit of the parent algebra is a root of $0$ of the block, in the central family: $e_0\diamond e_0=0$, so the unit of the four general products and of the two algebras is a square-zero element of the block, and the whole central line is annihilated by the block in both slots.

The parent unit is a root of $0$. The sharpest element of the central family is $e_0$: the unit of the parent algebra satisfies $e_0\diamond e_0=0$ in the block, so the object that is a unit and an idempotent for the four general products is a square-zero element of the block. The whole central line shares the reading, $\lambda e_0\diamond\lambda e_0=0$ for every complex $\lambda$, and the central line is annihilated by the block in both slots; this is the element-theoretic form of the absence of a unit for the block, and it is recorded again in Norm and Invertibility in the Antisymmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra.

Remark (the central family and the centre of the block). The central family of the root set of $0$ is exactly the intersection of the root set with the centre. The block has no other central element of interest: no scalar is an idempotent and no scalar is a unit, so the central line is the family of the block that is annihilated rather than multiplied. The reading is the counterpart, on the side of $0$, of the emptiness of the roots of $+1$: the central line is sent to the origin by the diagonal of the block.

The Roots of Zero and the Diagonal

The roots of $0$ of the block are a strict part of its zero-divisor set: every root of $0$ is a two-sided zero divisor and is its own annihilating partner, and the whole space with the origin removed is a zero-divisor set much larger than the cone of the diagonal.

Every root of $0$ is a zero divisor. Let $\tilde X$ be a nonzero root of $0$. Then $\tilde X\diamond\tilde X=0$, so $\tilde X$ annihilates itself: the same element serves as the left and as the right witness, and $\tilde X$ is a two-sided zero divisor and its own annihilating partner. The zero-divisor set of the block is the whole space with the origin removed, so the cone of the diagonal is a strict part of it; the comparison is Zero Divisors of the Antisymmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra.

Remark (the root set and the index). The roots of $0$ are exactly the square-zero elements of the block, hence exactly the nilpotents of index two; the block also carries nilpotents of index four, so the root set of $0$ is strictly smaller than the nilpotent class, and the two sets are the two layers of §The Nilpotents. The square-zero sets of the block and of the four general products are different sets, and the block's set meets those of the general plain product and of the general plain sesquilinear product at the origin alone, while it meets those of the general quaternionic product and of the general quaternionic sesquilinear product in the family of the elements $\pm i\lambda e_0+\lambda\mathbf v$ with $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$ and $\mathbf v$ a unit real direction; the comparison of the four is Nilpotents in the 12 Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space, and the zero-divisor sets are Zero Divisors in the 12 Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space.

The Nilpotents

The nilpotents of the block form two classes and no third: the square-zero elements, of index two, whose vector part is a complex multiple of a real vector, and the elements of index four, whose vector part is isotropic for the complex bilinear square without being a complex multiple of a real vector; the remaining elements have no vanishing power. The index is therefore two or four, never three, and it depends on the bracketing of the powers.

The nilpotent class of the block is the set of the elements some left-nested power of which vanishes; the definition is the general one, restated below for the product in force. The block is one of the members of the twelve in which the class has an index above two: the model block has index two or nothing, the two plain sesqualgebras carry nilpotents of index three, and the block has index two and index four.

The Definition, Relative to a Product

Definition (nilpotent of a product). Let $\star$ be a product on $\mathbb{B}$ and let $\tilde X\in\mathbb{B}$. The left-nested powers of $\tilde X$ are $\tilde X^{\star1}=\tilde X$ and $\tilde X^{\star(k+1)}=\tilde X^{\star k}\star\tilde X$, the nesting on the left of the product. The element $\tilde X$ is a nilpotent of $\star$ when $\tilde X^{\star k}=0$ for some $k\geq1$, and the least such $k$ is its index of nilpotency; an element of index two is a square-zero element of $\star$. The origin is the nilpotent of index one, the trivial one, and the others are the nonzero nilpotents. The definition is the one of Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures, §The Nilpotent, read with §Powers, the Square and the Roots.

Throughout this article the product is the block $\diamond$, so that, unqualified, a nilpotent is a nilpotent of the block, its powers are the left-nested powers of the block, and its index is the index of the block.

Remark (the powers depend on the bracketing, and the corpus fixes one). For an associative product the bracketing of a power is immaterial; the block is not associative, and its powers depend on the bracketing, as the witness $e_1+ie_2$ shows below, whose left-nested powers vanish at the fourth and whose right-nested powers never vanish. The corpus fixes the left-nested bracketing, the nesting on the left, so that, unqualified, the $k$-th power of the block is the left-nested one; the right-nested powers, the nesting on the right, are a reading of their own, and they are given for the witness as a warning. The diagonal of the block vanishes identically, so the equation of the class is a single vector equation, as the recurrence below records.

The Recurrence of the Left-Nested Powers

The left-nested powers of the block satisfy $\mathbf u_{k+2}=-\overline{c}\,\mathbf u_k$ for $k\geq2$, with $\mathbf u_k$ the vector parts, $c=(\mathbf X,\mathbf X)$ the complex bilinear square and $\overline{c}$ its conjugate; the third power is the exceptional seat of the recurrence, and the scalar coordinate is free throughout.

Lemma (the powers of the block). Let $\tilde X=X_0e_0+\mathbf X$ be a biquaternion, let $c=(\mathbf X,\mathbf X)$ be the complex bilinear square of its vector part and let $\lvert\mathbf X\rvert^{2}=(\mathbf X,\overline{\mathbf X})$. The left-nested powers of $\tilde X$ have vanishing scalar part and vector parts

$$ \mathbf u_1=\mathbf X,\qquad \mathbf u_2=\mathbf X\times\overline{\mathbf X},\qquad \mathbf u_3=\overline{\mathbf X}\lvert\mathbf X\rvert^{2}-\overline{c}\,\mathbf X, $$

and, for $k\geq2$,

$$ \mathbf u_{k+2}=-\overline{c}\,\mathbf u_k . $$

Proof. The scalar coordinate never enters a value of the block, so every power is a pure vector with vector part $\mathbf u_k$, and $\mathbf u_1=\mathbf X$. Write $\mathbf Y=\overline{\mathbf X}$ for the conjugate of the vector part, so that the left-nested powers read $\mathbf u_{k+1}=\mathbf u_k\times\mathbf Y$ and the complex bilinear square of $\mathbf Y$ is the conjugate of $c$. The second power is the square, $\mathbf u_2=\mathbf X\times\mathbf Y$. For the third, the identity $\mathbf a\times(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)=\mathbf b(\mathbf a,\mathbf c)-\mathbf c(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)$ for the complex bilinear dot product, applied with $\mathbf a=\mathbf X$ and $\mathbf b=\mathbf c=\mathbf Y$, gives $\mathbf u_3=(\mathbf X\times\mathbf Y)\times\mathbf Y=\mathbf Y(\mathbf X,\mathbf Y)-\mathbf X(\mathbf Y,\mathbf Y)=\overline{\mathbf X}\lvert\mathbf X\rvert^{2}-\overline{c}\,\mathbf X$. For the recurrence, apply the same identity to $\mathbf u_{k+2}=(\mathbf u_k\times\mathbf Y)\times\mathbf Y$, which gives $\mathbf u_{k+2}=\mathbf Y(\mathbf u_k,\mathbf Y)-\mathbf u_k(\mathbf Y,\mathbf Y)$; the first term vanishes for $k\geq2$, because a value of the block is orthogonal to $\mathbf Y$: $(\mathbf u_{k+1},\mathbf Y)=(\mathbf u_k\times\mathbf Y,\mathbf Y)=0$, the scalar triple product of two equal vectors, and the second term is $-\mathbf u_k(\mathbf Y,\mathbf Y)=-\overline{c}\,\mathbf u_k$.

Remark (the third power is the exceptional seat). The recurrence is an identity from the fourth power on, and the third power carries the extra term $\overline{\mathbf X}\lvert\mathbf X\rvert^{2}$, which vanishes only at the origin. The third power is therefore not of the form $-\overline{c}\,\mathbf u_1$: on the isotropic cone, where $c=0$ and the recurrence would make every even step vanish from the second on, the third power is the whole of that extra term, $\mathbf u_3=\overline{\mathbf X}\lvert\mathbf X\rvert^{2}$, and it does not vanish; it is the value the fourth power then annihilates. The displayed third power is written in the two elements $e_1$ and $e_2$ in the remark on the bracketing below.

Corollary (the scalar coordinate is free). An element and its translate by a central element have the same powers and the same index: the scalar coordinate never enters a value of the block, so a root of $0$ remains a root of $0$ after the addition of any complex scalar, and a nilpotent remains a nilpotent of the same index. The central line is therefore a free parameter of the whole class, and the classification below is a classification of the vector parts.

The Index of Nilpotency

The index of a nilpotent of the block takes the values two and four and never three. The index is two exactly when the vector part is a complex multiple of a real vector, that is exactly when the element is a root of $0$, and the class of index two is the cone of the diagonal, of real dimension six. The index is four exactly when the vector part is isotropic for the complex bilinear square without being a complex multiple of a real vector, and the class of index four is the isotropic cone of the vector subspace lifted over a free scalar coordinate. The remaining elements have no vanishing power.

Theorem (the index is two or four, never three). Let $\tilde X=X_0e_0+\mathbf X$ be a nonzero biquaternion. Then exactly one of the three holds.

  1. If $\mathbf X$ is a complex multiple of a real vector, then $\tilde X$ is a square-zero element of the block, of index two.
  2. If $\mathbf X$ is not a complex multiple of a real vector and $(\mathbf X,\mathbf X)=0$, then $\tilde X$ is a nilpotent of the block of index four.
  3. If $\mathbf X$ is not a complex multiple of a real vector and $(\mathbf X,\mathbf X)\neq0$, then no left-nested power of $\tilde X$ vanishes, and $\tilde X$ is not a nilpotent.

Proof. Write $c=(\mathbf X,\mathbf X)$. The first case is the criterion of §The Roots of Zero: $\mathbf X$ a complex multiple of a real vector is exactly $\mathbf u_2=0$, so the index is two.

In the second and third cases $\mathbf u_2\neq0$. If $c=0$, the recurrence of the lemma gives $\mathbf u_{k+2}=-\overline{c}\,\mathbf u_k=0$ for $k\geq2$, so $\mathbf u_4=0$ and the index is four, provided that the powers before the fourth do not vanish: $\mathbf u_1=\mathbf X\neq0$, $\mathbf u_2\neq0$, and $\mathbf u_3=\overline{\mathbf X}\lvert\mathbf X\rvert^{2}-\overline{c}\,\mathbf X=\overline{\mathbf X}\lvert\mathbf X\rvert^{2}$, which is nonzero because $\lvert\mathbf X\rvert^{2}=(\mathbf X,\overline{\mathbf X})$ is a sum of squared moduli and vanishes only for $\mathbf X=0$, excluded here by $\mathbf u_2\neq0$. So the index is exactly four.

If $c\neq0$, the recurrence reads $\mathbf u_{k+2}=-\overline{c}\,\mathbf u_k$ for every $k\geq2$, so the sequence of the vector parts from the second on is geometric with ratio $-\overline{c}\neq0$, and none of its terms vanishes: $\mathbf u_k\neq0$ for every $k\geq2$, and $\mathbf u_1=\mathbf X\neq0$ as well, so no power vanishes and the element is not a nilpotent.

Corollary (the two classes and their dimensions). The nilpotents of the block are the union of the cone of the diagonal and of the isotropic cone of the vector subspace lifted over a free scalar coordinate; the vector part of the second runs over the isotropic cone, of real dimension four, and the free scalar coordinate adds the two real dimensions of the central line to each class, so that the two cones are of real dimension six. The class of index four is the set of the elements $\tilde X=X_0e_0+\mathbf X$ with $\mathbf X\neq0$ isotropic and not a complex multiple of a real vector, with $X_0$ free; no nonzero isotropic vector part is a complex multiple of a real vector, so the class is the isotropic cone with the origin removed, lifted over the central line.

Remark (the complementarity of the two conditions). The two conditions of the theorem are complementary and neither implies the other. The condition $(\mathbf X,\mathbf X)=0$ is not the condition that $\mathbf X$ be a complex multiple of a real vector: the element $e_1+ie_2$ is isotropic, $(e_1+ie_2,e_1+ie_2)=1-1=0$, without being such a multiple, and it is the elementary element of the class of index four; on the other hand the vector $e_1+2ie_2$ is not a complex multiple of a real vector and is not isotropic, $(e_1+2ie_2,e_1+2ie_2)=1-4=-3\neq0$, so it is not a nilpotent. The two conditions therefore cut the vector subspace into the cone of the diagonal and the isotropic cone off it, and the complement of their union is the class of the elements with no vanishing power.

Remark (the index and the bracketing). The index of the block is a property of the left-nested bracketing. The left-nested powers of the witness are $e_1+ie_2$, $-2ie_3$, $2(e_1-ie_2)$ and then zero, and the factor $2$ of the third term is the factor $\lvert\mathbf X\rvert^{2}=2$ of the element. The right-nested powers of the same witness are

$$ e_1+ie_2,\quad -2ie_3,\quad -2(e_1+ie_2),\quad 4ie_3,\quad 4(e_1+ie_2),\quad -8ie_3,\ \dots $$

and never vanish: the alternation returns to the direction of the element with the doubled magnitude at every other step, and no term is zero. The same element therefore has index four under one bracketing and no index at all under the other.

The Two Classes of Nilpotents

The two classes are the square-zero elements and the elements of index four; the class of index two is the cone of the diagonal, whose vector part is a complex multiple of a real vector, and the class of index four is the isotropic cone of the vector subspace off that cone, with a free scalar coordinate in both cases. Every nilpotent is a zero divisor, no nilpotent is an idempotent, and the square-zero set of the block meets the square-zero sets of the four general products only in the family of the elements $\pm i\lambda e_0+\lambda\mathbf v$ with $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$ and $\mathbf v$ a unit real direction.

The class of index two. The class of index two is the root set of $0$, computed in §The Roots of Zero: the cone of the diagonal, of real dimension six, containing the central family and the non-central family. Its elementary elements are $e_1$ and $ie_1$, and the parent unit $e_0$ belongs to it, in the central family. This is the only class of the block that the model block also has: the two sets differ, the scalar coordinate being free in the block and zero in the model, but the index is two in both.

The class of index four. The class of index four is the set of the elements whose vector part is isotropic without being a complex multiple of a real vector, lifted over a free scalar coordinate. Its elementary element is $e_1+ie_2$, the witness of the whole classification: its left-nested powers are $e_1+ie_2$, $-2ie_3$, $2(e_1-ie_2)$ and then zero, and its right-nested powers never vanish. The class is the isotropic cone of the vector subspace off the cone of the diagonal, and it is the class that the block has and the model block does not.

Remark (the index of the model block). The model block, the general plain product, has index two or nothing; its square-zero elements are the isotropic elements of its vector part, and it carries no nilpotent of a higher index. The block of the section is the member of the twelve in which the index reaches four, and the reading is the exact antisymmetric counterpart of the reading of the roots of $+1$, which the model block has and the block does not: the two products exchange one existence for the other.

Remark (the two classes and the square-zero sets of the other products). The square-zero set of the block is the cone of the diagonal, and the square-zero sets of the four general products are different sets; the isotropic cone of the vector subspace, which is the vector part of the class of index four, is not the square-zero set of the block, and the cone of the diagonal and the isotropic cone of the vector subspace meet at the origin alone. The block's set and the sets of the four general products meet only in the family of the elements $\pm i\lambda e_0+\lambda\mathbf v$ with $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$ and $\mathbf v$ a unit real direction: that family is the whole intersection with the cone of the general quaternionic product and with the proper subfamily of the general quaternionic sesquilinear product, while the pure isotropic cone of the general plain product and the origin-alone set of the general plain sesquilinear product meet the block's set at the origin alone. The comparison of the four is Nilpotents in the 12 Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space.

The Conjugations and the Star

The star preserves the classes of the block and negates its values, $(\tilde X^{*})\diamond(\tilde Y^{*})=-(\tilde X\diamond\tilde Y)^{*}$; the natural conjugation is a skew-automorphism, $(\tilde X^{\natural})\diamond(\tilde Y^{\natural})=\tilde X\diamond\tilde Y=-(\tilde X\diamond\tilde Y)^{\natural}$, and preserves the classes; and the bar is the true automorphism, $(\overline{\tilde X})\diamond(\overline{\tilde Y})=\overline{\tilde X\diamond\tilde Y}$.

Proposition (the three symmetries of the product). For all biquaternions $\tilde X,\tilde Y$:

  1. $(\overline{\tilde X})\diamond(\overline{\tilde Y})=\overline{\tilde X\diamond\tilde Y}$;
  2. $(\tilde X^{*})\diamond(\tilde Y^{*})=-(\tilde X\diamond\tilde Y)^{*}$;
  3. $(\tilde X^{\natural})\diamond(\tilde Y^{\natural})=\tilde X\diamond\tilde Y=-(\tilde X\diamond\tilde Y)^{\natural}$.

Proof. The bar is an automorphism of the algebra and the natural conjugation is an anti-automorphism of it; applying them to the two slots of $\mathbf X\times\overline{\mathbf Y}$ gives the three identities, the second with the negative sign produced by the conjugate-linearity of the second slot composed with the star, and the third from the fact that every value of the block is a pure vector, which the natural conjugation negates.

Remark (the classes and the signs). The bar is the only one of the three with the sign $+1$ on the product; the star and the natural conjugation both carry the sign $-1$, and all three preserve the classes of the nilpotents: a power of a conjugated element is the conjugate of the same power, with the possible sign produced by the identity, and the vanishing is therefore preserved in every case. The two bracketings are the reading of the sign $+1$ against the sign $-1$: the sign absorbed by the left-nested bracketing at each step is not absorbed by the right-nested one, whose powers of the witness grow in magnitude, $2,2,4,4,8,8,\dots$, instead of vanishing.

Remark (the star and the classes). The star, the natural conjugation and the bar all have period two and all preserve the square-zero set and the class of index four: each sends a nilpotent to a nilpotent of the same index, and each sends a non-nilpotent to a non-nilpotent. The star is the reading of the block that pairs a class with itself and fixes none of its elements; the natural conjugation fixes the real directions, and it sends the class of index four into itself. The three symmetries are recorded in the same sense in the sibling articles of the twelve, and their reading against the diagonal is The Conjugate Cross Product and the Jacobi Failure of the Antisymmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra.

The Relation to the Zero Divisors, to the Idempotents and to the General Products

Every nilpotent of the block is a zero divisor and every nonzero root of $0$ is its own annihilating partner; no nilpotent of the block is an idempotent, and the nilpotents and the idempotents meet at the origin alone; and the nilpotent class of the block is a proper part of its zero-divisor set, the block being one of the nine structures of the twelve in which the nilpotents do not generate the zero divisors.

Every nilpotent is a zero divisor. A nonzero nilpotent $\tilde\Upsilon$ satisfies $\tilde\Upsilon^{k}=0$ for some $k\geq2$, and the annihilation of the highest power is a mutual annihilation of two nonzero elements: the power $\tilde\Upsilon^{k-1}$ is nonzero, and $\tilde\Upsilon\diamond\tilde\Upsilon^{k-1}=\tilde\Upsilon^{k}=0$, with the reading on the other side by the conjugate-alternation of the block. A nilpotent is therefore a two-sided zero divisor and the partner of its own power; in the class of index two the partner is the element itself, $\tilde\Upsilon\diamond\tilde\Upsilon=0$, and in the class of index four the partner is the third power.

No nilpotent is an idempotent. The two classes are disjoint: an idempotent $\tilde\Pi$ satisfies $\tilde\Pi^{k}=\tilde\Pi\neq0$ for every $k\geq1$, since every power of an idempotent is the idempotent itself, and a nilpotent has a vanishing power, so no nonzero element is both. Here the block has one idempotent, the origin, and the intersection of the two classes is the origin alone; the statement is therefore the general one read on the block, and it is recorded in Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the Antisymmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra.

The nilpotents do not generate the zero divisors. The zero-divisor set of the block is the whole space with the origin removed, and the nilpotent class is the cone of the diagonal together with the isotropic cone of the vector subspace off it, a strict part of it: the block is one of the nine structures of the twelve in which the square-zero set is strictly smaller than the zero-divisor set. The reading is Zero Divisors of the Antisymmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra, and the comparison of the twelve is Zero Divisors in the 12 Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space.

Remark (the classes of the row and of the parent). The block is the antisymmetric part of the general quaternionic sesquilinear product, and the two square-zero sets are different: the set of the parent is the proper subfamily $\overline{\tilde P}\tilde P=0$ of the cone, which contains $\tilde P=e_0+ie_1$ and misses $\tilde P=ie_0+e_1+e_2+ie_3$, while the set of the block is the cone of the diagonal, which contains the unit and is not contained in the cone; the two sets meet in the family of the elements $\pm i\lambda e_0+\lambda\mathbf v$ with $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$ and $\mathbf v$ a unit real direction, and outside that family they have no common element. The reading is the exact mirror of the reading of the roots of $+1$: the parent has the roots of $+1$, the unit among them, and the block has none; the block has the unit among its square-zero elements and the parent has not. The comparison of the twelve square-zero sets is Nilpotents in the 12 Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space.

The Matrix Model

In the matrix model the value of the block is a traceless matrix whose determinant is the complex bilinear square of the value, so the square-zero criterion is the vanishing of the determinant of the matrix of the square, equivalently the linear dependence of the two real vectors of the root's vector part, and the class of index four is the singular set of the matrix of the vector part, whose determinant is the complex bilinear square of that vector part.

The criterion in the model. The model is The Antisymmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra in the $2\times2$ Matrix Element Representation $M_2(\mathbb{C})$, and in it the value is the traceless matrix of the conjugate cross product, $\mathsf M_2(\tilde X\diamond\tilde X)=\mathsf M_2(-2i\,\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)$, whose determinant is the complex bilinear square of the value,

$$ \det\mathsf M_2\bigl(\tilde X\diamond\tilde X\bigr)=V_1^{2}+V_2^{2}+V_3^{2}=-4\,\lvert\mathbf a\times\mathbf b\rvert^{2}, $$

with $\mathbf V=\mathbf X\times\overline{\mathbf X}=-2i\,\mathbf a\times\mathbf b$. The determinant is a quadratic form read with the complex coefficients and not with their conjugates, and it vanishes exactly when the two real vectors $\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf b$ of the vector part of the root are linearly dependent, that is when the real $3\times2$ matrix with those two columns has rank at most one; the criterion of §The Roots of Zero is the vanishing of this determinant. It is not the Hermitian square, which is the sum of the squared moduli $\lvert V_1\rvert^{2}+\lvert V_2\rvert^{2}+\lvert V_3\rvert^{2}$ and vanishes only at the origin.

The classes in the model. The two classes are read from two determinants. The class of index two is the vanishing of the square itself, so its elements are those whose vector part is a complex multiple of a real direction, the determinant of the matrix of the square being zero there while the determinant of the matrix of the vector part is $c=(\mathbf X,\mathbf X)$ and need not vanish: the witness $e_1$ has $c=1$. The class of index four is the singular set of the matrix of the vector part, $\det\mathsf M_2(\mathbf X)=X_1^{2}+X_2^{2}+X_3^{2}=c=0$, on which the square is nonzero; a traceless $2\times2$ matrix of vanishing determinant has rank one, its two columns proportional with a complex factor, and the class of index four is that rank-one set off the complex multiples of the real directions, lifted over the free scalar coordinate, the witness $e_1+ie_2$ carrying the rank-one matrix and the square $-2ie_3$. The square-zero class is a complex cone over the real directions of the vector subspace, of real dimension four before the free scalar coordinate and of real dimension six with it; the parent unit belongs to it, in the central family, its vector part being zero while its scalar coordinate is one; and the class of index four is the cone off the square-zero class of the model.

Summary

The roots of $0$ of the block are exactly its square-zero elements: the element $\tilde X=X_0e_0+\mathbf X$ satisfies $\tilde X\diamond\tilde X=0$ exactly when its vector part is a complex multiple of a real vector, $\mathbf X=\lambda\mathbf v$ with $\lambda$ complex and $\mathbf v$ real, that is exactly when the two real vectors $\mathbf a,\mathbf b$ of $\mathbf X=\mathbf a+i\mathbf b$ are dependent. The set is a cone of real dimension six, whose scalar coordinate is free; it is the sum of the central family of the complex multiples of the parent unit and of the non-central family of the complex multiples of the real directions, which meet at the origin alone; every nonzero root of $0$ is a two-sided zero divisor and its own annihilating partner; and it is a strict part of the zero-divisor set, which is the whole space with the origin removed.

The nilpotents of the block form two classes and no third. The square-zero elements are the roots of $0$, of index two, the cone of the diagonal of real dimension six, containing the parent unit $e_0$; the elements of index four are those whose vector part is isotropic for the complex bilinear square without being a complex multiple of a real vector, lifted over a free scalar coordinate, with the elementary witness $e_1+ie_2$, whose left-nested powers are $e_1+ie_2$, $-2ie_3$, $2(e_1-ie_2)$ and then zero. The remaining elements have no vanishing power and are not nilpotents. The index is therefore two or four, never three, and it depends on the bracketing of the powers, the right-nested reading of the witness never vanishing. The whole reading comes from the recurrence $\mathbf u_{k+2}=-\overline{c}\,\mathbf u_k$ of the left-nested powers, with $c=(\mathbf X,\mathbf X)$ and $k\geq2$; the scalar coordinate is free throughout, every nilpotent is a zero divisor, no nilpotent is an idempotent, and the square-zero set of the block meets the square-zero sets of the four general products only in the family of the elements $\pm i\lambda e_0+\lambda\mathbf v$ with $\lambda$ complex and $\mathbf v$ a unit real direction.
