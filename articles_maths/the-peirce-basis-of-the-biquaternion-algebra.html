The Peirce Basis of the Biquaternion Algebra

Introduction

A root of one gives the Peirce decomposition of the biquaternion algebra $\mathbb{B}$: the four lines are fixed by the root. A Peirce basis is more. It is a choice of one nonzero element in each line, with the multiplication rule of the lines. The two diagonal elements are forced by the idempotents. The two mixed elements are not: the root fixes each mixed line as a set, but it does not choose a point in it.

The extra datum is a nonzero element of one mixed line. Once it is chosen, the element of the other mixed line is forced by the product normalization. The remaining freedom is a nonzero complex scale, the gauge. So a basis is a root together with a gauge, and the gauge is not a property of the root.

The article proves that every root admits a basis, gives the construction from a root and a transverse vector, computes the coordinates and their product, states the gauge as a torsor, its stabiliser and its gauge fixings, proves that every basis of a fixed family is conjugate to the constructed one, and counts the real dimensions. The second root is treated in a remark at the end. No family is preferred. An example is given at the end.

Definition

Let $\tilde\Pi_{+},\tilde\Pi_{-}$ be a nondegenerate complete family, with root $\tilde\Xi=\tilde\Pi_{+}-\tilde\Pi_{-}$, and let $\mathbb{B}_{st}=\tilde\Pi_{s}\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_{t}$.

Definition. A Peirce basis relative to the family is a family $E_{st}\in\mathbb{B}_{st}$, one element for each pair $s,t\in\{+,-\}$, such that $$ E_{st}E_{uv}=\delta_{tu}E_{sv},\qquad E_{ss}=\tilde\Pi_{s}. $$ The diagonal elements are therefore $E_{++}=\tilde\Pi_{+}$ and $E_{--}=\tilde\Pi_{-}$. The mixed elements are nonzero, and they satisfy $$ E_{+-}E_{-+}=\tilde\Pi_{+},\qquad E_{-+}E_{+-}=\tilde\Pi_{-},\qquad E_{+-}^{2}=E_{-+}^{2}=0. $$

From the Root to a Basis

Theorem. Let $\tilde\Xi=i\vec u$ with $\vec u\cdot\vec u=1$. Let $\vec v$ be a complex vector with $$ \vec u\cdot\vec v=0,\qquad q=\vec v\cdot\vec v\ne0. $$ Put $$ \vec\Upsilon_{+-}=\vec v+i\,\vec u\times\vec v,\qquad \vec\Upsilon_{-+}=\frac{-\vec v+i\,\vec u\times\vec v}{4q}. $$ Then $$ E_{++}=\tilde\Pi_{+},\quad E_{+-}=\vec\Upsilon_{+-},\quad E_{-+}=\vec\Upsilon_{-+},\quad E_{--}=\tilde\Pi_{-} $$ is a Peirce basis. Such a $\vec v$ exists: the transverse plane is two-dimensional, and its isotropic vectors form two complex lines, so any other vector works.

Proof. Let $T=\vec u\times\vec v$. Since $\vec u\cdot\vec v=0$ and $\vec u\cdot\vec u=1$, $$ \vec u\cdot T=0,\qquad \vec v\cdot T=0,\qquad T\cdot T=q, $$ $$ \vec v\,T=q\vec u,\qquad T\vec v=-q\vec u,\qquad \vec u\,T=-\vec v,\qquad T\vec u=\vec v. $$ Then $$ \vec\Upsilon_{+-}^{2}=(\vec v+iT)^{2} =\vec v^{2}-T^{2}+i(\vec vT+T\vec v)=-q+q+0=0, $$ and similarly $\vec\Upsilon_{-+}^{2}=0$. For the product, $$ \vec\Upsilon_{+-}\vec\Upsilon_{-+} =\frac{(\vec v+iT)(-\vec v+iT)}{4q} =\frac{-\vec v^{2}+i(\vec vT-T\vec v)-T^{2}}{4q} =\frac{2q+2i q\vec u}{4q} =\tilde\Pi_{+}, $$ and the same computation with the signs exchanged gives $\vec\Upsilon_{-+}\vec\Upsilon_{+-}=\tilde\Pi_{-}$. A direct computation with the same relations gives the corner conditions $$ \tilde\Pi_{+}\vec\Upsilon_{+-}=\vec\Upsilon_{+-}=\vec\Upsilon_{+-}\tilde\Pi_{-},\qquad \tilde\Pi_{-}\vec\Upsilon_{-+}=\vec\Upsilon_{-+}=\vec\Upsilon_{-+}\tilde\Pi_{+}. $$ All other products vanish by the product rule of the lines. Hence the four elements form a Peirce basis.

What the construction uses. The root $\vec u$ fixes the two mixed lines. The vector $\vec v$ chooses a point in $\mathbb{B}_{+-}$ and, through the normalization, a point in $\mathbb{B}_{-+}$. The conditions are: $\vec v$ is transverse to $\vec u$, and $\vec v$ is not isotropic, $q\ne0$. These are conditions on the extra datum, not on the root. The root has a transverse plane, but it chooses no vector in it.

The gauge is the scale of $\vec v$. For $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}$, replace $\vec v$ by $\lambda\vec v$. Then $$ \vec\Upsilon_{+-}\mapsto\lambda\,\vec\Upsilon_{+-},\qquad \vec\Upsilon_{-+}\mapsto\lambda^{-1}\vec\Upsilon_{-+}. $$ So the same root and the same family carry a one-parameter family of bases. The parameter $\lambda$ is the gauge. The basis is determined by the root and by $\vec v$, modulo this scale. If $\vec v$ is normalized by $\vec v\cdot\vec v=1$, the scale is reduced to $\lambda=\pm1$; the unnormalized $\vec v$ is the object that carries the full gauge.

Coordinates

For $\tilde Q\in\mathbb{B}$ write $$ \tilde Q=Q_{++}\tilde\Pi_{+}+Q_{+-}E_{+-}+Q_{-+}E_{-+}+Q_{--}\tilde\Pi_{-}. $$ The four coefficients are the Peirce coordinates of $\tilde Q$. They are complex numbers, and they depend on the basis.

The product. If $\tilde Q'$ has coordinates $Q'_{st}$, the coordinates of $\tilde Q\tilde Q'$ are $$ \begin{aligned} Q''_{++}&=Q_{++}Q'_{++}+Q_{+-}Q'_{-+},\\ Q''_{+-}&=Q_{++}Q'_{+-}+Q_{+-}Q'_{--},\\ Q''_{-+}&=Q_{-+}Q'_{++}+Q_{--}Q'_{-+},\\ Q''_{--}&=Q_{-+}Q'_{+-}+Q_{--}Q'_{--}. \end{aligned} $$ This is the product rule of the lines written in coordinates.

The norm. A direct expansion gives $$ Q_{++}Q_{--}-Q_{+-}Q_{-+}=Q_{t}^{2}+Q_{x}^{2}+Q_{y}^{2}+Q_{z}^{2}, $$ where $\tilde Q=Q_{t}e_{t}+Q_{x}\vec e_{x}+Q_{y}\vec e_{y}+Q_{z}\vec e_{z}$. The left side is therefore independent of the basis, as it must be.

The Gauge

A change of basis of the same family. Let $E_{st}$ and $F_{st}$ be two bases of the same family. Each line is one-dimensional and the diagonal elements are fixed, so $$ F_{st}=c_{st}E_{st},\qquad c_{++}=c_{--}=1. $$ The product $F_{+-}F_{-+}=\tilde\Pi_{+}$ gives $c_{+-}c_{-+}=1$. Put $t=c_{-+}$. Then $$ F_{+-}=t^{-1}E_{+-},\qquad F_{-+}=tE_{-+},\qquad t\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}. $$ This is the gauge transformation. In the construction above, $t=\lambda^{-1}$.

The gauge group. The same transformation reads $$ E_{st}\longmapsto d_{s}d_{t}^{-1}E_{st},\qquad d_{+},d_{-}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}. $$ The pairs $(d_{+},d_{-})$ and $(\mu d_{+},\mu d_{-})$ agree on every line, because $(\mu d_{s})(\mu d_{t})^{-1}=d_{s}d_{t}^{-1}$. The gauge group is therefore $$ G=(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{2}/\mathbb{C}^{\times}\cong\mathbb{C}^{\times},\qquad t=d_{-}d_{+}^{-1}. $$ The quotient is by the scalars, which act trivially. The same definition applies to a system of matrix units for $n$ orthogonal idempotents in $M_{n}(\mathbb{C})$: $$ G=(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n}/\mathbb{C}^{\times}\cong(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n-1},\qquad E_{ij}\longmapsto d_{i}d_{j}^{-1}E_{ij}, $$ and for $n=2$ this is the group above. The dimension $n-1$ is the number of independent rescalings of the lines.

The bases form a torsor. Fix the family $F$ and let $\mathcal{B}(F)$ be the set of its Peirce bases. Then $\mathcal{B}(F)$ is a $G$-torsor: the action is free and transitive.

Free. If $d_{s}d_{t}^{-1}=1$ for all $s,t$, then $d_{+}=d_{-}$, and the pair is the diagonal class, the identity of $G$.

Transitive. For bases $E$ and $F$, write $F_{st}=c_{st}E_{st}$ as above and take $d_{+}=c_{+-}$, $d_{-}=1$. Then $d_{+}d_{-}^{-1}=c_{+-}$ and $d_{-}d_{+}^{-1}=c_{+-}^{-1}=c_{-+}$, so the action carries $E$ to $F$.

A torsor is not a group: it has no identity, because no basis is distinguished. The group $G$ is the gauge group; a choice of basis is a point of $\mathcal{B}(F)$. The root fixes the family and hence the torsor, but no point of it.

The stabiliser. The action is free, so the stabiliser of a basis in $G$ is trivial. In the unit group the same fact reads as follows. The units that fix the two idempotents are $$ \tilde U=a\tilde\Pi_{+}+b\tilde\Pi_{-},\qquad a,b\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}, $$ and conjugation by $\tilde U$ acts on the basis by $$ E_{+-}\longmapsto\frac{b}{a}E_{+-},\qquad E_{-+}\longmapsto\frac{a}{b}E_{-+}. $$ The map $\tilde U\mapsto(a,b)$ is an isomorphism $U(F)\cong\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}$, and the centre is the diagonal $a=b$, so $$ G\cong U(F)/Z(\mathbb{B}^{\times}), $$ and the stabiliser of a basis in $U(F)$ is the centre. Two bases are therefore conjugate by a unit, with $t=a/b$, and the conjugating unit is unique up to the centre. This is the inner form of the torsor statement.

Coordinates under the gauge. The element is fixed, so $$ Q'_{st}=Q_{st}\,d_{t}d_{s}^{-1}, $$ that is, $$ Q'_{++}=Q_{++},\qquad Q'_{+-}=tQ_{+-},\qquad Q'_{-+}=t^{-1}Q_{-+},\qquad Q'_{--}=Q_{--}. $$ The product and the norm above are unchanged.

Gauge fixing. A gauge fixing is a section of the torsor: a rule that selects one basis in each family. Two remarks separate a condition on a basis from a choice of one.

  • The nonvanishing of the mixed elements and the product normalization $E_{+-}E_{-+}=\tilde\Pi_{+}$ are part of the definition of a basis. They do not fix $t$: the rescaled pair $(t^{-1}E_{+-},tE_{-+})$ is again a basis.
  • The Hermitian condition $E_{+-}^{*}=E_{-+}$, when the root is Hermitian, reduces the orbit to $|t|=1$. It restricts the gauge but does not fix it: a circle remains.

A point of the torsor requires an extra datum. In the construction above that datum is the transverse vector $\vec v$; the section is defined over the families together with a chosen transverse direction, not over the root alone. The gauge is not a symmetry of the algebra: it changes the coordinates, not the element and not the lines.

Dimensions

The count is $6=4+2$ in real dimensions.

A basis is a family together with a nonzero element of one mixed line. The family is a root modulo sign; a root is $\tilde\Xi=i\vec u$ with $\vec u\cdot\vec u=1$, which has two complex parameters, that is four real. The nonzero element of a complex line has two real parameters. Hence the bases have $$ 4+2=6 $$ real parameters.

The fiber over a family is the torsor $\mathcal{B}(F)$, whose group $G$ has one complex parameter, that is two real parameters. So $$ \underbrace{6}_{\text{bases}}=\underbrace{4}_{\text{families}}+\underbrace{2}_{\text{gauge}}. $$

A check against the construction. The root $\vec u$ has four real parameters. The transverse vector $\vec v$ modulo scale is a point of a complex projective line, of two real parameters. The pair $(\vec u,\vec v)$ modulo scale therefore has six real parameters. This is the same count.

Example: The Root $i\vec e_{z}$

This example is given at the end because it is one point in the four-real-parameter space of roots, not a preferred one.

For $\tilde\Xi=i\vec e_{z}$ take $\vec v=\vec e_{x}$. Then $q=1$ and the construction gives $$ \vec\Upsilon_{+-}=\vec e_{x}+i\vec e_{y},\qquad \vec\Upsilon_{-+}=\frac{-\vec e_{x}+i\vec e_{y}}{4}. $$ The rescalings $$ \vec\Upsilon_{1}=\frac{i}{2}\vec\Upsilon_{+-}=\frac{i\vec e_{x}-\vec e_{y}}{2},\qquad \vec\Upsilon_{2}=-2i\,\vec\Upsilon_{-+}=\frac{i\vec e_{x}+\vec e_{y}}{2} $$ give the pair often written down. It satisfies $$ \vec\Upsilon_{1}\vec\Upsilon_{2}=\tilde\Pi_{+},\qquad \vec\Upsilon_{2}\vec\Upsilon_{1}=\tilde\Pi_{-},\qquad \vec\Upsilon_{1}^{2}=\vec\Upsilon_{2}^{2}=0. $$ The rescaling is a gauge transformation with $t=-2i$.

Remark: The Second Root

A transverse root is a basis, not a second primitive. Call $\tilde\Xi'$ transverse to $\tilde\Xi$ when $$ \tilde\Xi\tilde\Xi'+\tilde\Xi'\tilde\Xi=0,\qquad \tilde\Xi'^{2}=e_{t}. $$ In a basis $E$ of the family of $\tilde\Xi$, every transverse root is $$ \tilde\Xi'=\tau E_{+-}+\tau^{-1}E_{-+},\qquad \tau\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}, $$ and conversely. The diagonal part of $\tilde\Xi'$ commutes with $\tilde\Xi$ and the mixed part anticommutes, so a transverse element is purely mixed; its square is $\tau\sigma e_{t}$, and $\tau\sigma=1$.

It is a point of the torsor. A transverse root determines a basis with no further choice: $$ E_{+-}=\frac12\left(\tilde\Xi'+\frac12[\tilde\Xi,\tilde\Xi']\right),\qquad E_{-+}=\frac12\left(\tilde\Xi'-\frac12[\tilde\Xi,\tilde\Xi']\right). $$ For $\tilde\Xi'=\tau E_{+-}+\tau^{-1}E_{-+}$ one has $\tfrac12[\tilde\Xi,\tilde\Xi']=\tau E_{+-}-\tau^{-1}E_{-+}$, so these give $\tau E_{+-}$ and $\tau^{-1}E_{-+}$, whose product is $\tilde\Pi_{+}$. The inverse map is $\tilde\Xi'=E_{+-}+E_{-+}$. Hence the transverse roots are in canonical bijection with the Peirce bases: a transverse root is exactly a point of the gauge torsor. The root gives the family and the torsor; the transverse root chooses the point, that is, the basis. It produces no new idempotents: those come from $\tilde\Xi$ alone.

In $M_{n}(\mathbb{C})$. A transverse root exists only when the two eigenvalues of $\tilde\Xi$ have equal multiplicity, so $n=2m$; for odd $n$ there is none. In blocks, $$ \tilde\Xi'=\begin{pmatrix}0&A\\A^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad A\in GL_{m}(\mathbb{C}), $$ and the transverse roots have $m^{2}$ complex parameters. For a family of $n$ idempotents the gauge has $n-1=2m-1$ parameters. The two counts agree only for $m=1$, that is $n=2$. For $n>2$ a transverse root does not determine a frame: it identifies the two blocks through $A$ but fixes no basis inside either block. The second root is therefore a phenomenon of dimension two. In higher dimension the torsor and its gauge group are the general notion, and no single element plays its role.

Summary

A Peirce basis of a nondegenerate family of $\mathbb{B}$ is a choice $E_{st}\in\mathbb{B}_{st}$ with $E_{st}E_{uv}=\delta_{tu}E_{sv}$ and $E_{ss}=\tilde\Pi_{s}$. The root fixes the four lines but not the mixed elements. To build a basis one adds a transverse vector $\vec v$, with $\vec u\cdot\vec v=0$ and $\vec v\cdot\vec v\ne0$; the formulas $\vec\Upsilon_{+-}=\vec v+i\vec u\times\vec v$ and $\vec\Upsilon_{-+}=(-\vec v+i\vec u\times\vec v)/(4\vec v\cdot\vec v)$ give the mixed elements, and the product normalization is then automatic. The scale of $\vec v$ is the gauge, $E'_{+-}=t^{-1}E_{+-}$ and $E'_{-+}=tE_{-+}$. The bases of a fixed family form a torsor under $G\cong\mathbb{C}^{\times}$; the action is free, so the stabiliser of a basis in $G$ is trivial, and in the unit group it is the centre. Nonvanishing and product normalization belong to the definition of a basis and leave $t$ free; the Hermitian condition restricts the gauge to $|t|=1$ but does not fix it. The coordinates multiply by the four-term rule, and the norm is $Q_{++}Q_{--}-Q_{+-}Q_{-+}$. Every basis of a fixed family is conjugate to the constructed one. The dimensions are $6=4+2$: six real for the bases, four for the families, two for the gauge. A second root is a point of the torsor, hence a gauge choice; a single such element exists only in dimension two.

Summary of Notation

Symbol Meaning
$\mathbb{B}$ The biquaternion algebra
$\tilde\Xi=i\vec u$ The root, with $\vec u\cdot\vec u=1$
$\tilde\Pi_{\pm}$ The complete family of two idempotents
$\mathbb{B}_{st}=\tilde\Pi_{s}\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_{t}$ A Peirce line
$E_{st}$ A Peirce basis, with $E_{ss}=\tilde\Pi_{s}$
$\vec\Upsilon_{+-},\vec\Upsilon_{-+}$ The mixed elements of a basis
$Q_{st}$ The Peirce coordinates of $\tilde Q$
$\mathcal{B}(F)$ The set of Peirce bases of the family $F$
$G=(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{2}/\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ The gauge group, a $\mathbb{C}^{\times}$
$t$ The gauge parameter, $t=d_{-}d_{+}^{-1}$
