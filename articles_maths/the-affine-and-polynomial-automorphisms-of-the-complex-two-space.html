The Affine and Polynomial Automorphisms of $\mathbb{C}^2$

Introduction

The group of biholomorphisms of $\mathbb{C}^2$ is enormous: it contains the affine group $\mathbb{C}^2\rtimes GL_2(\mathbb{C})$, the triangular maps $(Q_{\blacktriangle},Q_{\blacktriangledown})\mapsto(Q_{\blacktriangle},Q_{\blacktriangledown}+f(Q_{\blacktriangle}))$ for an arbitrary entire $f$, and many compositions of these. The affine group is therefore not the whole group, in contrast with the one-variable case, where every biholomorphism of $\mathbb{C}$ is affine. Inside the biholomorphism group sits the group of polynomial automorphisms, the polynomial maps with a polynomial inverse; in dimension two this group has a complete structure, the Jung–van der Kulk theorem, and it is generated by the affine and the elementary maps.

This article treats the affine group, the biholomorphisms of $\mathbb{C}^2$, the polynomial automorphisms and their Jacobian, the tame automorphisms and the Jung–van der Kulk theorem, the Nagata automorphism of $\mathbb{C}^3$ as the first wild example, and the Jacobian conjecture. The Hénon maps of The Hénon Maps are the polynomial automorphisms of degree two that generate the interesting dynamics; the affine group is also the group of the flat connection of The Flat Kähler Structure of $\mathbb{C}^2$.

The dividing line is between dimension two and dimension three. Every polynomial automorphism of $\mathbb{C}^2$ is tame, by the theorem of Jung and van der Kulk, while $\mathbb{C}^3$ has the wild Nagata automorphism. The Jacobian conjecture asks whether the polynomial maps with constant nonzero Jacobian are exactly the polynomial automorphisms, and it is open in dimension at least two.

The Affine Group

The affine group is the semidirect product $\mathbb{C}^2\rtimes GL_2(\mathbb{C})$; it is a subgroup of the biholomorphisms, of real dimension $12$, and it is the group of the affine biholomorphisms.

Definition. The affine group of $\mathbb{C}^2$ is $$ \operatorname{Aff}_2(\mathbb{C})=\mathbb{C}^2\rtimes GL_2(\mathbb{C}) =\left\{Q\longmapsto AQ+Q_0:A\in GL_2(\mathbb{C}),\ Q_0\in\mathbb{C}^2\right\}, $$ with the composition $(A,b)(A',b')=(AA',Ab'+b)$.

Proposition (the group). The affine group is a Lie group of complex dimension $6$ and real dimension $12$; the translations $\mathbb{C}^2$ form a normal subgroup, and the quotient is $GL_2(\mathbb{C})$.

Proof. The pair $(A,b)$ has $4+2=6$ complex parameters; the composition law is the one of the semidirect product, and the projection $(A,b)\mapsto A$ is a homomorphism with kernel the translations.

Proposition (the affine maps are biholomorphisms). Every element of $\operatorname{Aff}_2(\mathbb{C})$ is a biholomorphism of $\mathbb{C}^2$, with inverse $Q\mapsto A^{-1}(Q-Q_0)$.

Proof. The map is holomorphic, and the displayed inverse is holomorphic.

Remark (the affine group is not normal in the biholomorphism group). The affine group is not the whole group of biholomorphisms, and it is not normal in it: conjugating a translation by a nonlinear biholomorphism produces a nonlinear map. The affine group is the stabiliser of the flat connection and of the linear structure, and the isometry group of the flat Kähler metric, $\mathbb{C}^2\rtimes\mathrm{U}(2)$, is the subgroup of the affine group that preserves the Hermitian form (The Flat Kähler Structure of $\mathbb{C}^2$).

Remark (the affine group in one variable). In one variable the affine group $\mathbb{C}\rtimes\mathbb{C}^*$ is the whole group of biholomorphisms of $\mathbb{C}$: an injective entire function is affine. The failure of this statement in two variables is the beginning of the present article: the map $(Q_{\blacktriangle},Q_{\blacktriangledown})\mapsto(Q_{\blacktriangle},Q_{\blacktriangledown}+e^{Q_{\blacktriangle}})$ is a biholomorphism and is not affine.

The Biholomorphisms of $\mathbb{C}^2$

The biholomorphism group of $\mathbb{C}^2$ is infinite-dimensional; it contains the triangular maps with an arbitrary entire function, and it is much larger than the affine group.

Proposition (the triangular biholomorphisms). For every entire function $f$ of one variable, the map $$ F_f(Q_{\blacktriangle},Q_{\blacktriangledown})=(Q_{\blacktriangle},Q_{\blacktriangledown}+f(Q_{\blacktriangle})) $$ is a biholomorphism of $\mathbb{C}^2$, with inverse $F_{-f}$.

Proof. The map is holomorphic; the inverse is $(Q_{\blacktriangle},Q_{\blacktriangledown}-f(Q_{\blacktriangle}))$, which is holomorphic. The two compositions are the identity.

Proposition (the conjugate triangular maps). For every entire $g$, the map $$ G_g(Q_{\blacktriangle},Q_{\blacktriangledown})=(Q_{\blacktriangle}+g(Q_{\blacktriangledown}),Q_{\blacktriangledown}) $$ is a biholomorphism, with inverse $G_{-g}$.

Proof. The same computation with the roles of the variables exchanged.

Corollary (the group is infinite-dimensional). The biholomorphism group of $\mathbb{C}^2$ contains the infinite-dimensional family $f\mapsto F_f$, and it is not a finite-dimensional Lie group.

Proof. The maps $F_f$ are distinct for distinct $f$, and they depend on the infinitely many coefficients of the entire function $f$.

Remark (the one-variable contrast). In one variable the only biholomorphisms of $\mathbb{C}$ are the affine maps. In two variables the triangular maps are the simplest non-affine biholomorphisms, and they are the source of the richness of the group. The polynomial triangular maps, with $f$ a polynomial, are the elementary automorphisms of the next sections.

Remark (the Jacobian). The triangular map $F_f$ has Jacobian determinant $1$, because the matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\f'(Q_{\blacktriangle})&1\end{pmatrix}$. Every triangular map is volume preserving, and the family is a continuous family of biholomorphisms with the same Jacobian.

Polynomial Automorphisms and the Jacobian

A polynomial automorphism is a polynomial map with a polynomial inverse; its Jacobian determinant is a nonzero constant, and the Jacobian conjecture asks for the converse.

Definition. A polynomial automorphism of $\mathbb{C}^2$ is a map $F=(F_1,F_2):\mathbb{C}^2\to\mathbb{C}^2$ with $F_1,F_2\in\mathbb{C}[Q_{\blacktriangle},Q_{\blacktriangledown}]$ that has an inverse $F^{-1}$ whose components are also polynomials. The group of polynomial automorphisms is denoted $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{C}^2)$.

Proposition (the Jacobian is constant). If $F$ is a polynomial automorphism, then $$ \det JF\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}, $$ where $JF=\bigl(\partial F_i/\partial Q_j\bigr)$ is the complex Jacobian matrix.

Proof. The chain rule gives $JF(Q)\cdot JF^{-1}(F(Q))=I$, so $\det JF$ is a polynomial that never vanishes and whose inverse is a polynomial; hence it is a nonzero constant.

Proposition (the affine maps are polynomial automorphisms). Every affine map $Q\mapsto AQ+Q_0$ with $A\in GL_2(\mathbb{C})$ is a polynomial automorphism, with Jacobian determinant $\det A$.

Proof. The inverse is $Q\mapsto A^{-1}(Q-Q_0)$, whose components are polynomials of degree one.

Remark (polynomial automorphisms are rare among biholomorphisms). The triangular map $F_f$ is a polynomial automorphism exactly when $f$ is a polynomial. The polynomial automorphisms form a countable union of finite-dimensional complex constructible sets inside the infinite-dimensional biholomorphism group, and the inclusion is strict: $(Q_{\blacktriangle},Q_{\blacktriangledown})\mapsto(Q_{\blacktriangle},Q_{\blacktriangledown}+e^{Q_{\blacktriangle}})$ is a biholomorphism that is not polynomial.

Remark (the degree). The degree of a polynomial automorphism is the maximum of the total degrees of its components. The affine maps have degree one; the Hénon maps of The Hénon Maps have degree two. The degree is not multiplicative in general, but it grows under iteration, and the growth rate is the dynamical degree of the next article.

Tame Automorphisms and the Jung–van der Kulk Theorem

The tame automorphisms are the compositions of affine maps and elementary maps; in dimension two every polynomial automorphism is tame.

Definition. An elementary automorphism of $\mathbb{C}^2$ is a map of one of the two forms $$ (Q_{\blacktriangle},Q_{\blacktriangledown})\longmapsto(Q_{\blacktriangle}+f(Q_{\blacktriangledown}),Q_{\blacktriangledown}),\qquad (Q_{\blacktriangle},Q_{\blacktriangledown})\longmapsto(Q_{\blacktriangle},Q_{\blacktriangledown}+g(Q_{\blacktriangle})), $$ with $f,g\in\mathbb{C}[t]$, composed with a permutation of the coordinates. The tame group is the subgroup of $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{C}^2)$ generated by the affine group and the elementary automorphisms.

Proposition (the elementary maps are automorphisms). Every elementary map is a polynomial automorphism, with inverse obtained by replacing $f$ by $-f$.

Proof. The inverse of $(Q_{\blacktriangle}+f(Q_{\blacktriangledown}),Q_{\blacktriangledown})$ is $(Q_{\blacktriangle}-f(Q_{\blacktriangledown}),Q_{\blacktriangledown})$, and the inverse of $(Q_{\blacktriangle},Q_{\blacktriangledown}+g(Q_{\blacktriangle}))$ is $(Q_{\blacktriangle},Q_{\blacktriangledown}-g(Q_{\blacktriangle}))$; both are polynomial.

Theorem (Jung–van der Kulk). Every polynomial automorphism of $\mathbb{C}^2$ is tame: $$ \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{C}^2)=\operatorname{Tame}(\mathbb{C}^2). $$

Proof. This is the theorem of Jung (1942) and van der Kulk (1953). The proof uses the degree and the decomposition of a polynomial automorphism according to its action on the line at infinity: an automorphism of large degree has a nontrivial reduction, and the reduction is used to factor it into elementary and affine pieces. The group is the amalgamated free product of the affine group and the de Jonquières group over their intersection.

Remark (the structure of the group). The Jung–van der Kulk theorem gives more than the generation: it gives the amalgamated free product structure $$ \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{C}^2)=\operatorname{Aff}_2(\mathbb{C})\ast_{\operatorname{Aff}\cap\mathcal{J}}\mathcal{J}, $$ where $\mathcal{J}$ is the de Jonquières group of triangular automorphisms, and hence a normal form for every polynomial automorphism, a word in affine and triangular maps that is unique up to the relations of the intersection. The word length is a complexity measure, and the degree is comparable to it.

Remark (the triangular maps). The elementary maps are the triangular polynomial maps whose diagonal part is the identity, up to a permutation of the coordinates; a triangular map with an arbitrary linear diagonal part is an affine map composed with an elementary one. The affine maps are the degree-one automorphisms. The Hénon map is a polynomial automorphism of degree two, hence tame by the Jung–van der Kulk theorem.

Remark (the de Jonquières group). The group generated by the diagonal affine maps and the triangular maps $(Q_{\blacktriangle},Q_{\blacktriangledown}+f(Q_{\blacktriangle}))$ is the de Jonquières group; in dimension two it is a proper subgroup of the tame group, because the elementary maps in the other direction are not in it. The tame group is generated by the two de Jonquières groups, one for each direction, together with the permutation, and the Jung–van der Kulk theorem is the statement that the two triangular directions generate everything.

The Nagata Automorphism

In dimension three there are wild polynomial automorphisms; the Nagata automorphism is the standard example, and it is not tame.

Definition. The Nagata automorphism of $\mathbb{C}^3$ is $$ \sigma(x,y,z)=\bigl(x-2y\Delta-z\Delta^2,\ y+z\Delta,\ z\bigr),\qquad \Delta=xz+y^2. $$

Proposition (the Nagata map is an automorphism). The map $\sigma$ is a polynomial automorphism of $\mathbb{C}^3$ with Jacobian determinant $1$.

Proof. The inverse is obtained by reversing the two triangular steps: from $(X,Y,Z)=\sigma(x,y,z)$ one has $Z=z$, $Y=y+z\Delta$, $X=x-2y\Delta-z\Delta^2$, and the relation $\Delta=XZ+Y^2$ is preserved; solving gives $y=Y-Z(XZ+Y^2)$ and $x=X+2y\Delta+z\Delta^2$, which are polynomials. The Jacobian is computed directly and equals $1$.

Theorem (Nagata; Shestakov–Umirbaev). The Nagata automorphism is not tame: it is not a composition of affine and elementary automorphisms of $\mathbb{C}^3$.

Proof. The result was proved by Shestakov and Umirbaev in 2004, by the theory of the Poisson bracket and the stapled forms of the automorphism. It answered a question of Nagata from 1972. The proof is beyond the scope of this article; it is treated in Polynomial Automorphisms and the Jacobian Conjecture.

Remark (the dimension three phenomenon). The existence of a wild automorphism in dimension three and the absence in dimension two is the sharp contrast between the two cases. The Jung–van der Kulk theorem is a theorem of dimension two, and it fails in dimension three; the Nagata automorphism is the first obstruction.

Remark (the Jacobian of Nagata). The Nagata automorphism has Jacobian determinant $1$ and is wild; it is not a counterexample to the Jacobian conjecture, because it is invertible. The conjecture asks the converse: whether every polynomial map with constant nonzero Jacobian is invertible.

The Jacobian Conjecture

The Jacobian conjecture asserts that a polynomial map of $\mathbb{C}^n$ with constant nonzero Jacobian is a polynomial automorphism; it is open for $n\ge2$ and is the central open problem of the theory.

Conjecture (Jacobian). Let $F:\mathbb{C}^n\to\mathbb{C}^n$ be a polynomial map with $$ \det JF\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}. $$ Then $F$ is a polynomial automorphism.

Proposition (the one-variable case). The conjecture is true for $n=1$: a polynomial $P$ with $P'\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ is affine, hence invertible.

Proof. A polynomial with constant nonzero derivative is linear.

Status (the two-variable case is open). The Jacobian conjecture is open for $n=2$ and for every $n\ge2$. It is known in special cases: for maps of degree at most two (Wang), and for several families of maps with additional structure.

Proof. The conjecture is equivalent to the statement that the polynomial automorphism group of $\mathbb{C}^n$ is the set of polynomial maps with constant nonzero Jacobian. The known cases are obtained by degree estimates and by the theory of the Newton polygon; the general case remains open. The history and the partial results are in the monograph of van den Essen, Polynomial Automorphisms and the Jacobian Conjecture.

Remark (the equivalent forms). The conjecture has several equivalent forms: the Dixmier conjecture for the Weyl algebra, and the statement that the Jacobian map is injective on the automorphism group. The equivalence with the Dixmier conjecture is a theorem of Tsuchimoto and of Belov-Kanel–Kontsevich, and it is one of the reasons the conjecture is central.

Remark (the role of the Jacobian). A polynomial map with constant nonzero Jacobian is a local biholomorphism at every point, and it is a covering map onto its image if it is proper; the conjecture is the statement that the local invertibility implies the global polynomial invertibility. In one variable the local invertibility forces the degree one; in several variables the map can be a local biholomorphism and fail to be injective, and the conjecture says that this cannot happen when the Jacobian is a nonzero constant.

Remark (the Hénon maps). The Hénon maps of The Hénon Maps have constant Jacobian $a\neq0$, and they are polynomial automorphisms for every $a\neq0$; they are the degree-two examples of the conjecture's conclusion. The conjecture is not about the existence of invertible maps but about the converse, and the Hénon family shows that the invertible maps are abundant.

Summary

The affine group $\mathbb{C}^2\rtimes GL_2(\mathbb{C})$ is a subgroup of the biholomorphism group of $\mathbb{C}^2$, of real dimension $12$; it is not the whole group, because the triangular maps $(Q_{\blacktriangle},Q_{\blacktriangledown})\mapsto(Q_{\blacktriangle},Q_{\blacktriangledown}+f(Q_{\blacktriangle}))$ are biholomorphisms for every entire $f$. The polynomial automorphisms are the polynomial maps with polynomial inverse; their Jacobian determinant is a nonzero constant. The elementary automorphisms are the triangular polynomial maps, and the Jung–van der Kulk theorem states that in dimension two every polynomial automorphism is tame, with the amalgamated free product structure of the affine and de Jonquières groups.

In dimension three the Nagata automorphism is a polynomial automorphism of Jacobian $1$ that is not tame, by the theorem of Shestakov–Umirbaev. The Jacobian conjecture asserts that a polynomial map of $\mathbb{C}^n$ with constant nonzero Jacobian is a polynomial automorphism; it is true for $n=1$, open for $n\ge2$, and equivalent to the Dixmier conjecture for the Weyl algebra. The Hénon maps of the next article are the degree-two polynomial automorphisms whose iteration is the subject of the fractal geometry of $\mathbb{C}^2$.
