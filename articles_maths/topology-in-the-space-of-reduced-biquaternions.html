Topology in the Space of Reduced Biquaternions

Introduction

The reduced biquaternion algebra is a real four-dimensional vector space, and its Euclidean topology is the standard topology of $\mathbb{R}^{4}$ read on the coordinates. This article fixes the norm that produces it, states that the topology it defines is the only one compatible with the vector-space structure, records the metric, and records how the topology interacts with the algebra: the four general products are continuous, the three conjugations are isometries, the unit group is open and the zero-divisor set is closed. Of the four algebraic norms only the general quaternionic sesquilinear one, $\langle T\rangle_{\natural*}=\lvert A\rvert^{2}+\lvert B\rvert^{2}$, is positive definite, so only $\langle\cdot\rangle_{\natural*}$ yields a topological norm; the other three are complex-valued or indefinite and give at best a degenerate quantity. The multiplication has zero divisors and is not a division algebra, and the zero-divisor set is exactly the closed set where the unit group ends.

Conventions. The basis is $1,i,j,ij$ with $i^{2}=-1$, $j^{2}=+1$, $ji=ij$; the element is $T=A+Bj$ with $A=a+ia'$, $B=b+ib'$; the idempotent coordinates are $q_1=A+B$, $q_2=A-B$. The four general products and the forms $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle, \langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\natural}, \langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{*}, \langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\natural*}$ are The Four Products, Their Forms and Their Algebraic Norms; the Euclidean norm is $\lVert T\rVert=\sqrt{\langle T\rangle_{\natural*}}=\sqrt{a^{2}+a'^{2}+b^{2}+b'^{2}}$. The element glyph is $T$, without a tilde. The word algebraic norm names the diagonal of a form and is never the topological norm; only $\langle\cdot\rangle_{\natural*}$ is both.

Boundaries. The forms and their definiteness are The Forms of the General Plain Algebra of the Reduced Biquaternions; the zero divisors are Zero Divisors of the General Plain Algebra of the Reduced Biquaternions; the unit group is The Group of Units of the General Plain Algebra of the Reduced Biquaternions; the four-vector coordinates are The Four-Vector Element Representation of the Reduced Biquaternions. The companions of this article are The Euclidean Topology of the Reduced Biquaternion Algebra, for the metric and the unit sphere, and Topology and Metric for Each of the Operations, for the metric attached to each product. The biquaternion counterpart is Topology in the Space of Biquaternions.

The Euclidean Norm

The form $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\natural*}$ is positive definite, and its square root is the Euclidean norm.

Theorem (the norm). The map $$ \lVert T\rVert=\sqrt{\langle T\rangle_{\natural*}}=\sqrt{A\bar A+B\bar B}=\sqrt{a^{2}+a'^{2}+b^{2}+b'^{2}} $$ is a norm on $\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{r}}$: it is non-negative and vanishes only at $T=0$, it is homogeneous, $\lVert\lambda T\rVert=\lvert\lambda\rvert\lVert T\rVert$ for real $\lambda$, and it satisfies the triangle inequality.

Proof. $\langle T\rangle_{\natural*}=A\bar A+B\bar B$ is a sum of four squares of real numbers, non-negative and vanishing only when all four vanish, which is $T=0$; homogeneity is the homogeneity of a square root of a sum of squares under real scaling; and the square root of a positive definite quadratic form is the Euclidean norm of the coordinate quadruple, for which the triangle inequality is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.

Corollary (only $\langle\cdot\rangle_{\natural*}$ gives a topological norm). Of the four algebraic norms, only $\langle\cdot\rangle_{\natural*}$ is positive definite, so only $\langle\cdot\rangle_{\natural*}$ yields a topological norm; the complex-valued $\langle\cdot\rangle$ and $\langle\cdot\rangle_{\natural}$ and the indefinite $\langle\cdot\rangle_{*}$ do not. Their moduli $\lvert B\rvert,\lvert N\rvert,\lvert H\rvert$ are real and non-negative but vanish on non-zero elements, so no square root of them is a norm; the metric question is taken up in Topology and Metric for Each of the Operations.

Proof. $\langle\cdot\rangle$ and $\langle\cdot\rangle_{\natural}$ take complex values and vanish on the isotropic sets of the two bilinear forms; $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{*}$ is indefinite of signature $(2,2)$ and vanishes on its null set; a norm must vanish only at the origin, which none of the three does.

The Topological Norm: Equivalence and Uniqueness

All norms on the finite-dimensional space are equivalent, so the topology they give is unique.

Theorem (equivalence). Any two norms on $\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{r}}$ are equivalent: there are constants such that each is bounded by a constant multiple of the other, so they define the same open sets, the same convergent sequences and the same continuous functions.

Proof. On a finite-dimensional real vector space every norm is equivalent to the Euclidean one, by the compactness of the unit sphere; the space here is $\mathbb{R}^{4}$ through the coordinates $(a,a',b,b')$.

Corollary (the topology is the standard one). The topology of $\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{r}}$ is the standard topology of $\mathbb{R}^{4}$, independent of the norm chosen; it is metrisable, and the metric of $\langle\cdot\rangle_{\natural*}$, $$ d(T,W)=\lVert T-W\rVert=\sqrt{\langle T-W\rangle_{\natural*}}, $$ is one of the compatible metrics. The space is complete and locally compact.

Proof. The coordinate map $T\mapsto(a,a',b,b')$ is an isomorphism of real vector spaces onto $\mathbb{R}^{4}$, and it is a homeomorphism for the two Euclidean norms; the completeness and the local compactness are those of $\mathbb{R}^{4}$.

The Topology

Definition. The Euclidean topology of $\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{r}}$ is the topology induced by the norm $\lVert\cdot\rVert$; the open ball of centre $T$ and radius $r>0$ is $B(T,r)=\{W:\lVert T-W\rVertclosed ball is its closure.

Theorem (the basic properties). The space is connected and path-connected, indeed contractible, as a real vector space; it is not compact; and it is homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^{4}$, hence to the open ball and to the open four-dimensional cube.

Proof. Every vector space is contractible by the straight-line homotopy to the origin, hence connected and path-connected; the space is unbounded, so it is not compact; and the norm gives a homeomorphism with $\mathbb{R}^{4}$ by the coordinate map.

Corollary (the one-point compactification). The one-point compactification of the space is the four-sphere $S^{4}$; the spheres and the balls of the norm are the images of the standard ones under the coordinate homeomorphism.

Proof. The one-point compactification of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is $S^{n}$, here $n=4$; and the norm balls are the standard Euclidean balls read in the coordinates.

The Algebra Structure

The four products are continuous, and the three conjugations are isometries.

Theorem (continuity of the products). The four general products, as maps $\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{r}}\times\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{r}}\to\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{r}}$, are continuous; so is the addition, and so is the multiplication by a scalar. The algebra is a topological algebra for the Euclidean topology.

Proof. Each product is bilinear or sesquilinear over $\mathbb{C}$ with constant coordinates, hence a continuous map of the finite-dimensional space, by the continuity of the polynomial and conjugate-polynomial coordinate functions; the addition is continuous as the addition of $\mathbb{R}^{4}$.

Theorem (the involutions are isometries). The bar, the natural sign and the star preserve the norm and the metric, $$ \lVert\bar T\rVert=\lVert T^{\natural}\rVert=\lVert T^{*}\rVert=\lVert T\rVert, $$ so each is an isometry of $\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{r}}$ onto itself, and the three form a Klein four-group of isometries.

Proof. $\langle T\rangle_{\natural*}=A\bar A+B\bar B$; the bar replaces $A$ and $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ by their conjugates, leaving the two moduli; the natural sign negates $B$, leaving the two moduli; the star is their composite, so it too preserves them. The metric is $\lVert T-W\rVert$, and each involution is linear or antilinear and preserves the norm, hence the metric; the Klein group is The Two Involutions and the Group of Involutions.

Corollary (the unit group is open and the zero divisors closed). The unit group $\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{r}}^{\times}$ is open, and its complement, the zero-divisor set, is closed; the zero-divisor set has empty interior, so the unit group is dense.

Proof. The zero-divisor set is $\{\langle T\rangle_{\natural}=0\}=\{q_1=0\}\cup\{q_2=0\}$, the union of two real planes through the origin, which is closed as the vanishing set of the continuous functions $q_1$ and $q_2$; a plane has empty interior, and a finite union of sets of empty interior has empty interior, so the unit group is dense. The openness is the complement of a closed set.

The Relation to the Four Products

Each product is continuous and induces the same topology, the Euclidean one.

Theorem. For each of the four products $\circ$, the operation $W\mapsto X\circ W$ is a homeomorphism of $\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{r}}$ onto itself when $X$ is a unit, and the topology attached to the product is the Euclidean topology; the four products give one and the same topology.

Proof. A continuous bijection of $\mathbb{R}^{4}$ whose inverse is the multiplication by $X^{-1}$, also continuous, is a homeomorphism; and the topology is the unique vector-space topology, so all four agree. The differences between the four are in the metric-like quantities they carry, not in the topology, as Topology and Metric for Each of the Operations records.

Corollary (the product degenerates exactly at the zero divisors). For a zero divisor $X$ the map $W\mapsto XW$ is continuous but not injective and not surjective, its image being one of the two idempotent lines for the plain product; so the failure of the product is concentrated on the closed zero-divisor set.

Proof. Multiplication by a zero divisor has a non-trivial kernel and an image of dimension smaller than four; the extreme case is an element of an idempotent line, whose image is that line.

Summary

The reduced biquaternion algebra carries the Euclidean topology of $\mathbb{R}^{4}$ through the norm $\lVert T\rVert=\sqrt{\langle T\rangle_{\natural*}}=\sqrt{a^{2}+a'^{2}+b^{2}+b'^{2}}$, and only the form $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\natural*}$ is positive definite, so only $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\natural*}$ gives a topological norm. All norms are equivalent, so the topology is unique, metrisable, complete and locally compact; the space is contractible and its one-point compactification is $S^{4}$. The four products are continuous, the three conjugations are isometries forming a Klein four-group, and the unit group is open and dense while the zero-divisor set is closed with empty interior. Each of the four products is continuous and induces the same Euclidean topology; the differences between the products lie in the metric-like quantities, not in the topology. The multiplication has zero divisors and is not a division algebra, and the zero-divisor set is exactly the boundary of the unit group.
