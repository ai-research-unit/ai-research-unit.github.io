Square Roots of Minus One, Zero and Plus One in the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra

Introduction

This article computes the roots of the three central values $-1$, $0$ and $+1$ in the block, the symmetric quaternionic sesquilinear product $\tilde P\star\tilde Q=\tfrac12(\tilde P^{\natural}\tilde Q^{*}+\tilde Q^{*}\tilde P^{\natural})$ of Introduction to the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra of Biquaternions. It follows Nilpotents of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra and prepares Square Roots of a General Element in the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra, which reads the same equation for an arbitrary value.

The root is relative to a product, and the product is part of the definition: it is stated once for a general product in Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures, §Powers, the Square and the Roots, and read on the products of the space. The unqualified word means the root in the block. The three values are the central scalars $\lambda e_0$ with $\lambda\in\{-1,0,+1\}$, the values singled out by the polynomial equations $\xi^2=1$ and $\xi^2=0$ of the biquaternion algebra; the root of $0$ is the nilpotent of the block, computed in Nilpotents of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra, and is recalled here for the sake of the comparison, while the roots of $-1$ and $+1$ are new.

The product is Introduction to the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra of Biquaternions; the diagonal is The Non-Central Diagonal and the Two Halves of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra; the zero divisors are Zero Divisors of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra; the idempotents are Idempotents of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra; the roots of the three central values in the plain product are Square Roots of Minus One, Zero and Plus One in the General Plain Algebra; and the isotropic elements of the Krein form are The Isotropic Structure of the General Quaternionic Sesqualgebra.

Conventions. $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$ with basis $e_0,e_1,e_2,e_3$, $e_k^2=-e_0$ and $e_1e_2=e_3$; $\tilde Q=\sum_\mu Q_\mu e_\mu$ with $Q_\mu\in\mathbb{C}$ and central scalar imaginary $i$; $\mathbf Q=\sum_kQ_ke_k$; $(\mathbf P,\mathbf Q)=\sum_kP_kQ_k$; ${}^{\natural}$ negates $e_1,e_2,e_3$ and fixes the coefficients, ${}^{*}={}^{\natural}\circ\bar{\cdot}$; and $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ is the real quaternion subspace. The diagonal is $\tilde Q\star\tilde Q=K(\tilde Q,\tilde Q)e_0-Q_0\overline{\mathbf Q}-\overline{Q_0}\mathbf Q$ with $K(\tilde Q,\tilde Q)=|Q_0|^2-\sum_k|Q_k|^2$. Throughout this article the product is the block.

Definition

Definition (root of an element in a product). Let $\star$ be a product on $\mathbb{B}$ and let $\tilde A\in\mathbb{B}$. A square root of $\tilde A$ in $\star$ is an element $\tilde P$ with

$$ \tilde P\star\tilde P=\tilde A . $$

When $\tilde A=\lambda e_0$ is a central scalar with $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$, the roots are the roots of $\lambda$ in $\star$, and the equation reads $\tilde P\star\tilde P=\lambda e_0$.

This is Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures, §Powers, the Square and the Roots. Unqualified, the product is the block. The origin is a root of $0$ in every product and is excluded from the families below.

The Reduction to the Central Condition

Proposition (the shape of the square). For every $\tilde P=\alpha e_0+\mathbf x$ the diagonal is

$$ \tilde P\star\tilde P=K(\tilde P,\tilde P)e_0-2\,\mathrm{Re}\bigl(\alpha\overline{\mathbf x}\bigr), \qquad K(\tilde P,\tilde P)=|\alpha|^2-\lVert\mathbf x\rVert^2 , $$

whose scalar part is real and whose vector part is a real vector; the diagonal is a real quaternion.

Proof. This is the diagonal theorem of The Non-Central Diagonal and the Two Halves of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra.

Corollary (the two conditions for a root of a central value). $\tilde P\star\tilde P=\lambda e_0$ holds for a real $\lambda$ exactly when

$$ \alpha\overline{\mathbf x}\in i\mathbb{R}^3 \qquad\text{and}\qquad K(\tilde P,\tilde P)=\lambda , $$

and for a non-real complex $\lambda$ it holds never.

Proof. The right-hand side $\lambda e_0$ is central, so the vector part $-2\mathrm{Re}(\alpha\overline{\mathbf x})$ of the left side must vanish, that is $\alpha\overline{\mathbf x}\in i\mathbb{R}^3$; the scalar part $K(\tilde P,\tilde P)$ is real and must equal $\lambda$, so $\lambda$ is real. Conversely the two conditions make the two sides agree.

The Root of Zero

Theorem (the roots of $0$). The roots of $0$ in the block are the origin and the elements

$$ \alpha(e_0+i\boldsymbol\mu), \qquad \alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{\times},\quad \boldsymbol\mu\in\mathbb{R}^3,\quad \lVert\boldsymbol\mu\rVert=1 . $$

Proof. This is the square-zero set of Nilpotents of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra: the parameters $\mathbf c=|\alpha|^2\boldsymbol\mu$ of that theorem are exactly the real unit-vector directions here.

The root set of $0$, without the origin, is the nilpotent cone of real dimension four. It contains no pure element, in contrast with the plain product, whose roots of $0$ are the pure isotropic vectors.

The Roots of Minus One

Theorem (the roots of $-1$). The roots of $-1$ in the block are the disjoint union of the pure family

$$ \tilde P=\mathbf P\in\mathbb{C}^3,\qquad \lVert\mathbf P\rVert^2=1 , $$

the pure vectors of unit norm, and the non-pure family $\mathcal R_{-1}$ of the elements

$$ \tilde P=\alpha e_0+\frac{i\alpha}{|\alpha|^2}\mathbf c , \qquad \alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{\times},\quad \mathbf c\in\mathbb{R}^3,\quad \lVert\mathbf c\rVert^2=|\alpha|^2\bigl(|\alpha|^2+1\bigr) . $$

Proof. Let $\tilde P=\alpha e_0+\mathbf x$ and apply the corollary of §The Reduction.

If $\alpha=0$ the vector part vanishes automatically and $K=-\lVert\mathbf x\rVert^2=-1$, so $\lVert\mathbf x\rVert^2=1$: the pure unit vectors.

If $\alpha\neq0$ the condition $\alpha\overline{\mathbf x}\in i\mathbb{R}^3$ writes $\alpha\overline{x_k}=-ic_k$ with $c_k\in\mathbb{R}$, that is $x_k=i\alpha c_k/|\alpha|^2$, the vector $\mathbf c=(c_1,c_2,c_3)$ real; then $\lVert\mathbf x\rVert^2=\lVert\mathbf c\rVert^2/|\alpha|^2$ and $K=|\alpha|^2-\lVert\mathbf c\rVert^2/|\alpha|^2=-1$ reads $\lVert\mathbf c\rVert^2=|\alpha|^2(|\alpha|^2+1)$, which is always solvable because $|\alpha|^2(|\alpha|^2+1)>0$. Conversely the display satisfies both conditions. The two families are disjoint because the first has $\alpha=0$ and the second $\alpha\neq0$.

Corollary (the shape). The pure family is a real sphere of dimension five in the vector subspace and the non-pure family is a real manifold of dimension four; the root set of $-1$ has real dimension five.

Proof. The pure vectors of unit norm are the sphere $S^5$ of the real six-dimensional vector subspace; the parameters of the non-pure family are the punctured plane of real dimension two and the sphere of radius $|\alpha|\sqrt{|\alpha|^2+1}$ of real dimension two.

Corollary (the named witnesses). $e_1$, $e_2$ and $ie_1$ are roots of $-1$; the element $e_1+ie_2$ is not, it is a root of $-2$.

Proof. $K(e_1,e_1)=K(e_2,e_2)=K(ie_1,ie_1)=-1$, each a pure vector of unit norm. For $e_1+ie_2$, $K=-2$ with vanishing vector part, so its square is $-2e_0$.

The Roots of Plus One

Theorem (the roots of $+1$). The roots of $+1$ in the block are the elements

$$ \tilde P=\alpha e_0+\frac{i\alpha}{|\alpha|^2}\mathbf c , \qquad \alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{\times},\quad |\alpha|\geq1,\quad \mathbf c\in\mathbb{R}^3,\quad \lVert\mathbf c\rVert^2=|\alpha|^2\bigl(|\alpha|^2-1\bigr) . $$

There is no pure root of $+1$.

Proof. The reduction gives the same two conditions. If $\alpha=0$ then $K=-\lVert\mathbf x\rVert^2=1$ is impossible. If $\alpha\neq0$ the central condition writes $\mathbf x=i\alpha\mathbf c/|\alpha|^2$ with $\mathbf c$ real and $K=|\alpha|^2-\lVert\mathbf c\rVert^2/|\alpha|^2=1$ reads $\lVert\mathbf c\rVert^2=|\alpha|^2(|\alpha|^2-1)$, solvable exactly when $|\alpha|^2\geq1$.

Corollary (the unit candidate). $\tilde P=e_0$ is a root of $+1$: it is the case $\alpha=1$, $\mathbf c=\mathbf 0$. It is the only element of the block that is at once an idempotent and a root of $+1$.

Proof. $e_0\star e_0=e_0$ by the multiplication table, so the square of $e_0$ is $e_0$, that is $+1$ times $e_0$. If a nontrivial idempotent $-\tfrac12e_0+\boldsymbol\mu$ had square $e_0$ then the idempotent equation would give $-\tfrac12e_0+\boldsymbol\mu=e_0$, false; the origin is not a root of $+1$; so $e_0$ is the only common element.

Comparison of the Three Sets

value $\lambda$ pure roots non-pure roots real dimension
$-1$ unit vectors of $\mathbb{C}^3$ $\mathcal R_{-1}$, any $\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ $5$
$0$ none except the origin the nilpotent cone $\alpha(e_0+i\boldsymbol\mu)$ $4$
$+1$ none $\mathcal R_{+1}$, $|\alpha|\geq1$ $4$

The three sets are pairwise disjoint: the square is a function and its three values are distinct scalars. The root of $0$ is the nilpotent cone of Nilpotents of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra; the roots of $-1$ and of $+1$ are the two involutive values, and the unit candidate $e_0$ is the simplest root of $+1$. The root of $-1$ is the only one of the three whose solutions enter the vector subspace, and there they are exactly the pure vectors of unit norm: the vector subspace contributes an involution, an element of square $-\lVert\mathbf P\rVert^2 e_0$, and no element of square $+1$.

Unlike the plain product, the block's root of $-1$ is not the parametrisation of its idempotents: there the idempotent $\tfrac12(e_0+\xi i)$ and the root of $-1$ are built from the same $\xi$, while here the idempotents are $-\tfrac12e_0+\boldsymbol\mu$ and lie on no one of the three root sets. The comparison of the element theory is Idempotents of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra.

Worked Examples

A pure root of $-1$. $\tilde P=e_1$: $e_1\star e_1=-e_0$, and $\lVert e_1\rVert^2=1$.

A non-pure root of $-1$. $\tilde P=e_0+i\sqrt2\,e_1$: here $\alpha=1$ and $\mathbf c=(\sqrt2,0,0)$ with $\lVert\mathbf c\rVert^2=2=1\cdot(1+1)$, so $\tilde P\star\tilde P=-e_0$.

A root of $+1$. $\tilde P=e_0$; and $\tilde P=\sqrt2\,e_0+ie_1$ has $\alpha=\sqrt2$, $|\alpha|^2=2$, $\mathbf x=i e_1=i\alpha\mathbf c/|\alpha|^2$ with $\mathbf c=(\sqrt2,0,0)$ of $\lVert\mathbf c\rVert^2=2=|\alpha|^2(|\alpha|^2-1)$, so $\tilde P\star\tilde P=e_0$.

A root of $0$. $\tilde P=e_0+ie_1$: the square vanishes, and it is the nilpotent of the previous article.

A root of $-2$ that is neither of the three. $\tilde P=e_1+ie_2$: $K=-2$ and the vector part vanishes, so $\tilde P\star\tilde P=-2e_0$; this is not a root of $-1$, $0$ or $+1$.

Summary

The roots of the three central values in the block are the solutions of $\tilde P\star\tilde P=\lambda e_0$ for $\lambda\in\{-1,0,+1\}$. The root of $0$ is the nilpotent cone $\alpha(e_0+i\boldsymbol\mu)$, of real dimension four and with no pure element. The roots of $-1$ are the disjoint union of the pure unit vectors, a real five-sphere, and the family $\mathcal R_{-1}$ of real dimension four; those of $+1$ are the family $\mathcal R_{+1}$ with $|\alpha|\geq1$, of real dimension four, and contain $e_0$, the only common element of the idempotents and the roots of $+1$. The vector subspace contributes a root of $-1$ for each of its unit vectors and no root of $+1$, and the three root sets are pairwise disjoint; the idempotents $-\tfrac12e_0+\boldsymbol\mu$ lie on none of them.
