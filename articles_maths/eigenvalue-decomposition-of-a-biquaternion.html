Eigenvalue Decomposition of a Biquaternion

Introduction

The biquaternion algebra $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$ is simple and four-dimensional over $\mathbb{C}$, and the matrix model $\mathsf{M}_2$ identifies it with $M_2(\mathbb{C})$. This article reads the eigenvalue decomposition of a biquaternion: the writing of $\tilde Q$ as a complex linear combination of the two idempotents of a complete orthogonal pair, with the eigenvalues as coefficients. The general definition and the existence theorem are those of The Eigenvalue Decomposition of an Element; the biquaternion content is that, in the regime of distinct eigenvalues, the pair is the pair of idempotents of a pure root of $+1$, and the decomposition is the Peirce decomposition attached to that root in Ideals and Peirce Decomposition of the Biquaternion Algebra.

Placement. The article is in the Advanced Properties section of the Biquaternions category, after Ideals and Peirce Decomposition of the Biquaternion Algebra and before Modules over the Biquaternion Algebra.

Conventions. The notation is fixed in Conventions in Mathematics: the base is $e_t,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z$, a general element is $\tilde Q=Q_te_t+\vec Q$ with $\vec Q=Q_x\vec e_x+Q_y\vec e_y+Q_z\vec e_z$ and complex coefficients, an idempotent is $\tilde\Pi$, a root of $+1$ is $\tilde\Xi$ and a pure root is $\vec\Xi$, and a pure nilpotent is $\vec\Upsilon$. The characteristic polynomial, the spectrum and the eigenvalues $\lambda_\pm=Q_t\pm iB$, with $B^2=Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2$, are those of Biquaternion Spectral Theory, §The Biquaternion Spectrum; the root of $+1$, the halving map and the primitive idempotents are those of Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the Biquaternion Algebra, §The Idempotents; the four Peirce corners are those of Ideals and Peirce Decomposition of the Biquaternion Algebra, §The Peirce Decomposition; the centraliser and the diagonal part are those of The Centraliser and the Diagonal Peirce Part; the off-diagonal components are those of Off-Diagonal Peirce Components and Noncentral Idempotents; the reduced norm and the invertibility criterion are those of Introduction to the Biquaternion Algebra, §The Reduced Norm and Invertibility; the eigenvalue formula and the parity-matched lattice are developed in §The Algebraic Fibre of the Decomposition and used in Biquaternion Elementary Functions, §The Eigenvalue Formula.

Scope. The article proves the eigenvalue decomposition in the regime of distinct eigenvalues, identifies its eigenprojections with the idempotents of a pure root of $+1$, reads the decomposition as the Peirce decomposition relative to one of them, and treats the degenerate regimes: the scalar element, where the eigenvalue is repeated and the two-projector writing is not unique; the zero divisor, where one eigenvalue vanishes and the decomposition survives; and the nilpotent vector part, where no eigenvalue decomposition exists.

Boundaries. The split-complex and reduced biquaternion cases are those of Eigenvalue Decomposition of a Split-Complex Number and Eigenvalue Decomposition of a Reduced Biquaternion; the quaternion case, with its left and right eigenvalues, is the subject of Quaternion Spectral Theory; the exponential and the logarithm built on the decomposition are those of Biquaternion Elementary Functions; and the spectral theorem in its general form is that of Biquaternion Spectral Theory. The article cites these and does not restate them. The other products of the biquaternion space carry nilpotents and idempotents relative to the product, and are outside its scope.

The Spectrum and the Characteristic Polynomial

The Pure Root of the Vector Part

The square of the vector part is central, $$ \vec Q^2=-(Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2)e_t, $$ as in Biquaternion Spectral Theory, §The Biquaternion Spectrum. Let $B$ be a square root of $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2$, so that $\vec Q^2=-B^2e_t$.

Assume $B\neq0$. Put $$ \vec U=\frac{\vec Q}{B},\qquad \vec\Xi=-i\vec U=-\frac{i\vec Q}{B}. $$ Then $\vec U$ is a pure unit element of the biquaternion layer, $\vec U^2=-e_t$, and $\vec\Xi$ is a pure root of $+1$: $$ \vec\Xi^2=(-i)^2\vec U^2=e_t. $$ Conversely $\vec Q=B\vec U=iB\vec\Xi$. The root is pure, so it carries the arrow and not the tilde (§Pure Elements and their Coefficients of Conventions in Mathematics), and it belongs to the roots classified in Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the Biquaternion Algebra, §The Roots of One. For a real vector part, $B$ may be taken real and positive, $\vec U$ is then the real unit vector $\vec u$, and $\vec\Xi=-i\vec u$ lies in the family $i\vec u$ of the pure roots. More generally, for $B\neq0$, $\vec U$ is real exactly when $\vec Q$ is a complex multiple of a real vector, equivalently when $\tilde Q$ is normal (§The Hermitian Case); otherwise $\vec U$ and $\vec\Xi$ have nonzero imaginary parts.

The choice is two-fold: the replacement $B\mapsto-B$ replaces $\vec U$ and $\vec\Xi$ by their opposites. Only $B^2$ is determined by $\tilde Q$, and with it the unordered pair $\{\vec\Xi,-\vec\Xi\}$; in the real case the convention $B>0$ removes the choice.

The spectrum is the pair of roots of the characteristic polynomial, $\lambda_\pm=Q_t\pm iB$.

Since $\vec Q=iB\vec\Xi$, $$ (\tilde Q-Q_te_t)^2=\vec Q^2=-B^2e_t, $$ hence $$ \tilde Q^2-2Q_t\tilde Q+(Q_t^2+B^2)e_t=0,\qquad Q_t^2+B^2=\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}. $$ This is the quadratic identity of Biquaternion Spectral Theory, §The Biquaternion Spectrum, and the characteristic polynomial is $$ p_{\tilde Q}(X)=X^2-2Q_tX+\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=(X-Q_t)^2+B^2, $$ with roots $$ \lambda_\pm=Q_t\pm iB=Q_t\pm i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}. $$ The radical is one of the two square roots, and the other choice swaps $\lambda_+$ and $\lambda_-$. In the real case the positive root is taken, $B=|\vec Q|=\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}>0$, and $\lambda_\pm=Q_t\pm i|\vec Q|$. For example, $\tilde Q=(1+i)e_t+\vec e_x+i\vec e_y+\vec e_z$ has $B^2=1$, so with $B=1$ the eigenvalues are $\lambda_\pm=1+i\pm i$, that is, $\lambda_+=1+2i$ and $\lambda_-=1$. The discriminant is $$ (\lambda_+-\lambda_-)^2=(2iB)^2=-4B^2\neq0, $$ so the eigenvalues are distinct exactly when $B\neq0$. The minimal polynomial is then $p_{\tilde Q}$.

Under the matrix model the trace is $\operatorname{Tr}\mathsf{M}_2(\tilde Q)=2Q_t$ and the determinant is $\det\mathsf{M}_2(\tilde Q)=\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}$ (§The Matrix Model of Ideals and Peirce Decomposition of the Biquaternion Algebra), so $p_{\tilde Q}$ is the characteristic polynomial of $\mathsf{M}_2(\tilde Q)$ and $\lambda_\pm$ are its eigenvalues.

The Eigenprojections

The eigenprojections of a biquaternion with $B\neq0$ are the two primitive idempotents $\tilde\Pi_\pm$ of the pure root $\vec\Xi$.

Put $$ \tilde\Pi_+=\frac12(e_t+\vec\Xi),\qquad \tilde\Pi_-=\frac12(e_t-\vec\Xi), $$ the two idempotents of the root $\vec\Xi$ under the halving map of Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the Biquaternion Algebra, §The Idempotents. By that article the pair is complete and orthogonal, $\tilde\Pi_++\tilde\Pi_-=e_t$ and $\tilde\Pi_+\tilde\Pi_-=0$, and the root is their difference, $\vec\Xi=\tilde\Pi_+-\tilde\Pi_-$; by Ideals and Peirce Decomposition of the Biquaternion Algebra, §Idempotents and Orthogonal Idempotents, each member is primitive.

They are the eigenprojections of $\tilde Q$: $$ \tilde Q\tilde\Pi_+=\lambda_+\tilde\Pi_+,\qquad \tilde Q\tilde\Pi_-=\lambda_-\tilde\Pi_-. $$ Indeed, since $\vec Q=iB\vec\Xi$ and $\vec\Xi^2=e_t$, $$ \tilde Q\tilde\Pi_+=(Q_te_t+iB\vec\Xi)\frac12(e_t+\vec\Xi)=\frac12(Q_t+iB)(e_t+\vec\Xi)=\lambda_+\tilde\Pi_+, $$ and, with $Q_t-iB$ in place of $Q_t+iB$ and $e_t-\vec\Xi$ in place of $e_t+\vec\Xi$, $$ \tilde Q\tilde\Pi_-=\lambda_-\tilde\Pi_-. $$

Equivalently, by the eigenprojector formula of The Lagrange–Sylvester Eigenprojector Formula, §The Eigenprojector Formula, $$ \tilde\Pi_+=\frac{\tilde Q-\lambda_-e_t}{\lambda_+-\lambda_-},\qquad \tilde\Pi_-=\frac{\tilde Q-\lambda_+e_t}{\lambda_--\lambda_+},\qquad \lambda_+-\lambda_-=2iB. $$ Hence $\tilde\Pi_\pm$ are polynomials in $\tilde Q$, and in particular commute with $\tilde Q$.

The pair is collected in the table.

Idempotent Definition Eigenvalue Properties
$\tilde\Pi_+$ $\tfrac12(e_t+\vec\Xi)$ $\lambda_+=Q_t+iB$ $\tilde\Pi_+^2=\tilde\Pi_+$, noncentral, primitive
$\tilde\Pi_-$ $\tfrac12(e_t-\vec\Xi)$ $\lambda_-=Q_t-iB$ $\tilde\Pi_-^2=\tilde\Pi_-$, noncentral, primitive

The two idempotents are complementary and orthogonal, $\tilde\Pi_++\tilde\Pi_-=e_t$ and $\tilde\Pi_+\tilde\Pi_-=0$.

The Eigenvalue Decomposition

Every biquaternion with distinct eigenvalues is the complex linear combination of $\tilde\Pi_+,\tilde\Pi_-$ with its eigenvalues as coefficients.

Summing the two eigenvalue relations, $$ \boxed{\ \tilde Q=\lambda_+\tilde\Pi_++\lambda_-\tilde\Pi_-\ } =(Q_t+iB)\tilde\Pi_++(Q_t-iB)\tilde\Pi_-. $$ This is an eigenvalue decomposition in the sense of The Eigenvalue Decomposition of an Element, §The definition: the coefficients $\lambda_\pm$ are distinct, the idempotents $\tilde\Pi_\pm$ are nonzero, orthogonal and complete, and they are the eigenprojections (the eigenprojectors of that article). Separating scalar and vector parts, $$ \tilde Q=\frac{\lambda_++\lambda_-}{2}e_t+\frac{\lambda_+-\lambda_-}{2}\vec\Xi=Q_te_t+iB\vec\Xi=Q_te_t+B\vec U. $$ So the decomposition writes $\tilde Q$ as a complex linear combination of the two primitive idempotents of $\vec\Xi$, with the eigenvalues as coefficients. For the example of §The Spectrum and the Characteristic Polynomial, $B=1$, $\vec U=\vec e_x+i\vec e_y+\vec e_z$ and $\vec\Xi=-i\vec e_x+\vec e_y-i\vec e_z$, so $\tilde\Pi_\pm=\frac12(e_t\pm\vec\Xi)$, and $$ (1+i)e_t+\vec e_x+i\vec e_y+\vec e_z=(1+2i)\tilde\Pi_++\tilde\Pi_-, $$ as the scalar-vector form confirms: $\frac{\lambda_++\lambda_-}{2}e_t+\frac{\lambda_+-\lambda_-}{2}\vec\Xi=(1+i)e_t+i\vec\Xi=\tilde Q$.

Uniqueness. By the uniqueness theorem of The Eigenvalue Decomposition of an Element, §Uniqueness, an element with distinct eigenvalues has a unique eigenprojection attached to each eigenvalue. Here the two eigenprojections are the two idempotents of the root $\vec\Xi$, and the root is read back from them as $\vec\Xi=\tilde\Pi_+-\tilde\Pi_-$. The unordered pair $\{\tilde\Pi_+,\tilde\Pi_-\}$ is determined by $\tilde Q$; the ordered pair is determined only up to the swap that sends $B$ to $-B$ and $\vec\Xi$ to $-\vec\Xi$. The labelling is canonical when $B$ is chosen real and positive, which is possible exactly when $B^2$ is a positive real number; otherwise the choice of square root is a convention.

Semisimplicity. Since $\lambda_+\neq\lambda_-$, the minimal polynomial $p_{\tilde Q}$ is squarefree, and $\tilde Q$ is semisimple (Nilpotent Elements and the Jordan–Chevalley Decomposition, §Semisimple Elements). The decomposition is the diagonalisation of $\tilde Q$ with respect to the noncentral idempotents $\tilde\Pi_\pm$.

Under the matrix model, $\mathsf{M}_2(\tilde\Pi_\pm)$ are the rank-one eigenprojections of $\mathsf{M}_2(\tilde Q)$.

The Peirce Decomposition Relative to the Eigenprojection

The Peirce decomposition relative to $\tilde\Pi_+$ has two diagonal lines and two square-zero mixed lines, and its diagonal part is the centraliser of $\tilde Q$.

Let $$ \mathbb{B}_{st}=\tilde\Pi_s\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_t,\qquad s,t\in\{+,-\}, $$ be the Peirce corners of the pair, in the notation of Ideals and Peirce Decomposition of the Biquaternion Algebra, §The Biquaternion Case. The Peirce decomposition is $$ \mathbb{B}=\mathbb{B}_{++}\oplus\mathbb{B}_{+-}\oplus\mathbb{B}_{-+}\oplus\mathbb{B}_{--}. $$ Each corner is one complex line, the diagonal corners are $\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_+$ and $\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_-$, and the two mixed corners are square-zero. This is the decomposition attached to the root $\vec\Xi=-i\vec Q/B$ in §The Peirce Decomposition of that article.

The four corners are collected in the table.

Corner Definition Value Dimension over $\mathbb{C}$
$\mathbb{B}_{++}$ $\tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+$ $\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_+$ $1$
$\mathbb{B}_{+-}$ $\tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$ one complex line, square-zero $1$
$\mathbb{B}_{-+}$ $\tilde\Pi_-\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+$ one complex line, square-zero $1$
$\mathbb{B}_{--}$ $\tilde\Pi_-\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$ $\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_-$ $1$

The diagonal part is $$ \mathbb{B}_{++}\oplus\mathbb{B}_{--}=\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_+\oplus\mathbb{C}\tilde\Pi_-=\mathbb{C}[\tilde Q]=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{e_t,\tilde Q\}. $$ It is a commutative subalgebra isomorphic to $\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}$. By The Centraliser and the Diagonal Peirce Part, §The theorem, applied to the eigenvalue decomposition, it is the centraliser of $\tilde Q$; by Off-Diagonal Peirce Components and Noncentral Idempotents, §The Commutator Interpretation, the mixed part is $[\tilde\Pi_+,\mathbb{B}]$, so an element commutes with $\tilde Q$ exactly when its two mixed Peirce components vanish.

On $\tilde Q$ the Peirce decomposition reads $$ \tilde Q=\lambda_+\tilde\Pi_++\lambda_-\tilde\Pi_-, $$ with zero mixed components. The eigenvalue decomposition is therefore the Peirce decomposition read on the element: the element lies in the diagonal part, and its two diagonal Peirce coordinates are its eigenvalues $Q_t\pm iB$. The mixed corners are the directions that fail to commute with $\tilde Q$.

The two signs of the pair are the two eigenvalues of multiplication by the root: an element of $\mathbb{B}_{st}$ is multiplied by $s$ on the left and by $t$ on the right by $\vec\Xi$ (§The Biquaternion Case). The diagonal corners are the components on which the two signs agree; $\tilde Q$ acts on them by $\lambda_+$ and $\lambda_-$ respectively.

Powers, Polynomial Calculus, and the Group of Units

The eigenvalue decomposition carries the polynomial calculus and the inverse, and the unit group inherits an eigenvalue map that is surjective but not injective.

By Sylvester's formula of The Lagrange–Sylvester Eigenprojector Formula, §Sylvester's Formula, every polynomial $f\in\mathbb{C}[X]$ acts as $$ f(\tilde Q)=f(\lambda_+)\tilde\Pi_++f(\lambda_-)\tilde\Pi_-. $$ In particular $$ \tilde Q^n=\lambda_+^n\tilde\Pi_++\lambda_-^n\tilde\Pi_-,\qquad n\geq0, $$ with $\tilde\Pi_++\tilde\Pi_-=e_t$ for $n=0$. In this regime, when $\tilde Q$ is a unit, the two eigenvalues are nonzero, since $\lambda_+\lambda_-=\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}\neq0$ (Introduction to the Biquaternion Algebra, §The Reduced Norm and Invertibility), and the formula extends to negative powers: $$ \tilde Q^{-1}=\lambda_+^{-1}\tilde\Pi_++\lambda_-^{-1}\tilde\Pi_-. $$ The result agrees with the inverse formula $\tilde Q^{-1}=\tilde Q^{\natural}/\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}$ of that article, since $\lambda_+\lambda_-=\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}$ and $\lambda_-\tilde\Pi_++\lambda_+\tilde\Pi_-=Q_te_t-B\vec U=\tilde Q^{\natural}$.

The group of units. The eigenvalue map $$ \mathbb{B}^{\times}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}, \qquad \tilde Q\longmapsto(\lambda_+,\lambda_-) $$ is surjective, because $\lambda_+\lambda_-=\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}$ and every pair of nonzero complex numbers occurs. It is not injective: two units with the same eigenvalues differ by an element of the mixed Peirce part $\mathbb{B}_{+-}\oplus\mathbb{B}_{-+}$. It is not a group homomorphism either, because the eigenvalues of a product are not determined by the eigenvalues of the factors. The polynomial calculus is a statement about the diagonal part $\mathbb{C}[\tilde Q]$, where the eigenvalues are coordinates; it does not extend as a coordinatewise calculus to the whole unit group.

The Algebraic Fibre of the Decomposition

The eigenvalue coordinates and the basis coordinates are two linear coordinate systems on the same diagonal part.

Fix the ordered pair $(\tilde\Pi_+,\tilde\Pi_-)$, equivalently the root $\vec\Xi=\tilde\Pi_+-\tilde\Pi_-$. The fibre of the map that sends an element with distinct eigenvalues to its ordered pair of eigenprojections is the set of elements $\mu_+\tilde\Pi_++\mu_-\tilde\Pi_-$ with $\mu_+\neq\mu_-$. Every element of the plane $\mathbb{C}[\tilde Q]$ is $$ \tilde Q_1=\mu_+\tilde\Pi_++\mu_-\tilde\Pi_-,\qquad \mu_\pm\in\mathbb{C}, $$ and the map $(\mu_+,\mu_-)\mapsto\tilde Q_1$ is a $\mathbb{C}$-linear isomorphism $\mathbb{C}^2\to\mathbb{C}[\tilde Q]$.

For $\mu_+\neq\mu_-$ this is the eigenvalue decomposition of $\tilde Q_1$, with the eigenprojections $\tilde\Pi_\pm$ and the eigenvalue data $(\mu_+,\mu_-)$. For $\mu_+=\mu_-=a$ the element is $\tilde Q_1=ae_t$, scalar; the writing is still valid but every complete orthogonal pair gives it, so the two-projector pair is not determined by $\tilde Q_1$, although the eigenprojection $e_t$ of the one-term decomposition is. The eigenvalue decomposition is then the one-term writing $\tilde Q_1=ae_t$ of §The Scalar Regime.

For $\mu_+\neq\mu_-$ the unordered pair $\{\mu_+,\mu_-\}$ is the spectrum, and with the pair of idempotents fixed the coordinate map to the unordered spectrum is two-to-one: the two preimages of $\{\mu_+,\mu_-\}$ are $\mu_+\tilde\Pi_++\mu_-\tilde\Pi_-$ and $\mu_-\tilde\Pi_++\mu_+\tilde\Pi_-$, which differ by $(\mu_+-\mu_-)(\tilde\Pi_+-\tilde\Pi_-)\neq0$. For eigenvalues constrained to subsets $S_\pm\subseteq\mathbb{C}$, the corresponding elements are $$ \{\mu_+\tilde\Pi_++\mu_-\tilde\Pi_-:\ \mu_+\in S_+,\ \mu_-\in S_-\}. $$

The Coordinate Change to Basis Coordinates

The scalar and vector coordinates of $\tilde Q_1$ are $$ \tilde Q_1=\frac{\mu_++\mu_-}{2}e_t+\frac{\mu_+-\mu_-}{2}\vec\Xi=ae_t+\Theta\vec U, \qquad a=\frac{\mu_++\mu_-}{2},\quad \Theta=\frac{\mu_+-\mu_-}{2i}, $$ the second equality using $\vec\Xi=-i\vec U$. The map $(\mu_+,\mu_-)\mapsto(a,\Theta)$ is a $\mathbb{C}$-linear isomorphism of $\mathbb{C}^2$; the scalar $a$ is the scalar part and $\Theta$ is the complex angle along the axis $\vec U$ used in Biquaternion Elementary Functions, §The Eigenvalue Formula. The two coordinate systems are the eigenvalue coordinates and the basis coordinates on the same diagonal part.

The Lattice of Eigenvalue Data

The eigenvalue coordinates in $2\pi i\mathbb{Z}^2$ give $$ L=\bigl\{2\pi i k_+\tilde\Pi_++2\pi i k_-\tilde\Pi_-:\ k_\pm\in\mathbb{Z}\bigr\}. $$ Using $\tilde\Pi_\pm=\tfrac12(e_t\pm\vec\Xi)$, $$ 2\pi i k_+\tilde\Pi_++2\pi i k_-\tilde\Pi_-=\pi i(k_++k_-)e_t+\pi i(k_+-k_-)\vec\Xi. $$ With $m=k_++k_-$ and $n=k_+-k_-$ one has $m-n=2k_-$, so $m\equiv n\pmod2$; conversely every pair $(m,n)$ of the same parity comes from the integers $k_\pm=(m\pm n)/2$. Hence $$ L=\bigl\{\pi i m\,e_t+\pi i n\,\vec\Xi:\ m,n\in\mathbb{Z},\ m\equiv n\pmod2\bigr\} =\bigl\{\pi i m\,e_t+\pi n\,\vec U:\ m,n\in\mathbb{Z},\ m\equiv n\pmod2\bigr\}. $$ This is the parity-matched lattice. In this regime it is the kernel of the exponential on the diagonal part, and Biquaternion Elementary Functions, §The Eigenvalue Formula, uses it for the logarithm. It is not the rectangular lattice $2\pi i\mathbb{Z}e_t\oplus2\pi\mathbb{Z}\vec U$: the rectangular lattice is the sublattice of index two in $L$ on which $m$ and $n$ are both even, the other coset is $\pi i e_t+\pi\vec U$, and the scalar and the vector coordinate cannot be chosen independently, because the idempotents $\tilde\Pi_\pm$ mix the two directions.

The Hermitian Case

Two further notions enter. Hermitian conjugation is the star, and normality is the condition $\tilde Q\tilde Q^{*}=\tilde Q^{*}\tilde Q$, as in Biquaternion Spectral Theory, §Hermitian and Normal Elements (§The Conjugations of Conventions in Mathematics). The proposition below identifies the case in which the pair is Hermitian.

Proposition. Let $B\neq0$. The following are equivalent: (i) $\tilde\Pi_\pm$ are Hermitian; (ii) $\vec U$ is real; (iii) $\vec\Xi$ is purely imaginary; (iv) $\tilde Q$ is normal.

Proof. Since $\vec\Xi=-i\vec U$ and $\vec\Xi^{*}=-\overline{\vec\Xi}$, $$ \tilde\Pi_\pm^{*}=\frac12\bigl(e_t\pm\vec\Xi^{*}\bigr)=\frac12\bigl(e_t\mp i\overline{\vec U}\bigr), $$ so (i) holds exactly when $\overline{\vec U}=\vec U$, which is (ii); and (ii) and (iii) are the same condition, since $\vec U=i\vec\Xi$. If (ii) holds, then $\tilde Q^{*}=\overline{Q_t}e_t-\overline{B}\,\vec U$ lies in $\mathbb{C}[\tilde Q]$, so $\tilde Q$ commutes with $\tilde Q^{*}$ and (iv) holds. Conversely, if (iv) holds, Theorem 11.2 of Biquaternion Spectral Theory, §The Spectral Theorem, writes $\tilde Q=\lambda_1\tilde\Pi_1+\lambda_2\tilde\Pi_2$ with Hermitian idempotents; since $B\neq0$ the eigenvalues are distinct, and the formulas of that theorem identify $\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Pi_2$ with $\tilde\Pi_\pm$, so (i) holds.

The pair is then the Hermitian pair of the root $\vec\Xi=-i\vec u$ over a real unit vector $\vec u$ (§The Families of Idempotents of Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the Biquaternion Algebra), and the decomposition is the spectral theorem of Biquaternion Spectral Theory, §The Spectral Theorem, read in the biquaternion algebra. When in addition $\tilde Q$ is Hermitian, $Q_t\in\mathbb{R}$ and $\vec Q=i\vec q'$ with $\vec q'$ real, and $B=i|\vec q'|$, so that $\lambda_\pm=Q_t\mp|\vec q'|$ are real.

Degenerate Regimes

The eigenvalue decomposition exists exactly when the minimal polynomial is split and squarefree (The Eigenvalue Decomposition of an Element, §Existence and Uniqueness). The spectrum separates the cases as follows. For $B\neq0$ the minimal polynomial is $p_{\tilde Q}=(X-Q_t)^2+B^2$, which is squarefree: the element is semisimple, with distinct eigenvalues, and the decomposition exists. When in addition $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=0$, one eigenvalue vanishes: this is the zero-divisor regime. For $B=0$ the characteristic polynomial is $(X-Q_t)^2$; the minimal polynomial is $(X-Q_t)^2$ when $\vec Q\neq0$, and $X-Q_t$ when $\vec Q=0$. The first is the nilpotent regime, where no eigenvalue decomposition exists; the second is the scalar regime, with the one-term decomposition.

The Scalar Regime

A scalar element has a repeated eigenvalue, the one-term eigenprojection $e_t$, and a non-unique two-projector refinement.

Let $\vec Q=0$, so $\tilde Q=Q_te_t$. The element is central and semisimple, with the single eigenvalue $Q_t$ and minimal polynomial $X-Q_t$; its centraliser is all of $\mathbb{B}$.

In the sense of the general definition the eigenvalue decomposition has one term, $\tilde Q=Q_te_t$, with the single eigenprojection $e_t$, and it is unique. A decomposition into two primitive idempotents with the repeated coefficient $Q_t$ is a different object: every complete orthogonal pair $\tilde\Pi_1,\tilde\Pi_2$ gives $$ \tilde Q=Q_t\tilde\Pi_1+Q_t\tilde\Pi_2, $$ and the pair is not determined by $\tilde Q$. The eigenvalue data therefore determine nothing beyond the scalar, and the two-projector writing is non-unique.

The Zero-Divisor Regime

A zero divisor with distinct eigenvalues has one eigenvalue equal to zero and still admits an eigenvalue decomposition.

Suppose $B\neq0$ and $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=Q_t^2+B^2=0$. Then $Q_t=\pm iB$ and $Q_t\neq0$, and the eigenvalues are $$ \lambda_\sigma=Q_t+\sigma iB=2Q_t,\qquad \lambda_{-\sigma}=Q_t-\sigma iB=0, $$ where $\sigma\in\{+,-\}$ is the sign for which $Q_t=\sigma iB$. They are distinct because $B\neq0$. The minimal polynomial is $$ p_{\tilde Q}(X)=X^2-2Q_tX=X(X-2Q_t), $$ which is squarefree, so $\tilde Q$ is semisimple. The eigenvalue decomposition is $$ \tilde Q=2Q_t\tilde\Pi_\sigma+0\cdot\tilde\Pi_{-\sigma}, $$ and $\tilde Q=2Q_t\tilde\Pi_\sigma$ is a nonzero scalar multiple of the idempotent $\tilde\Pi_\sigma$. The element is a zero divisor, since $\tilde Q\tilde\Pi_{-\sigma}=0$ with $\tilde\Pi_{-\sigma}\neq0$, and it is not a unit; the decomposition survives because one eigenvalue vanishes, exactly as in the split-complex and reduced biquaternion cases. The nilpotent regime of §The Nilpotent Regime is different: there the minimal polynomial has a repeated root.

The Nilpotent Regime

A nonzero nilpotent vector part makes the element non-semisimple, and no eigenvalue decomposition exists. The regime is named for the vector part: the element itself is nilpotent exactly when $Q_t=0$, in which case it is a nonzero zero divisor.

Let $B=0$ and $\vec Q\neq0$. Then $\vec Q^2=0$, so $\vec\Upsilon=\vec Q$ is a pure nilpotent, written with the arrow and without the tilde by §The Nilpotent Convention of Conventions in Mathematics, and $$ \tilde Q=Q_te_t+\vec\Upsilon,\qquad \vec\Upsilon^2=0. $$ The element is a unit exactly when $Q_t\neq0$, since $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=Q_t^2$ (Introduction to the Biquaternion Algebra, §The Reduced Norm and Invertibility), and its minimal polynomial is $$ (X-Q_t)^2, $$ which is not squarefree. By the theorem of The Eigenvalue Decomposition of an Element, §Existence and Uniqueness, $\tilde Q$ has no eigenvalue decomposition. In the matrix model $\mathsf{M}_2(\tilde Q)$ is a single Jordan block, conjugate to $$ \begin{pmatrix}Q_t&1\\0&Q_t\end{pmatrix}. $$

The centraliser of $\tilde Q$ is $$ \{ae_t+b\vec\Upsilon:\ a,b\in\mathbb{C}\}. $$ Its only idempotents are $0$ and $e_t$: if $(ae_t+b\vec\Upsilon)^2=ae_t+b\vec\Upsilon$, then $a^2=a$ and $2ab=b$, so $(a,b)=(0,0)$ or $(1,0)$. Hence no pair of nonzero orthogonal idempotents summing to $e_t$ lies in it, and there is no Peirce decomposition adapted to $\tilde Q$ into two primitive idempotents.

Remarks

  1. Relation to the model algebra. Under the matrix model the above is the eigenvalue decomposition of a $2\times2$ complex matrix with distinct eigenvalues: $\lambda_\pm$ are the eigenvalues, $\mathsf{M}_2(\tilde\Pi_\pm)$ are the rank-one eigenprojections, and the formulas for $\tilde\Pi_\pm$ are the Lagrange–Sylvester formulas. The biquaternion-specific content is the identification of the eigenprojections with the idempotents of a pure root of $+1$, and of the eigenvalue data with the scalar part and the complex angle along $\vec U$.

  2. Centrality and the Peirce decomposition. The idempotents $\tilde\Pi_\pm$ are noncentral: they commute with $\tilde Q$ and with its centraliser, but not with all of $\mathbb{B}$. There is no nontrivial central idempotent in $\mathbb{B}$, since the algebra is simple (§The Two-Sided Ideals: Simplicity of $\mathbb{B}$ of Ideals and Peirce Decomposition of the Biquaternion Algebra). The decomposition is therefore a Peirce decomposition relative to an element-dependent idempotent, not a decomposition of the algebra into ideals.

  3. Uniqueness and labelling. The decomposition is canonical only as the unordered set of pairs $\{(\lambda_+,\tilde\Pi_+),(\lambda_-,\tilde\Pi_-)\}$. The labelling is interchanged by $B\mapsto-B$ and $\vec\Xi\mapsto-\vec\Xi$; it is fixed by the convention of §The Eigenvalue Decomposition exactly when $B$ can be taken real and positive.

  4. Degenerate regimes. The eigenvalue decomposition is a property of semisimple elements; the number of its terms is the number of distinct eigenvalues. The zero-divisor regime is semisimple with one eigenvalue zero and keeps a two-term decomposition. The nilpotent regime is non-semisimple and admits none. The scalar regime is semisimple with a repeated eigenvalue: the general decomposition has one term, and any two-projector refinement is non-unique.

Summary

For a biquaternion with $B\neq0$, the eigenvalue decomposition is the writing $$ \tilde Q=\lambda_+\tilde\Pi_++\lambda_-\tilde\Pi_-, \qquad \lambda_\pm=Q_t\pm iB=Q_t\pm i\sqrt{Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2}, \qquad \tilde\Pi_\pm=\frac12(e_t\pm\vec\Xi), $$ where $B^2=Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2$, with $B=|\vec Q|$ in the real case, and $\vec\Xi=-i\vec Q/B$ is the pure root of $+1$ of the vector part. The eigenvalues are distinct, the idempotents are primitive, orthogonal and complete, and they are the eigenprojections. The decomposition is unique up to the swap of the two labels.

Regimes

The zero-divisor row is a subcase of the distinct row, separated because one eigenvalue vanishes; the scalar row includes $\tilde Q=0$. In the nilpotent row, $\tilde Q$ is a nonzero nilpotent exactly when $Q_t=0$.

Regime Condition Spectrum Minimal polynomial Eigenvalue decomposition
Distinct, not a zero divisor $B\neq0$, $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}\neq0$ $\{\lambda_+,\lambda_-\}$, distinct $(X-Q_t)^2+B^2$ $\tilde Q=\lambda_+\tilde\Pi_+ + \lambda_-\tilde\Pi_-$
Zero divisor $B\neq0$, $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=0$ $\{\lambda_\sigma,\lambda_{-\sigma}\}=\{2Q_t,0\}$ $X(X-2Q_t)$ $\tilde Q=2Q_t\tilde\Pi_\sigma + 0\cdot\tilde\Pi_{-\sigma}$
Scalar $\vec Q=0$ $\{Q_t\}$ $X-Q_t$ $\tilde Q=Q_t e_t$; refinement $Q_t\tilde\Pi_1 + Q_t\tilde\Pi_2$
Nilpotent $B=0$, $\vec Q\neq0$ $\{Q_t\}$ $(X-Q_t)^2$ none

The Peirce decomposition relative to $\tilde\Pi_+$ has corners $\mathbb{B}_{st}=\tilde\Pi_s\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_t$. Its diagonal part is the centraliser $\mathbb{C}[\tilde Q]=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{e_t,\tilde Q\}$, and $\tilde Q$ has the diagonal coordinates $\lambda_\pm$ and zero mixed coordinates. The mixed corners are the directions that fail to commute with $\tilde Q$. The polynomial calculus, the inverse and the parity-matched lattice all follow from the decomposition. The eigenvalue map on the unit group is surjective but not injective, and it is not a group homomorphism. The idempotents are Hermitian exactly when $\vec U$ is real, and the decomposition is then the spectral theorem.
