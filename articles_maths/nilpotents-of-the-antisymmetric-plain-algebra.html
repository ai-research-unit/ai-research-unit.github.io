Nilpotents of the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra

Introduction

The nilpotent is defined by an equation in which the product occurs, so it is a class of the product and not of the space (Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures, §The Nilpotent). The block of this article is the antisymmetric plain multiplication $$ \tilde P\wedge\tilde Q=\tfrac12\bigl(\tilde P\tilde Q-\tilde Q\tilde P\bigr)=\mathbf{P}\times\mathbf{Q}, $$ the cross product of the two vector parts, which is alternating (Introduction to the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra of Biquaternions). The article determines its nilpotent class and states the two coincidences the vanishing square produces.

The answer is total. The square of every element of the block is zero, $\tilde P\wedge\tilde P=0$, so every element of the space is a nilpotent: the origin is the trivial one, of index one, and every other element is of index two, its square vanishing while it does not. The nilpotent class is the whole space, and the nonzero nilpotents are the punctured space; the class coincides with the zero-divisor set and with the root set of $0$, and the implication nilpotent implies zero divisor holds for the block in the total form, with the origin as its only excluded element. The conjugations preserve the class, the remarkable subspaces inherit it, and the comparison with the parent product is the comparison of the whole space with the pure isotropic cone. The two companion articles are Zero Divisors of the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra and Square Roots of Minus One, Zero and Plus One in the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra, and the parent class is Nilpotents of the General Plain Algebra.

The Notion and Its Product

Definition (nilpotent of a product). Let $\star$ be a product on $\mathbb{B}$. An element $\tilde\Upsilon$ is nilpotent of $\star$ when some power of it vanishes, $$ \tilde\Upsilon^{\star k}=0\quad\text{for some }k\geq1, $$ the power being the left-nested reading $\tilde\Upsilon^{\star k}=\bigl(\cdots\bigl((\tilde\Upsilon\star\tilde\Upsilon)\star\tilde\Upsilon\bigr)\cdots\star\tilde\Upsilon\bigr)$ when the product is not associative. The least such $k$ is the index of nilpotency, and an element of index two, $\tilde\Upsilon\star\tilde\Upsilon=0$ with $\tilde\Upsilon\neq0$, is a square-zero element of $\star$. The origin is the trivial nilpotent, of index one (Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures, §The Nilpotent).

Throughout this article the product is the antisymmetric plain multiplication $\wedge$, and unqualified, nilpotent means a nilpotent of $\wedge$. The nilpotent is the root of $0$ in the sense of the definitions article; the general square and the powers of a non-associative product are The Symmetric and Antisymmetric Parts of an Algebra Product, and the nilpotents of the parent plain product, to which the block is compared, are Nilpotents of the General Plain Algebra.

The Criterion

Theorem (every element is a nilpotent). For every element $\tilde\Upsilon$, $$ \tilde\Upsilon\wedge\tilde\Upsilon=0 , $$ and consequently every element of $\mathbb{B}$ is a nilpotent of the block: the origin is of index one and every other element is of index two.

Proof. The block is alternating, $\tilde X\wedge\tilde X=0$ for every $\tilde X$, by Introduction to the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra of Biquaternions. Hence the second power of every element vanishes. An element whose square vanishes has every higher power zero, the powers being read from the square, so the index of a nonzero element is exactly two; the origin is the only element whose first power vanishes, and its index is one.

The criterion is the alternation of the product read as an element statement. There is no stratification by index: the index is one at the origin and two everywhere else, and no element of the block is of index three or more, because the square has already vanished.

The Nilpotent Class

Proposition (the class is the whole space). The nilpotent set of the block is the whole space, and the nonzero nilpotents are the punctured space: $$ \{\tilde\Upsilon:\tilde\Upsilon\text{ nilpotent of }\wedge\}=\mathbb{B},\qquad \{\tilde\Upsilon\neq0:\tilde\Upsilon\text{ nilpotent}\}=\mathbb{B}\setminus\{0\}. $$

Proof. The theorem gives the vanishing of the square at every element, so every element satisfies the defining condition with $k=2$ (the origin with $k=1$). The nonzero nilpotents are obtained by excluding the origin.

The class is as large as the space allows, and it is stable under every operation that preserves the product: the sum of two nilpotents is a nilpotent, the scalar multiple of a nilpotent is a nilpotent, and the bracket of two nilpotents is a nilpotent, all three being elements of the space and every element being a nilpotent. The class is therefore the whole space and not a proper subset of it.

The Relation to the Zero Divisors and to the Roots of Zero

Proposition (the three classes coincide). The nonzero nilpotents, the zero divisors and the roots of $0$ are one and the same set, $$ \{\text{nonzero nilpotents}\}=\{\text{zero divisors}\}=\{\text{roots of }0\}=\mathbb{B}\setminus\{0\}. $$

Proof. The nonzero nilpotents are the punctured space by the previous proposition; the zero divisors are the punctured space by Zero Divisors of the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra, because the block is alternating and every nonzero element is its own annihilating partner; and the roots of $0$ are the punctured space by Square Roots of Minus One, Zero and Plus One in the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra. The three descriptions select one set.

Corollary (the implication nilpotent implies zero divisor). For the block the implication holds in the total form: every nonzero nilpotent is a two-sided zero divisor, with itself as the annihilating partner; the origin is the only nilpotent that is not a zero divisor, and it is excluded by the definition of the zero divisor, which requires both the element and its partner to be nonzero.

Proof. A nonzero nilpotent $\tilde\Upsilon$ satisfies $\tilde\Upsilon\wedge\tilde\Upsilon=0$, and the element $\tilde\Upsilon$ itself is a nonzero partner for both sides; the element is therefore a two-sided zero divisor. The origin satisfies $0\wedge0=0$ and is not a zero divisor, the definition requiring nonzero elements.

The implication is one of the rows of the dictionary of the four general products, where it holds for the plain product and the natural product and fails for the plain sesquilinear product (Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures, §9; The Zero Divisors and the Twelve Structures). For the block it holds without exception on the punctured space, and the exception is the origin alone.

The Conjugations and the Remarkable Subspaces

Proposition (the conjugations preserve the class). The nilpotent class of the block is preserved by the bar, the natural conjugation and the star; the conjugates of a nilpotent are nilpotents.

Proof. For the block the statement is immediate from the criterion, every element being a nilpotent, so every conjugate is one; the compatibility of each conjugation with the bracket is recorded here for the comparison with the other products, where the class is proper and the preservation is a computation. The bar is an automorphism of the block, $\overline{\tilde P\wedge\tilde Q}=\bar{\tilde P}\wedge\bar{\tilde Q}$, so it carries a vanishing square to a vanishing square. The natural conjugation and the star are anti-automorphisms of the plain product and they reverse the bracket, $\tilde P^{\natural}\wedge\tilde Q^{\natural}=-(\tilde P\wedge\tilde Q)^{\natural}$ and likewise for the star, so they carry a vanishing square to the negative of a vanishing square, which vanishes as well.

Proposition (the restriction to the remarkable subspaces). On each of the remarkable subspaces the restriction of the block is again alternating, so every element of every subspace is a nilpotent of the restriction.

Proof. The square of an element of the subspace vanishes as the square of an element of the ambient algebra vanishes, and the subspace is stable under the block only where the operation stays inside it; the criterion applies to the elements in every case. The stable subspaces and their readings are Remarkable Subspaces under the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra of Biquaternions.

The Comparison with the Parent Product

Remark (the parent nilpotents). The parent general plain bilinear product is associative and unital, and its nilpotents are the pure elements with isotropic vector part, $$ \tilde\Upsilon^{2}=0\iff P_0=0\ \text{and}\ (\mathbf P,\mathbf P)=0 $$ (Nilpotents of the General Plain Algebra). The parent's nilpotent class is therefore a proper cone of the space, of complex dimension two, and the implication nilpotent implies zero divisor holds there with the pure isotropic cone as the set. The block is the antisymmetric half of the parent, and the comparison is the comparison of a proper cone with the whole space: the block's nilpotents strictly contain the parent's, the element $e_0$ being a nilpotent of the block ($e_0\wedge e_0=0$) and not of the parent ($e_0^{2}=e_0$). The enlargement is the removal of the scalar part: the parent's criterion is a condition on the square, and the block's square is identically zero, so the condition is satisfied by every element.

Summary

For the antisymmetric plain multiplication $\tilde P\wedge\tilde Q=\mathbf{P}\times\mathbf{Q}$ the square of every element vanishes, so every element is a nilpotent: the origin of index one and every other element of index two. The nilpotent class is the whole space, the nonzero nilpotents are the punctured space, and the class coincides with the zero-divisor set and with the root set of $0$, the three descriptions selecting one set. The implication nilpotent implies zero divisor holds in the total form, the origin being the only nilpotent excluded by the definition of the zero divisor; the conjugations preserve the class and every remarkable subspace inherits it. The comparison with the parent product is the comparison of the whole space with the pure isotropic cone, and the enlargement is the removal of the scalar part from the square.
