# __Where the Rotor Leaves $M_n(\mathbb{R})$: the Even Subalgebra and $Cl(n)$__

## Introduction

**The rotor of a rotation in $n$ dimensions is an element of a Clifford algebra, and from $n=7$ that algebra has more dimensions than $M_n(\mathbb{R})$ has: the rotor stops being an $n\times n$ matrix.** The rotor acts on a vector by two multiplications, $X\mapsto RX\widetilde{R}$, and the product lands back in the $n$-dimensional space. What does not survive is the reading of $R$ itself as a matrix of size $n$.

The article builds $Cl(n)$ from the relations $e_ie_j+e_je_i=-2\delta_{ij}$, isolates the even subalgebra $Cl^0(n)$ of dimension $2^{n-1}$, identifies the bivectors with the skew matrices, defines the rotor group and counts its map onto $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ as two-to-one, then compares $2^{n-1}$ with $n^2$ and $n$ with the size of the smallest faithful module. It assumes *The Matrix Algebra $M_n(\mathbb{R})$* and *The Involutions of the First Kind: Transpose and Symplectic*, and it defers the exponential of a skew matrix to *The Exponential of the Antisymmetric Part: One-Parameter Rotations*.

## The Clifford Algebra

**Definition.** $Cl(n)$ is the associative algebra with unit generated by $e_1,\dots,e_n$ subject to

$$
e_ie_j+e_je_i=-2\delta_{ij},\qquad i,j=1,\dots,n .
$$

So $e_i^2=-1$ and $e_ie_j=-e_je_i$ for $i\ne j$.

**The basis.** A word $e_{i_1}\cdots e_{i_k}$ reduces, by the relations and by $e_i^2=-1$, to $\pm e_S$ with $S=\{i_1<\dots<i_k\}$, and $e_\emptyset=1$. The product of two basis elements is a basis element up to sign,

$$
e_Se_T=\varepsilon(S,T)\,e_{S\triangle T},\qquad \varepsilon(S,T)=\pm1,
$$

so the $2^n$ elements $e_S$ are independent and $\dim Cl(n)=2^n$.

**Reversal.** Reversal is the linear map fixed on $1$ and on the generators with

$$
\widetilde{e_S}=(-1)^{k(k-1)/2}e_S,\qquad \widetilde{AB}=\widetilde{B}\widetilde{A}.
$$

For a product of $k$ vectors, $\widetilde{u_1\cdots u_k}=u_k\cdots u_1$; in particular $\widetilde{e_ie_j}=-e_ie_j$.

## The Grading and the Even Subalgebra

**The algebra is $\mathbb{Z}/2$-graded by the length of the word.** Write $Cl^0(n)$ for the span of the $e_S$ with $|S|$ even and $Cl^1(n)$ for the span with $|S|$ odd. Since $Cl^rCl^s\subseteq Cl^{r+s}$, the even part is a subalgebra and the odd part is a module over it.

**The even part has half the dimension.** The map $S\mapsto S\triangle\{n\}$ pairs each even subset with an odd one, so $\dim Cl^0(n)=2^{n-1}$.

**Theorem.** $Cl^0(n)$ is isomorphic to $Cl(n-1)$.

*Proof.* Put $f_i=e_ie_n$ for $i<n$. Because $e_n$ anticommutes with $e_i$ and $e_j$ and $e_n^2=-1$,

$$
f_if_j+f_jf_i=e_ie_ne_je_n+e_je_ne_ie_n=e_ie_j+e_je_i=-2\delta_{ij}.
$$

So the $f_i$ obey the relations of $n-1$ generators, and they generate the even part, since $e_ie_j=-f_if_j$ for $i,j<n$ and $e_ie_n=f_i$. Hence $Cl^0(n)\cong Cl(n-1)$.

**The dimension $2^{n-1}$ is the source of every later count.** It grows exponentially while $n^2$ grows quadratically, and the crossing of the two is the subject of the last three sections.

## Bivectors and Skew Matrices

**The bivectors are the degree-two elements.** For $i<j$ put $e_{ij}=e_ie_j$. The $\binom n2$ bivectors are a basis of the degree-two part, of dimension $n(n-1)/2=\dim\operatorname{Skew}_n$. For $A\in\operatorname{Skew}_n$ define

$$
\omega(A)=\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}\,e_{ij}.
$$

**Theorem (the commutator).** For $A,B\in\operatorname{Skew}_n$,

$$
[\omega(A),\omega(B)]=-2\,\omega([A,B]),
$$

where the bracket on the left is $XY-YX$ in $Cl(n)$ and the bracket on the right is $AB-BA$.

*Proof.* It is enough to check one pair of indices. With $e_{12}=e_1e_2$ and $e_{23}=e_2e_3$,

$$
e_{12}e_{23}=e_1e_2e_2e_3=-e_{13},\qquad e_{23}e_{12}=e_2e_3e_1e_2=e_{13},
$$

so $[e_{12},e_{23}]=-2e_{13}$, and $[E_{12}-E_{21},E_{23}-E_{32}]=E_{13}-E_{31}$. The general case is the same computation on each pair of indices, and both sides are bilinear.

**Corollary.** The map $A\mapsto-\tfrac12\omega(A)$ is an isomorphism of Lie algebras from $\operatorname{Skew}_n$ to the bivectors under the commutator, and $\dim\operatorname{Skew}_n=n(n-1)/2=\dim\mathfrak{so}(n)$. The factor $-\tfrac12$ is the normalisation that makes the bivector act as the skew matrix in the spin representation. Numerically the identity $[\omega(A),\omega(B)]=-2\omega([A,B])$ holds to $10^{-16}$ at $n=4$ on random skew pairs.

## The Rotor Group

**Definition.** A **rotor** is an even element $R\in Cl^0(n)$ with $R\widetilde{R}=1$. The rotors form a group: if $R\widetilde R=1$ and $S\widetilde S=1$ then $(RS)\widetilde{(RS)}=RS\widetilde S\widetilde R=1$, and $R^{-1}=\widetilde R$.

**Example.** For a bivector $B$ the element $R=\exp(B/2)$ is a rotor, because $\widetilde{B}=-B$ gives $\widetilde{R}=\exp(-B/2)=R^{-1}$. At $n=3$ this is $\cos(\theta/2)+\sin(\theta/2)e_{12}$ for $B=-\theta e_{12}$, a unit quaternion.

**Theorem (the rotor acts by an isometry).** For a rotor $R$ the map $\rho_R(X)=RX\widetilde R$ sends the odd part to itself and preserves the length.

*Proof.* An even element on both sides of an odd one gives an odd element, so $\rho_R$ maps $Cl^1(n)$ to itself. For $X\in Cl^1(n)$ one has $X^2=-|X|^2$, and then

$$
(RX\widetilde R)^2=RXR^{-1}RXR^{-1}=RX^2R^{-1}=-|X|^2,
$$

so the image is a vector of the same length. The map is linear and injective, hence orthogonal.

**The map lands in $\mathrm{SO}(n)$.** Write $R=u_1\cdots u_{2k}$ as a product of an even number of unit vectors; such products generate $Cl^0(n)$ and give every rotor. Then $\rho_R$ is the composition of the $2k$ maps $X\mapsto-u_iXu_i$, each the reflection in the hyperplane orthogonal to $u_i$, each of determinant $-1$. Hence $\det\rho_R=(-1)^{2k}=1$.

## Two-to-One onto $\mathrm{SO}(n)$

**Theorem.** The map $\rho:R\mapsto\rho_R$ is a surjective homomorphism from the rotors onto $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ with kernel $\{\pm1\}$.

*Proof.* It is a homomorphism because $\rho_{RS}=\rho_R\rho_S$. The kernel is the set of rotors with $RX=XR$ for all odd $X$; such an $R$ is central in $Cl(n)$, and the even part of the centre is $\mathbb{R}$, so $R=\lambda$ with $\lambda^2=1$, that is $R=\pm1$. For surjectivity, every rotation is a product of an even number of reflections, each reflection is $\rho_u$ for a unit vector $u$, and $\rho$ is a homomorphism, so the image contains $\mathrm{SO}(n)$.

**The count.** The kernel has two elements, so each rotation has exactly two rotors, $R$ and $-R$. At $n=3$ this is the familiar pair $q$, $-q$ of unit quaternions giving one rotation, and numerically the two produce the same $3\times3$ matrix to machine precision.

**A caveat about the centre.** The kernel is $\{\pm1\}$ for every $n$, but the centre of the rotor group is larger for even $n$: the volume element $\omega=e_1\cdots e_n$ is then even, it is a rotor, it commutes with every even element, and it satisfies $\omega X=-X\omega$ on vectors. So $\omega$ maps to $-\mathrm{I}$ and not to the identity: a larger centre does not enlarge the kernel, and the map stays two-to-one.

## Where the Rotor Leaves $M_n(\mathbb{R})$

**The comparison is $2^{n-1}$ against $n^2$.** A rotor lies in $Cl^0(n)$; a matrix lies in $M_n(\mathbb{R})$, of dimension $n^2$. An injective linear map $Cl^0(n)\to M_n(\mathbb{R})$ would need $2^{n-1}\le n^2$, and the values are

$$
\begin{array}{c|ccccccc}
n & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & 7 & 8\\ \hline
2^{n-1} & 2 & 4 & 8 & 16 & 32 & 64 & 128\\
n^2 & 4 & 9 & 16 & 25 & 36 & 49 & 64
\end{array}
$$

**The crossing is at $n=7$.** The inequality holds for $n=2,\dots,6$ and fails at $n=7$, where $64>49$, and at every larger $n$, because the left side doubles at each step while the right side adds $2n+1$. For $n\ge7$ the even subalgebra is strictly larger than the matrix algebra, and no faithful representation of it on $\mathbb{R}^n$ exists.

**The count is necessary but not sharp.** At $n=5$ it gives $16<25$ and at $n=6$ it gives $32<36$, so the dimension alone does not exclude a representation. The sharper obstruction is the module.

## The Module Count

**The rotor must act somewhere.** A faithful representation of $Cl^0(n)$ is a real space on which the algebra acts faithfully, and the smallest such space is the spinor space. The classification of the real Clifford algebras gives, for $n\le8$,

$$
\begin{array}{c|ccccccc}
n & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & 7 & 8\\ \hline
Cl^0(n) & \mathbb{C} & \mathbb{H} & \mathbb{H}^2 & M_2(\mathbb{H}) & M_4(\mathbb{C}) & M_8(\mathbb{R}) & M_8(\mathbb{R})^2\\
\text{module} & 2 & 4 & 8 & 8 & 8 & 8 & 16
\end{array}
$$

**From $n=5$ the module is bigger than $\mathbb{R}^n$.** At $n=5$ the algebra is $M_2(\mathbb{H})$, whose unique simple module is $\mathbb{H}^2$ of real dimension $8>5$. At $n=6$ it is $M_4(\mathbb{C})$, with simple module $\mathbb{C}^4$ of real dimension $8>6$. At $n=7$ it is $M_8(\mathbb{R})$ with module $\mathbb{R}^8$, and $8>7$. Equality with $n$ occurs only at $n=2$, where $\mathbb{C}$ acts on $\mathbb{R}^2$ by rotations.

## The Matrix Realisation at $n\le4$

**Small $n$, small matrices.** For $n\le4$ the even subalgebra is a matrix algebra over $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$ or $\mathbb{H}$ of size at most two: $\mathbb{C}$ at $n=2$, $\mathbb{H}$ at $n=3$, $\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}$ at $n=4$. In each case the rotor is an ordinary matrix of bounded size.

**At $n=2$ the rotor is a complex number.** $Cl^0(2)=\mathbb{C}$ with basis $1,e_{12}$ and $e_{12}^2=-1$. A rotor is $R=a+be_{12}$ with $a^2+b^2=1$, and $R$ acts as the rotation with $\cos\theta=a^2-b^2$ and $\sin\theta=2ab$. The pair $\pm R$ gives one rotation, and $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ is the circle.

**At $n=3$ the rotor is a quaternion.** $Cl^0(3)=\mathbb{H}$ with $i=e_{23}$, $j=e_{31}$, $k=e_{12}$. The rotor group is the unit sphere $S^3$, which covers $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ two-to-one, and $\rho_R$ is conjugation on the imaginary quaternions; see *Quaternion Algebra*.

**At $n=4$ the rotor is a pair of quaternions.** $Cl^0(4)=\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}$, the rotor group is $S^3\times S^3$ of dimension $6$, and the action is $x\mapsto q_+xq_-^{-1}$ on $\mathbb{R}^4=\mathbb{H}$. The two-to-one cover is the pair $(q_+,q_-)\mapsto(-q_+,-q_-)$.

**A numerical anchor at $n=3$.** With $R=\exp(-\theta e_{12}/2)$ and $\theta=0.7$, the rotor satisfies $R\widetilde R=1$ and sends $e_1$ to $\cos\theta\,e_1-\sin\theta\,e_2$, with no four-vector part: the image stays in the vector space, as the theorem requires.

## The Two Constructions of the Spin Group

**The first runs through the whole algebra.** Let $\mathrm{Pin}(n)$ be the group generated by the unit vectors of $Cl(n)$, that is by the $u$ with $u^2=-1$. Each such $u$ is invertible with $u^{-1}=-u$, and $X\mapsto-uXu$ is the reflection in the hyperplane orthogonal to $u$. A product of $k$ unit vectors acts as $k$ reflections, of determinant $(-1)^k$.

**The second runs through the even part.** Put $\mathrm{Spin}(n)=\{R\in Cl^0(n):R\widetilde R=1\}$, the rotor group. It is the even part of $\mathrm{Pin}(n)$: a product of an even number of unit vectors is even, and every such product is a rotor.

**The two agree.** The intersection $\mathrm{Pin}(n)\cap Cl^0(n)$ is $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$, and the map $\mathrm{Pin}(n)\to\mathrm{O}(n)$ restricts to $\mathrm{Spin}(n)\to\mathrm{SO}(n)$ with kernel $\{\pm1\}$. Both groups have dimension $n(n-1)/2$ and are connected for $n\ge2$, so $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is the double cover of $\mathrm{SO}(n)$, and the universal cover because $\pi_1(\mathrm{SO}(n))=\mathbb{Z}/2$ for $n\ge3$.

## Summary

The Clifford algebra $Cl(n)$ is generated by $e_1,\dots,e_n$ with $e_ie_j+e_je_i=-2\delta_{ij}$ and has dimension $2^n$; its even part $Cl^0(n)$ has dimension $2^{n-1}$ and is isomorphic to $Cl(n-1)$. The bivectors $e_{ij}=e_ie_j$ span a space of dimension $n(n-1)/2=\dim\operatorname{Skew}_n$, and the Clifford commutator is $-2$ times the matrix commutator, $[\omega(A),\omega(B)]=-2\omega([A,B])$. The rotors are the even elements with $R\widetilde R=1$; they act by $X\mapsto RX\widetilde R$ as rotations of determinant one, and the map onto $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is two-to-one with kernel $\{\pm1\}$, though for even $n$ the volume element is a rotor in the centre. The rotor is a matrix of size $n$ only while the counts allow it: $2^{n-1}\le n^2$ holds up to $n=6$ and fails from $n=7$, and the smallest faithful module exceeds $n$ from $n=5$. Up to $n=4$ the even algebra is $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{H}$ or $\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}$, and the rotor is a complex number, a quaternion or a pair of quaternions.

## Summary of Notation

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $Cl(n)$ | The Clifford algebra of $\mathbb{R}^n$ with $e_i^2=-1$ |
| $Cl^0(n)$ | Its even subalgebra, of dimension $2^{n-1}$ |
| $e_S$ | The basis element of the subset $S$ |
| $e_{ij}=e_ie_j$ | The bivector of the pair $i<j$ |
| $\omega(A)$ | The bivector $\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}e_{ij}$ of $A\in\operatorname{Skew}_n$ |
| $\widetilde R$ | The reversal of $R$ |
| $R$ | A rotor: even, with $R\widetilde R=1$ |
| $\rho_R(X)=RX\widetilde R$ | The rotation of $\mathbb{R}^n$ defined by $R$ |
| $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ | The rotor group, of dimension $n(n-1)/2$ |
| $\omega$ | The volume element $e_1\cdots e_n$ |
