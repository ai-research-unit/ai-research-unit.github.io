Annihilators of the Symmetric Plain Algebra

Introduction

The zero divisor of a product is a property of one element alone: it says that some nonzero partner exists that the element kills. The annihilator says which partners those are, and it is the finer object. It is the set of elements killed by a fixed element, $$ A_\star(\tilde p)=\{\tilde q:\tilde p\star\tilde q=0\},\qquad B_\star(\tilde p)=\{\tilde q:\tilde q\star\tilde p=0\}, $$ the left and the right annihilator. When the product is carried by a multiplication operator the annihilator is the kernel of that operator, so it is a linear subspace.

The block of this article is the symmetric plain multiplication $$ \tilde p\bullet\tilde q=\tfrac12\bigl(\tilde p\tilde q+\tilde q\tilde p\bigr) =(p_0q_0-\vec p\cdot\vec q)e_0+p_0\vec q+q_0\vec p, $$ the Jordan product (Introduction to the Symmetric Plain Algebra of Quaternions). It is commutative, so the left and the right multiplication by an element are one operator and the two annihilators of the element are one set. The zero-divisor set of the block is the vector subspace with the origin removed, the single pure family of the criterion (Zero Divisors of the Symmetric Plain Algebra, above this article in this category). The annihilator reads that family: a nonzero pure element annihilates a real plane, a nonzero non-pure element annihilates nothing, and the origin alone annihilates the whole space. The article owns that reading, the operator with its rank and its determinant, and the shape of the annihilator on the remarkable subspaces; the zero-divisor set and its criterion are the cited article's.

The distinction from the structure-level article. The chapter also carries Annihilators in the Algebraic Structures over the Quaternion $\mathbb{R}$ Space (Comparison of the Algebraic Structures over the Quaternion $\mathbb{R}$ Space), where the object is the set of elements that kill the whole space for a fixed product, that is the radical; the present article instead fixes the element and reads the set it kills, which is the finer question and the one the zero divisors ask.

The Annihilator of a Product

Definition (annihilator). Let $\star$ be a product on $\mathbb{H}$ and let $\tilde q\in\mathbb{H}$. The left annihilator and the right annihilator of $\tilde q$ in $\star$ are $$ A_\star(\tilde q)=\{\tilde x:\tilde q\star\tilde x=0\},\qquad B_\star(\tilde q)=\{\tilde x:\tilde x\star\tilde q=0\}. $$ They are the kernels of the left and the right multiplication, and they are nonzero exactly when $\tilde q$ is a zero divisor of the corresponding side.

Throughout this article the product is the symmetric plain multiplication $\bullet$, and unqualified, annihilator means the annihilator of $\bullet$. The left and the right multiplication of the block are the operator $$ L^{\bullet}_{\tilde p}\tilde q=\tilde p\bullet\tilde q,\qquad R^{\bullet}_{\tilde p}\tilde q=\tilde q\bullet\tilde p, $$ and the block is the operation $\mathrm{SPA}$ of The Algebraic Structures over the Quaternion $\mathbb{R}$ Space. It is the symmetric half of the plain product, $\mathrm{GPA}=\mathrm{SPA}+\mathrm{APA}$, and the plain product is a division algebra, where no nonzero element annihilates anything; the block is where that changes.

Remark (only the annihilator, not the ideal). The annihilator of a zero divisor is a subspace, not a two-sided ideal: the plane of a pure element does not contain $e_0$, and it is not carried into itself by the product with an arbitrary element. It is the vanishing of the operator, not an algebraic ideal, that the block carries, and the block has no proper ideal because the ambient algebra is simple.

The Two Annihilators Coincide

Proposition (the two annihilators coincide). For every $\tilde p$, $$ B_\bullet(\tilde p)=A_\bullet(\tilde p)=\ker L^{\bullet}_{\tilde p}. $$

Proof. The block is commutative, $\tilde q\bullet\tilde p=\tilde p\bullet\tilde q$, so $\tilde q\bullet\tilde p=0$ exactly when $\tilde p\bullet\tilde q=0$; the two annihilators are therefore one set, and it is the kernel of the one operator of the block, $L^{\bullet}_{\tilde p}=R^{\bullet}_{\tilde p}$ (The Multiplication Operators of the Symmetric Plain Algebra, below this article in this category).

The Annihilator of an Element

The criterion of the block is the single clause $p_0=0$: a nonzero element is a zero divisor exactly when it is pure, equivalently when its annihilator is nonzero (Zero Divisors of the Symmetric Plain Algebra, §The Criterion). The annihilator of a pure element is the following.

Theorem (the annihilator of a pure element). For a nonzero pure element $\tilde p=\vec p$, $$ A_\bullet(\vec p)=B_\bullet(\vec p) =\{\tilde q=q_0e_0+\vec q:\ q_0=0,\ \vec p\cdot\vec q=0\}, $$ the plane of the vector subspace orthogonal to $\vec p$, of real dimension two.

Proof. For a pure element, $\vec p\bullet\tilde q=-(\vec p\cdot\vec q)e_0+q_0\vec p$, which vanishes exactly when $q_0=0$ and $\vec p\cdot\vec q=0$. In the three-dimensional vector subspace the single equation $\vec p\cdot\vec q=0$ has a solution space of real dimension two.

Remark (the element is not its own partner). The square of a pure element is $\vec p\bullet\vec p=-|\vec p|^2e_0$, which is nonzero for $\vec p\neq0$. The element is therefore not orthogonal to itself, and a nonzero pure element does not lie in its own annihilator; the line $\mathbb{R}\vec p$ meets the plane only at the origin.

Corollary (the annihilator vanishes off the pure elements). For a nonzero element that is not pure the annihilator is $\{0\}$: the element is regular, and indeed a unit.

Proof. A nonzero element with $p_0\neq0$ has no nonzero partner, by the criterion of the cited article; equivalently its multiplication operator has determinant $p_0^2N(\tilde p)\neq0$, as the next section computes.

The Multiplication Operator and the Rank

Proposition (the operator and its invariants). In the basis $e_0,e_1,e_2,e_3$ the operator $L^{\bullet}_{\tilde p}$ of an element $\tilde p=p_0e_0+\vec p$ has matrix $$ \begin{pmatrix} p_0 & -p_1 & -p_2 & -p_3 \\ p_1 & p_0 & 0 & 0 \\ p_2 & 0 & p_0 & 0 \\ p_3 & 0 & 0 & p_0 \end{pmatrix}, $$ its trace is $4p_0$ and its determinant is $$ \det L^{\bullet}_{\tilde p}=p_0^2\bigl(p_0^2+|\vec p|^2\bigr)=p_0^2N(\tilde p). $$

Proof. The columns are the values $\tilde p\bullet e_j$; the first is $(p_0,p_1,p_2,p_3)$, and the others are $(-p_1,p_0,0,0)$, $(-p_2,0,p_0,0)$ and $(-p_3,0,0,p_0)$. The trace is the sum of the diagonal entries, $4p_0$. For $p_0\neq0$ the determinant is the product of the three eigenvalues $p_0$ of the vector block with the Schur complement $p_0+|\vec p|^2/p_0$, namely $p_0^3(p_0+|\vec p|^2/p_0)=p_0^2(p_0^2+|\vec p|^2)$; the identity is polynomial in the coordinates, so it holds at $p_0=0$ as well.

Corollary (the rank and the annihilator). The rank of $L^{\bullet}_{\tilde p}$ and the annihilator of $\tilde p$ are

$\tilde p$ $\operatorname{rank}L^{\bullet}_{\tilde p}$ $\dim\ker L^{\bullet}_{\tilde p}$ $\ker L^{\bullet}_{\tilde p}$
$0$ $0$ $4$ $\mathbb{H}$
$\tilde p\neq0$, $p_0=0$ $2$ $2$ $\{\tilde q:q_0=0,\ \vec p\cdot\vec q=0\}$
$p_0\neq0$ $4$ $0$ $\{0\}$

Proof. A nonzero pure element has $p_0=0$: its first column is $\vec p$ and the other three columns are the multiples $-p_ke_0$ of the scalar direction, so the columns span $\mathbb{R}e_0\oplus\mathbb{R}\vec p$, of dimension two, and the rank is two. The kernel is the plane of the pure elements orthogonal to $\vec p$, of real dimension two. A non-pure element has $p_0\neq0$: the determinant $p_0^2N(\tilde p)$ is nonzero because the norm $N(\tilde p)=p_0^2+|\vec p|^2$ is positive definite, so the rank is four and the kernel is $\{0\}$. At the origin the matrix is zero, of rank zero and kernel the whole space.

The Dichotomy

The zero-divisor set of the block has one criterion and one family, and the annihilator splits the space by it: a nonzero pure element annihilates a real plane, every other nonzero element annihilates nothing, and the origin alone annihilates the whole space. The dimension of the annihilator reads the pure family where the criterion already does, and the operator reads it again: the plane is the kernel of a rank-two operator, and a unit has the empty kernel of a rank-four operator.

The key difference from the complex block. In the complex symmetric plain algebra the annihilator had a second value: the scalar family $\{Q_0\neq0,\ C(\tilde Q)=0\}$ annihilated a complex line. Over $\mathbb{R}$ that family is empty, because the central square $C(\tilde p)=p_0^2+|\vec p|^2$ vanishes only at $\tilde p=0$. The complex line therefore does not occur, and over $\mathbb{R}$ the annihilator of an element is either $\{0\}$ or a real plane, the whole space occurring only at the origin. The definiteness of the norm, which collapses the criterion to the single clause $p_0=0$, collapses the annihilator to the plane as well.

The Annihilator on the Remarkable Subspaces

Over $\mathbb{R}$ the remarkable subspaces are the scalar line, which is the centre, and the vector subspace (Introduction to the Remarkable Subspaces of the Quaternions).

The centre. A nonzero central element $\tilde p=\lambda e_0$ is not pure, so its annihilator is $\{0\}$: the centre annihilates nothing, and the unit $e_0$ in particular kills no partner.

The vector subspace. A nonzero pure element $\tilde p=\vec p$ has the plane annihilator of §The Annihilator of an Element, and that plane already lies inside the vector subspace: the product $\vec p\bullet\tilde q=-(\vec p\cdot\vec q)e_0+q_0\vec p$ vanishes exactly on the pure elements orthogonal to $\vec p$, so the annihilator is the two-plane $\vec p^{\perp}\cap\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})$ and it does not leave the subspace. The element itself is not in it, the line $\mathbb{R}\vec p$ being the orthogonal complement of that plane in the subspace.

Summary

The annihilator of an element in a product is the set of partners the element kills, the left and the right annihilator being the kernels of the left and the right multiplication. The symmetric plain multiplication is commutative, so the two annihilators of an element coincide and are the kernel of the one operator $\tilde p\bullet\tilde q=(p_0q_0-\vec p\cdot\vec q)e_0+p_0\vec q+q_0\vec p$, of trace $4p_0$ and determinant $p_0^2N(\tilde p)$. A nonzero pure element annihilates the real plane $\{\tilde q:q_0=0,\ \vec p\cdot\vec q=0\}$ inside the vector subspace, a nonzero non-pure element annihilates nothing, and the origin annihilates the whole space. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the second family of the complex block is empty: the annihilator of an element is either $\{0\}$ or a real plane, and no annihilator is a line. On the remarkable subspaces the centre annihilates nothing and the annihilator of a pure element is the orthogonal plane inside the vector subspace, which does not contain the element.
