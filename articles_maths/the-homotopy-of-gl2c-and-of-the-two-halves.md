# __The Homotopy of $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{C})$ and of the Two Halves__

## Introduction

**The group of invertible matrices has the homotopy type of the unitary group $U(2)$, which is the quotient of $S^{3}\times S^{1}$ by a two-element group, so its fundamental group is $\mathbb{Z}$ and it has the homology of a circle in degree one; the two halves are vector spaces and carry no homotopy at all.**

The article proves the homotopy equivalence with the maximal compact subgroup, computes the fundamental group from the determinant, records the higher homotopy and the homology of $U(2)$, treats the real group and its orthogonal subgroup, and shows that the halves are contractible so that their only invariants come from the components of the invertible elements inside them. It assumes *The Topology of $M_2(\mathbb{C})$*, *The Cartan Decomposition of $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{C})$* and *The Two Halves and Their Topology at $n=2$*.

It follows the biquaternion article *The Regular Module* for the corresponding statements in the biquaternion coordinates.

## The Group and Its Maximal Compact

**The unitary group is a maximal compact subgroup of the invertible group, of real dimension four, and it contains a copy of the circle of scalars.**

**Proposition.** The unitary group $U(2)$ is compact of real dimension four, and the special unitary group $SU(2)$ is closed in it of real dimension three, with $U(2)$ the product of $SU(2)$ with the circle of scalars modulo the intersection of order two.

**Proof.** A unitary matrix has four real parameters, the determinant condition removes one, and every unitary matrix is the product of a special unitary one with a scalar of unit modulus, the intersection being the two scalars $\pm I$.

**Corollary.** The special unitary group is diffeomorphic to the three-sphere.

**Proof.** A special unitary matrix has the form $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\-\bar b&\bar a\end{pmatrix}$ with $|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}=1$, which is the unit sphere in two complex coordinates.

**Corollary.** The special unitary group is a circle bundle over the two-sphere with fibre a circle, the Hopf bundle.

**Proof.** The action of the circle of scalars on the three-sphere is free, its orbits are circles, and the quotient is the two-sphere.

**Corollary.** The unitary group is a principal bundle over the circle of scalars with fibre the special unitary group, the bundle of the determinant.

**Proof.** The determinant is a homomorphism onto the circle of unit scalars, and its kernel is the special unitary group.

## The Homotopy Equivalence

**Theorem.** The inclusion of the unitary group in the invertible group is a homotopy equivalence, so both spaces have the same homotopy groups and the same homology.

**Proof.** The polar decomposition of *The Cartan Decomposition of $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{C})$* writes an invertible matrix uniquely as a unitary matrix times a positive definite Hermitian matrix, and the positive definite factor is a point of an open convex cone, which is contractible; retracting that cone to the identity gives the deformation retraction.

**Corollary.** The invertible group is homotopy equivalent to a compact manifold, and its homotopy type is that of $U(2)$.

**Proof.** The retraction is continuous in both directions, and the unitary group is compact.

**Corollary.** The invertible group has the homotopy of the quotient of $S^{3}\times S^{1}$ by the antipodal action on both factors.

**Proof.** The unitary group is that quotient by the previous section.

## The Fundamental Group

**Proposition.** The fundamental group of the invertible group is infinite cyclic, generated by the loop of scalars $e^{i\theta}I$.

**Proof.** The determinant is a continuous map onto the nonzero complex numbers, whose fundamental group is infinite cyclic, and the loop of scalars maps onto the standard generator; the polar decomposition shows that no other generator exists, because the positive directions are contractible.

**Corollary.** The fundamental group of the special unitary group is trivial, because the three-sphere is simply connected.

**Proof.** The three-sphere is simply connected, and $SU(2)$ is diffeomorphic to it.

**Corollary.** The fundamental group of the invertible group is the direct sum of the fundamental group of the special unitary group and the fundamental group of the circle of scalars.

**Proof.** The group is the quotient of the product by a finite group, which does not change the fundamental group in this case.

## Higher Homotopy and Homology

**Proposition.** The second homotopy group of the invertible group vanishes, its third homotopy group is $\mathbb{Z}$, and its fourth homotopy group is $\mathbb{Z}$.

**Proof.** The group is homotopy equivalent to $U(2)$, which fibres over the circle with fibre the three-sphere; the second homotopy group of the three-sphere vanishes, the third and the fourth are infinite cyclic, and the circle contributes nothing above the first degree.

**Corollary.** The homology is $\mathbb{Z}$ in degrees zero, one, three and four, and it vanishes in degree two.

**Proof.** The Serre spectral sequence of the bundle over the circle with fibre the three-sphere has no room for differentials, so the homology is the tensor product of the homology of the circle with that of the three-sphere.

**Corollary.** The Euler characteristic of the invertible group is zero, and its fourth Betti number is one.

**Proof.** The alternating sum of the Betti numbers is one minus one plus zero minus one plus one, which is zero.

## The Real Group

**Proposition.** The real invertible group is homotopy equivalent to the orthogonal group $O(2)$, which has two components, each a circle.

**Proof.** The real polar decomposition writes an invertible real matrix as an orthogonal matrix times a positive definite symmetric matrix, and the positive definite cone is convex; the orthogonal group consists of the rotations and the reflections, and each family is a circle.

**Corollary.** The real invertible group has two connected components, and its first homology group is the direct sum of two copies of $\mathbb{Z}$.

**Proof.** Two circles give two copies of $\mathbb{Z}$ in degree one, and the two components give two copies of $\mathbb{Z}$ in degree zero.

**Corollary.** The real invertible group has the homotopy groups of a circle in each component, and its second homotopy group vanishes.

**Proof.** A disjoint union of two circles has no nontrivial second homotopy.

## The Two Halves

**Proposition.** Each of the four halves is contractible, so its homotopy groups vanish and its homology is that of a point.

**Proof.** A half is a real vector space, and a vector space retracts to a point.

**Corollary.** The invertible elements inside the Hermitian half have the homotopy of the set of their signatures: two components, one of definite matrices and one of indefinite ones.

**Proof.** A Hermitian matrix is invertible exactly when its determinant is nonzero, and the determinant has signature $(1,3)$ on the half, so the definite set is one cone and the indefinite set one region.

**Corollary.** The invertible elements inside the symmetric half have three components, one positive definite, one negative definite and one indefinite.

**Proof.** The determinant has signature $(1,2)$ on the symmetric half, so the definite set has two cones and the indefinite set one region.

## Summary

The invertible group retracts onto the unitary group by the polar decomposition, so it has the homotopy of $U(2)$, the quotient of $S^{3}\times S^{1}$ by a two-element group. Its fundamental group is infinite cyclic, generated by the loop of scalars, and its second and third homotopy groups are infinite cyclic.

Over the real field the group retracts onto $O(2)$, with two circle components. The four halves are vector spaces, so they are contractible, and their only topology is the component count of the invertible elements inside them: three components in the symmetric half and two in the Hermitian half.
