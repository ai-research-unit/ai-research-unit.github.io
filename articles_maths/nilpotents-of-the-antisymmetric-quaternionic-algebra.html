Nilpotents of the Antisymmetric Quaternionic Algebra

Introduction

The nilpotent is defined by an equation in which the product occurs, so it is a class of the product and not of the space (Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures, §The Nilpotent). The block of this article is the antisymmetric quaternionic multiplication $$ \tilde P\diamond\tilde Q=\tfrac12\bigl(\tilde P^{\natural}\tilde Q-\tilde Q^{\natural}\tilde P\bigr) =P_0\mathbf Q-Q_0\mathbf P-\mathbf P\times\mathbf Q , $$ which is alternating and without a unit (Introduction to the Antisymmetric Quaternionic Algebra of Biquaternions). The article determines its nilpotent set and its index.

The answer is total. The block is alternating, so $\tilde\Upsilon\diamond\tilde\Upsilon=0$ for every element, and therefore every element of $\mathbb{B}$ is a nilpotent of the block, of index two when it is nonzero and of index one at the origin; the square-zero set is the punctured space, and no element has index three or more. The nilpotent set of the block is thus the whole space, the largest set a nilpotent class can be, and it strictly contains the nilpotent cone of the parent quaternionic product, which is the zero-divisor cone $c=0$ and a proper cone. The square-zero set is at once the zero-divisor set of the block and the root set of $0$, and the two companion articles are cited. The higher powers are read with the left-nested bracketing of the definitions, since the block is not associative.

The Notion and Its Product

Definition (nilpotent of a product). Let $\star$ be a product on $\mathbb{B}$. An element $\tilde\Upsilon$ is nilpotent of $\star$ when some power of it vanishes, $$ \tilde\Upsilon^{\star k}=0 \quad\text{for some } k\geq1 , $$ the power being the left-nested reading $\tilde\Upsilon^{\star k}=\bigl(\cdots\bigl((\tilde\Upsilon\star\tilde\Upsilon)\star\tilde\Upsilon\bigr) \cdots\star\tilde\Upsilon\bigr)$ when the product is not associative. The least such $k$ is the index of nilpotency, and an element of index two, $\tilde\Upsilon\star\tilde\Upsilon=0$ with $\tilde\Upsilon\neq0$, is a square-zero element of $\star$. The origin is the trivial nilpotent.

Throughout this article the product is the antisymmetric quaternionic multiplication $\diamond$, and unqualified, nilpotent means a nilpotent of the block. The nilpotents of the general plain product, to which the block is compared, are Nilpotents of the General Plain Algebra; the nilpotent cone of the parent quaternionic product is The Nilpotents and the Zero Divisors of the Quaternionic Product.

The Criterion

Theorem (the criterion for the block). Every element of $\mathbb{B}$ is a nilpotent of the antisymmetric quaternionic multiplication. Equivalently, the nilpotent set of the block is the whole space, and the square-zero set is the punctured space.

Proof. The block is alternating, $\tilde\Upsilon\diamond\tilde\Upsilon=0$ for every element $\tilde\Upsilon$, by Introduction to the Antisymmetric Quaternionic Algebra of Biquaternions; the square therefore vanishes, so the element is nilpotent of index at most two, and it is of index exactly two when it is nonzero. The set of the elements whose square vanishes is the whole space, and the square-zero set, which excludes the origin, is the same space without it.

The criterion is the alternation of the product read as an element statement, and it is met by every element, the origin included. There is no hypothesis on the element, no scalar part and no vector part to test: the equation $\tilde\Upsilon\diamond\tilde\Upsilon=0$ is an identity of the block and not a condition on $\tilde\Upsilon$.

The Index

Proposition (index two, and no higher index). For every element $\tilde\Upsilon$ and every $k\geq2$ the left-nested power vanishes, $$ \tilde\Upsilon^{\diamond k}=0 \quad (k\geq2), $$ and the index of a nonzero element is exactly two while the index of the origin is one. The block carries no nilpotent of index three or more.

Proof. The left-nested square is $\tilde\Upsilon\diamond\tilde\Upsilon=0$; the third power is $\bigl(\tilde\Upsilon\diamond\tilde\Upsilon\bigr)\diamond\tilde\Upsilon =0\diamond\tilde\Upsilon=0$, and each higher power is formed from a vanishing power and therefore vanishes again. A nonzero element has a nonvanishing first power and a vanishing second power, so its index is exactly two; the origin has vanishing first power, so its index is one.

The boundedness of the index is a second sense in which the block is extreme. In the general plain algebra the index is two or nothing, and an element is a nilpotent exactly on the pure isotropic cone; here the index is two for every nonzero element, and the membership test is vacuous. The block has the largest nilpotent set and the smallest positive index.

The Relation to the Zero Divisors

Proposition (every nonzero nilpotent is a two-sided zero divisor). Every nonzero nilpotent $\tilde\Upsilon$ of the block satisfies $\tilde\Upsilon\diamond\tilde\Upsilon=0$ on both sides, so it lies in its own left annihilator and in its own right annihilator, and it is a two-sided zero divisor with itself as the annihilating partner.

Proof. The square is zero by the criterion, so the element is a partner of itself on the left and on the right; the partner is nonzero because the nilpotent is, and the definition of the zero divisor is met (Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures, §The Zero Divisor and the Annihilator). The annihilators of the two sides coincide by the alternation of the block (Zero Divisors of the Antisymmetric Quaternionic Algebra).

The implication nilpotent implies zero divisor therefore holds for the block at every nonzero nilpotent: the nilpotent set is the whole space, the zero-divisor set is the punctured space, and the two differ by the origin alone, which is the trivial nilpotent and is excluded from the zero divisors by their definition. Of the four general products the implication holds for two and fails for one, at the plain sesquilinear product whose only square-zero element is the origin (Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures, §The Nilpotent); for the block it holds at every element other than the origin, the origin being the trivial nilpotent and not a zero divisor.

The Relation to the Idempotents

Remark (the two classes meet at the origin). No nonzero element of the block is both an idempotent and a nilpotent. The idempotent set of the block is the origin alone (Idempotents of the Antisymmetric Quaternionic Algebra), so an idempotent of the block is the origin, which is the trivial nilpotent; and a nonzero nilpotent has vanishing square while a nonzero idempotent would satisfy $\tilde\Pi\diamond\tilde\Pi=\tilde\Pi\neq0$. The two classes therefore meet at the origin and nowhere else.

The statement is the sharp form of a general fact. In an associative unital product the idempotents and the nilpotents are disjoint outside the origin, and in the block they are the origin and the whole space, the two extremes that the definitions allow: the smallest idempotent set and the largest nilpotent set.

The Conjugations and the Matrix Model

Proposition (the conjugations preserve the nilpotent class). The natural conjugation ${}^{\natural}$, the complex conjugation $\overline{\cdot}$ and the star ${}^{*}$ carry the nilpotent set of the block onto itself, the set being the whole space; and on the values of the block the natural conjugation acts as the negative of the identity, $$ (\tilde P\diamond\tilde Q)^{\natural}=-\tilde P\diamond\tilde Q . $$

Proof. The nilpotent set is the whole space, so a bijection carries it onto itself. For the display, the natural conjugation is an anti-automorphism of the plain product, so $(\tilde P^{\natural}\tilde Q)^{\natural}=\tilde Q^{\natural}\tilde P$; applying it to the two terms of the definition of the block gives $\tfrac12(\tilde Q^{\natural}\tilde P-\tilde P^{\natural}\tilde Q)=-\tilde P\diamond\tilde Q$.

Remark (the matrix model). In either matrix model of the block the square of an element vanishes, so the matrix of the square is the zero matrix and the nilpotent set is the whole matrix space: $\mathsf{M}(\tilde P\diamond\tilde P)=\mathsf{M}(0)=0$ for every $\tilde P$, in the $2\times2$ model and in the $4\times4$ model alike (The Antisymmetric Quaternionic Algebra in the $2\times2$ Matrix Element Representation $M_2(\mathbb{C})$, The Antisymmetric Quaternionic Algebra in the $4\times4$ Matrix Element Representation $M_4(\mathbb{C})_L$). The matrix models therefore carry no nilpotent condition beyond the vanishing of the square.

The Relation to the Parent Product

Remark (the parent's nilpotent cone). The parent quaternionic product $\tilde P^{\natural}\tilde Q$ has the square $\tilde P^{\natural}\tilde P=N(\tilde P)e_0$, so its square-zero set is the norm cone $N(\tilde P)=0$, which is the zero-divisor cone, a proper cone of the space; its nilpotents are the square-zero elements of that cone, the index being two there by the central-square identity (The Nilpotents and the Zero Divisors of the Quaternionic Product). The block is the parent with the scalar part removed, and with the scalar part removed the square of the parent, which was a central reading of the norm, becomes the zero of the block: the parent's cone widens to the whole space, and the parent's index two is kept by every nonzero element. The block's nilpotent set strictly contains the parent's, and the difference is the complement of the cone, on which the parent's square is a nonzero central value and the block's square is zero.

Summary

For the antisymmetric quaternionic multiplication $\tilde P\diamond\tilde Q=P_0\mathbf Q-Q_0\mathbf P-\mathbf P\times\mathbf Q$ every element is a nilpotent: the square $\tilde\Upsilon\diamond\tilde\Upsilon$ vanishes identically, the square-zero set is the punctured space, the index of a nonzero element is exactly two and no element has index three or more. The nilpotent set is therefore the whole space, and it strictly contains the nilpotent cone $N(\tilde\Upsilon)=0$ of the parent quaternionic product, which is the zero-divisor cone. Every nonzero nilpotent is a two-sided zero divisor with itself as the annihilating partner, and the implication nilpotent implies zero divisor holds for the block at every element other than the origin; the nilpotents and the idempotents meet at the origin alone, the idempotent set being the origin. The conjugations carry the class onto itself, and on the values of the block the natural conjugation acts as the negative of the identity; in either matrix model the square is the zero matrix.
