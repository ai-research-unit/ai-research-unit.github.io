Automaton and Contracting Groups

Introduction

An automaton is a finite machine with a set of states: a state reads a letter of the alphabet, prints a letter, and passes to a state. If the machine is invertible, each state defines an automorphism of the regular rooted tree of Self-Similar Groups, and the group generated by the states is an automaton group. The machine does two things at once. It computes the action of an element on a word, letter by letter; and it computes the sections of the element, because the section of a state at a letter is again a state. An automaton group is therefore a self-similar group, and conversely every self-similar group is generated by its complete automaton, the automaton whose states are the elements of the group. The complete automaton is finite exactly when the group has finitely many sections, and that is the condition under which the group can be described by a finite machine.

The second notion of the article is the contraction of an action. A self-similar action is contracting when the sections of every element, taken deep enough in the tree, lie in one finite set, the nucleus; the group is then generated by the nucleus, and the machine that computes the sections shrinks the word length as it descends. Contraction is the hypothesis under which the self-similar structure answers algorithmic questions: a contracting group is finite-state, its word problem is solvable, and the standard examples — the adding machine, the Grigorchuk group, the Gupta–Sidki groups, the iterated monodromy groups of sub-hyperbolic rational maps — are all contracting. The article develops the automata and their groups, the word problem with its hardness in general and its decidability under contraction, the contracting actions with the nucleus and the contraction coefficient, and the class of bounded automata, which is the class whose limit space is post-critically finite.

The article assumes Self-Similar Groups, written before it, for the tree, the sections, the wreath recursion, the portraits, the branch groups and the standard examples, and it cites that article rather than restating it. It assumes Graph Theory for the Moore diagram, which is a labelled directed graph, Combinatorial Group Theory for the word problem, and Groups and Infinite Groups for the presentations and the wreath products. It uses Metric, Uniform and Complete Spaces for the metric on a level of the tree and Profinite Groups and the Krull Topology for residual finiteness.

The boundaries are these. The Schreier graphs of the action and the limit spaces, together with the tiles and the limit dynamical system, are the subject of Limit Spaces and Schreier Graphs, written after this one; the present article uses the nucleus and the automaton that compute them but does not build a space. The spectral theory of the Schreier graphs, the invariant measures on the boundary and the operator algebras of the group are Part III's, where the measure and the Hilbert space are available. The iterated monodromy groups are introduced here only as examples of contracting automaton groups; the complex dynamics of the rational map that produces them, and the theory of the Julia set, are Part III's, and the fractal dimension of a limit space is Part IV's. No physics is invoked.

Automata and the Groups They Generate

Mealy Automata

Definition. A Mealy automaton (an automaton in this article) over the alphabet $X$ is a triple $\mathcal{A} = (Q, \lambda, \pi)$ where $Q$ is the set of states, $\lambda: Q \times X \to X$ is the output function and $\pi: Q \times X \to Q$ is the transition function. The automaton is finite when $Q$ is finite and invertible when, for every state $q$, the map $x \mapsto \lambda(q,x)$ is a permutation of $X$.

Definition (Moore diagram). The Moore diagram of $\mathcal{A}$ is the directed labelled graph whose vertices are the states and which has, for every $q \in Q$ and $x \in X$, an arrow $q \to \pi(q,x)$ carrying the label $(x, \lambda(q,x))$. It is a directed labelled graph in the sense of Graph Theory, and it is the standard way to draw a finite automaton.

Definition (the transformation of a state). Extend $\lambda$ and $\pi$ to words by

$$ \pi(q, xv) = \pi\bigl(\pi(q,x), v\bigr), \qquad \lambda(q, xv) = \lambda(q,x)\,\lambda\bigl(\pi(q,x), v\bigr), \qquad \pi(q, \varnothing) = q, \quad \lambda(q, \varnothing) = \varnothing . $$

For a fixed initial state $q$ the map $w \mapsto \lambda(q,w)$ is a transformation $A_q$ of $X^*$, and the second recursion defines it on $X^{\omega}$ as well, so $A_q$ is an endomorphism of the tree. If $\mathcal{A}$ is invertible then $A_q$ is invertible for every $q$: the inverse automaton has the same states and the transition $\pi^*(q,x) = \pi(q, \lambda^{-1}(q,x))$, so the inverse of a finite-state transformation is finite-state.

Proposition (sections of a state). For $x \in X$ the section of $A_q$ at $x$ is the state at the target of the arrow $q \to \pi(q,x)$:

$$ A_q|_x = A_{\pi(q,x)} . $$

Proof. Reading a word $xw$ through the machine from $q$ prints $\lambda(q,x)$ and continues from the state $\pi(q,x)$ with the remaining word $w$; this is exactly the recursion $A_q(xw) = \lambda(q,x)\,A_{\pi(q,x)}(w)$, and the definition of the section of Self-Similar Groups reads off the factor. $\square$

The proposition is the point of the notion: sections of states are again states, so the finite set $Q$ is closed under the operation of taking sections, and the machine contains all the sections of the automorphisms it defines. It is the converse direction — an arbitrary section of an arbitrary element of the group need not be a state of a given small machine — that the contraction below measures.

Definition (finite-state transformation). A transformation of the tree is finite-state, or automatic, if it is $A_q$ for some state $q$ of a finite invertible automaton. A group of automorphisms of the tree is finite-state if every element of it is finite-state.

Example (an automaton for the adding machine). Over the binary alphabet let $Q = \{a, e\}$, with the identity state $e$ printing every letter and returning to itself, and with the state $a$ printing the other letter and passing to $e$ after the letter $0$ and to $a$ after the letter $1$. Thus $A_a(0w) = 1\,A_e(w) = 1w$ and $A_a(1w) = 0\,A_a(w)$, so $A_a$ is the adding machine $a = (\mathrm{id}, a)\varepsilon$ of Self-Similar Groups, and its sections are $a|_0 = \mathrm{id}$ and $a|_1 = a$. The Moore diagram is the two-vertex graph with the arrows $a \to e$ labelled $(0,1)$ and $a \to a$ labelled $(1,0)$, and the two loops $e \to e$ labelled $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$.

Remark (the reading convention). This article reads a word from left to right and matches the recursions of Self-Similar Groups, where the root permutation acts on the first letter. Under the opposite convention every recursion is mirrored, so a computation transported between the two conventions must have its order reversed; the same warning applies to the products below. All the examples here use the convention of Self-Similar Groups.

The Group of an Automaton

Definition. Let $\mathcal{A} = (Q, \lambda, \pi)$ be a finite invertible automaton. The group generated by $\mathcal{A}$ is

$$ G(\mathcal{A}) = \langle A_q : q \in Q \rangle \leq \operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{T}). $$

A group is an automaton group if it is $G(\mathcal{A})$ for some finite invertible automaton $\mathcal{A}$. The complete automaton of an action of a group $G$ on $\mathcal{T}$ is the automaton with states $G$, output $\lambda(g,x) = g(x)$ and transition $\pi(g,x) = g|_x$.

Theorem (self-similarity is generation by an automaton). Let $G \leq \operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{T})$. The following are equivalent.

(a) The action of $G$ is self-similar and $G$ has finitely many sections, that is, the set $\{g|_v : g \in G,\ v \in X^*\}$ is finite.

(b) $G = G(\mathcal{A})$ for a finite invertible automaton $\mathcal{A}$.

If only the first half of (a) is assumed, that is, if $G$ is self-similar without the finiteness, then $G$ is generated by the states of its complete automaton.

Proof sketch. (a) $\Rightarrow$ (b): take for $Q$ the finite set of sections of the elements of $G$; define $\lambda(q,x) = q(x)$ and $\pi(q,x) = q|_x$. The set is closed under sections because $(g|_v)|_x = g|_{vx}$, and the axioms of an automaton are satisfied; the state $q$ defines the automorphism $q$ itself, so the states generate $G$ and (b) holds. (b) $\Rightarrow$ (a): the sections of the states are the states, by the proposition above, and the section of a product is a product of sections, by the multiplication rule of Self-Similar Groups; hence every section of every element is a product of states, so all sections lie in the finite set of products of states and the action is self-similar. The same computation with $Q = G$ proves the last statement. $\square$

Corollary. Every self-similar group is a subgroup of the profinite group $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{T})$, hence is residually finite, and every automaton group is finitely generated.

The first statement is the general fact that a subgroup of a residually finite group is residually finite, applied to $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{T})$ of Self-Similar Groups; the second is that $G(\mathcal{A})$ has the finite generating set $Q$.

Remark (the machine and the group). The passage from the group to the machine is not unique: different automata, even with different numbers of states, generate the same group, and a minimal machine generating a given automaton group is the nucleus of the contraction below when one exists. What is canonical is the complete automaton, with one state per element, and the passage to a finite machine is precisely the assertion that the group is finite-state. The automaton is the combinatorial input of the third article's construction, and the reason the theory has content is that finitely many states already produce groups that the classical presentation theory cannot describe.

The Word Problem

The Problem and the Machine

The word problem of Combinatorial Group Theory asks, for a group $G$ with a finite generating set $S$, for an algorithm that decides of a word $w$ in $S \cup S^{-1}$ whether $w$ represents the identity. For an automaton group the generators are the states and the elements are the machines' transformations, so the question becomes: given a word $q_1q_2\cdots q_\ell$ in the states, decide whether the composite transformation is the identity of $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{T})$.

The machine gives the first step of the decision: $A_q$ lies in the level-$n$ stabiliser $\operatorname{St}_n$ exactly when the machine started at $q$ acts trivially on all words of length $n$, which is a finite check on $|X|^n$ words. A word represents a nontrivial element exactly when the machine read through the word acts nontrivially on some level, and the question is whether this can be decided.

Hardness in General

Theorem (hardness of the word problem for automaton groups). There is an automaton group over a binary alphabet whose word problem is PSPACE-complete and whose compressed word problem is EXPSPACE-complete; no automaton group over a one-letter alphabet can be harder, so the alphabet is optimal. For finitary automaton groups, those generated by an invertible finite-state machine all of whose states have finite order, the uniform word problem is coNP-complete and the compressed word problem is PSPACE-complete.

The first statement is that of Wächter and Weiß, answering a conjecture of Steinberg: the construction simulates a Turing machine in an automaton group and combines the simulation of Boolean circuits of bounded degree and logarithmic depth in the group of even permutations with an earlier semigroup construction. The second is that of Kotowsky and Wächter. The consequence for this article is a boundary: no polynomial-time algorithm for the word problem of a general automaton group exists under standard assumptions, so the decidability of the word problem is a genuine restriction on the class — and contraction is the restriction that makes the decidability provable.

Decidability under Contraction

Theorem. A contracting self-similar group has a solvable word problem.

The theorem is one of Nekrashevych's; the input is the reduction lemma of the next section, which says that the section map of a contracting action strictly shortens a word that is long and fixes a level. The decision is then the recursion down the tree: if the word acts nontrivially on the first level, it is nontrivial; otherwise it lies in $\operatorname{St}_1$ and its sections at the letters are shorter, so the same question is asked of the sections, one level at a time. Once the words involved have length at most a constant $C$, the ball of radius $C$ in the Cayley graph is finite and every nontrivial element of it is detected on one of finitely many levels, so the check is finite. The algorithm is therefore the automaton itself: it is the machine that carries the word down the tree.

Remark. The conjugacy problem is a different matter. It is solvable for the contracting groups that come from complex dynamics, such as the group $\mathrm{IMG}(z^2-1)$ of the last section, and for the Grigorchuk group, but the general theory of conjugacy in automaton groups is not reducible to the word problem, and no contraction argument of the kind above settles it. It is not pursued here.

Contracting Actions and the Nucleus

The Definition

Definition (contracting). A self-similar action of a group $G$ on $\mathcal{T}(X)$ is contracting if there is a finite set $N \subset G$ with the property that for every $g \in G$ there is $n$ such that

$$ g|_v \in N \qquad \text{for every } v \in X^* \text{ with } |v| \geq n . $$

The minimal such $N$ is the nucleus of the action. A contracting action is thus one in which the sections of every element become members of one fixed finite set once the element is restricted deep enough in the tree.

Theorem (properties of the nucleus). Let the action of $G$ be contracting with nucleus $N$.

(a) The action is finite-state, and the nucleus is a subautomaton of the complete automaton: the sections of an element of $N$ are again elements of $N$.

(b) If the action is recurrent, in the sense of Self-Similar Groups, then $N$ generates $G$; in general the nucleus generates a finite-index subgroup of $G$.

(c) Being contracting, and the finiteness of a nucleus, do not depend on the choice of a finite generating set of $G$.

Proof sketch. (a) is the definition read once: the section of a section is a section, $(g|_v)|_w = g|_{vw}$, with $|vw| \geq |v|$, so the sections of the elements of $N$ are in $N$ once the bound $n$ is taken. (b) For a recurrent action the virtual endomorphism $\varphi_x$ is onto, so every element is a section of an element of a level stabiliser, and (a) then writes it as a word in $N$ by induction on the level. (c) The change of generating set changes lengths by a bounded factor and does not change the set of sections of the elements of $G$, so the property is intrinsic to the action. $\square$

Remark. Contraction is a property of the action, not of the group: the same group may act self-similarly and contractingly on one tree and not on another, and a group may have a contracting action and a non-contracting one. The lamplighter group of Self-Similar Groups is the standard witness on the negative side; it is self-similar and level-transitive, but not contracting. Contraction is also not automatic for a finite-state group: the iterated monodromy group of a rational map that is not sub-hyperbolic is the standard family where it fails.

The Contraction Coefficient

The definition of a contracting action does not by itself say how fast the sections shrink. The rate is measured by the contraction coefficient.

Theorem. Let $G$ be a finitely generated self-similar group with finite generating set $S$ and word length $|g| = |g|_S$. The number

$$ \rho = \limsup_{n \to \infty} \max_{v \in X^n} \left( \limsup_{|g| \to \infty} \frac{|g|_v|}{|g|} \right)^{1/n} $$

is finite, depends on the action and not on the generating set, and satisfies the following: $\rho < 1$ if and only if the action is contracting.

The statement is that of Nekrashevych. When $\rho<1$ the ratio $\lvert g|_v\rvert/\lvert g\rvert$, the length of the section over the length of the element, tends to zero along the tree at a geometric rate, and the section map is a uniformly contracting self-map of the group for words of large length. This is the estimate that makes the reduction below quantitative, and it is the analogue for the tree of the contraction of a map of a metric space, in the sense in which a contracting action of a group on a hyperbolic space is used in Hyperbolic Groups; the tree is an ultrametric space at the boundary and the analogy is at the level of the length function only.

The Reduction of Words

Lemma (reduction). Let the action of $G$ be contracting, let $S$ be a finite generating set with $S \supseteq N$, and let $|w|$ denote the length of a word $w$ over $S \cup S^{-1}$. There is a constant $C$ with the following property: if $|w| > C$ and $w$ represents an element of the level-$n$ stabiliser $\operatorname{St}_n$, then each section $w|_v$, $v \in X^n$, can be written as a word over $S \cup S^{-1}$ of length strictly less than $|w|$.

Proof sketch. The word $w = s_1s_2\cdots s_\ell$ defines a sequence of states of the complete automaton; its section at $v = x_1x_2\cdots x_n$ is the word $s_1^{(1)}s_2^{(1)}\cdots s_\ell^{(1)}$ in the sections of the letters, where the superscript records the letter read. Because $S \supseteq N$ and the action is contracting, the letters of this new word lie in $N$ as soon as the depth $n$ exceeds the contraction bound; and because $w$ fixes the level $n$, the finite automaton of the nucleus must revisit a state before the word is exhausted, whenever $\ell$ exceeds the number of states. The part of $w$ between two visits acts trivially on the relevant subtree, and deleting it shortens the word; the section is therefore strictly shorter. The existence of the constant $C$, uniform in $n$, is the content of the contraction property, and the estimate is the one of Nekrashevych. $\square$

This is the engine of the word-problem algorithm. It is also the reason contraction proves statements about the elements by induction on the word length rather than by a presentation: in the original paper of Grigorchuk on the group of intermediate growth, the contraction is what proves that every element has finite order, and the same induction gives the torsion property of the Gupta–Sidki groups.

Bounded Automata

The class of bounded automata is the well-behaved subfamily of the contracting ones.

Definition (Sidki). Let $q$ be an automatic transformation and let $\theta(k,q)$ be the number of words $v \in X^k$ such that the section $q|_v$ acts nontrivially on the first level $X^1$ of the tree. Then $q$ is bounded if the sequence $k \mapsto \theta(k,q)$ is bounded, and an automaton is bounded if all its states are. The set $B$ of all bounded automorphisms of $\mathcal{T}$ is a group.

Theorem. A state is bounded if and only if it is defined by a finite automaton in which every two nontrivial cycles are disjoint and are not connected by a directed path. Every self-similar subgroup of the group $B$ of bounded automata is contracting, and a contracting group has a post-critically finite limit space, in the sense of the third article, if and only if it is a subgroup of $B$. A post-critically finite limit space is one-dimensional.

The characterisation of the finite automaton is Sidki's, the equivalence of boundedness with the post-critically finite limit space is that of Bondarenko and Nekrashevych, and the one-dimensionality is part of the same circle of results. The class contains the Grigorchuk group, the Gupta–Sidki groups, the AT-groups and the spinal groups, that is, most of the known examples; it is the class whose limit spaces are the tame ones, with tiles that meet in finite sets, and it is the reason the limit space of a bounded automaton group is a fractal in the sense of Fractal Geometry rather than a space of complicated local structure. The limit space itself is the subject of the next article; boundedness is defined here because it is a condition on the automaton, and the consequence for the space is named without being used.

The Examples and the Class

The Standard Examples

Example (the adding machine). The adding machine of Self-Similar Groups is contracting, with nucleus $\{a^{-1}, \mathrm{id}, a\}$: the section of $a^k$ at a letter is $a^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}$ or $a^{\lceil k/2\rceil}$ according to the letter and the parity, so all sections of large powers lie in the three-element set. The automaton with the states $a^{-1}, \mathrm{id}, a$ and the transitions of the odometer is the nucleus, and it is the machine drawn in the example above.

Example (the Grigorchuk group). The Grigorchuk group $G = \langle a,b,c,d\rangle$ is contracting with nucleus the generating set $\{1,a,b,c,d\}$ itself. The verification of Self-Similar Groups that the sections of the generators lie in the set is the first step; the second is that the same is true of every product, because the section of a product is a product of sections and the products of the elements of the nucleus lie in the nucleus, which is the closure computed for the four generators. The contraction is what gives the group its recursive structure, and the nucleus automaton is the machine that draws the portraits.

Example (the Gupta–Sidki and Sierpiński gasket groups). The Gupta–Sidki groups and the group of the Sierpiński gasket of Self-Similar Groups are contracting. For the group of the Sierpiński gasket the sections of a generator are the generator itself and the identity, by the recursions recorded there, so the set of generators is closed under sections and is the nucleus; for the Gupta–Sidki groups the sections of the generators lie in the set of the generators together with their inverses, and that set is the nucleus.

Example (the lamplighter group). The lamplighter group of Self-Similar Groups is not contracting. The sections of a product are products of sections, and the products of the two states of the machine are new elements at every level; no finite set contains the sections of all elements, so the definition fails.

Example (the iterated monodromy groups). Let $f \in \mathbb{C}(z)$ be a rational map of degree $d$, let a base point be chosen and let $\mathrm{IMG}(f)$ be the group defined by the recursion of the covering $f$ on the tree of preimages of the base point. Then $\mathrm{IMG}(f)$ is a self-similar group on the $d$-ary tree, and Nekrashevych's theorem states that if $f$ is sub-hyperbolic, in particular if $f$ is post-critically finite, then $\mathrm{IMG}(f)$ is contracting and its limit space is homeomorphic to the Julia set of $f$. The group is the automaton group of the machine read from the covering; the complex dynamics that defines it is Part III's, and the statement is quoted here for the example it supplies. The group $\mathrm{IMG}(z^2-1)$ is torsion free, has exponential growth, is just non-solvable and contains no free subgroup of rank two, and its word and conjugacy problems are solvable; it is the standard group of this family that is not hyperbolic in the sense of Hyperbolic Groups.

The Class

Theorem (structure). The following hold for automaton groups.

(a) Every automaton group is a finitely generated subgroup of $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{T})$, hence is residually finite.

(b) A branch automaton group is just infinite; the Grigorchuk group and the Gupta–Sidki groups are of this kind.

(c) A contracting self-similar group is finite-state and has a solvable word problem; the conjugacy problem is solvable for the families known from dynamics, and it is not a consequence of contraction in general.

Proof sketch. (a) is the corollary above. (b) is the theorem on branch groups of Self-Similar Groups, applied to the automaton group. (c) is the two theorems above. $\square$

The class of automaton groups is therefore governed by a dichotomy of method: the general automaton group is a finitely generated residually finite group whose word problem may be computationally hard, while the contracting automaton group is a group for which the machine decides the word problem and the tree supplies the space. The dividing line is the existence of the finite nucleus, and the third article shows what the nucleus builds: the Schreier graphs of the action, the limit space and the limit dynamical system.

Summary

An automaton is a finite machine $(Q,\lambda,\pi)$ whose states act on the regular rooted tree; the section of a state at a letter is a state, $A_q|_x = A_{\pi(q,x)}$, so the machine closes under sections, and a group is finite-state exactly when its complete automaton has finitely many states. A self-similar group is generated by the states of its complete automaton, and the finite-state self-similar groups are the automaton groups; every automaton group is finitely generated and residually finite, being a subgroup of the profinite group $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{T})$.

The word problem of an automaton group is hard in general: there is an automaton group over a binary alphabet with $\mathrm{PSPACE}$-complete word problem, and the uniform word problem of the finitary automaton groups is $\mathrm{coNP}$-complete. It becomes solvable under contraction: a contracting action has a finite nucleus $N$, closed under sections, which generates the group when the action is recurrent, and its contraction coefficient $\rho$ is less than one exactly for the contracting actions. The reduction lemma says that a long word fixing a level has strictly shorter sections, and the word problem is decided by carrying the word down the tree through the nucleus. A bounded automaton, one whose states have boundedly many nontrivial sections at every level, is contracting, and the contracting groups with post-critically finite limit space are exactly the subgroups of the group $B$ of bounded automorphisms, whose limit space is one-dimensional.

The examples are the adding machine with nucleus $\{a^{-1},1,a\}$; the Grigorchuk group with nucleus $\{1,a,b,c,d\}$; the Gupta–Sidki and Sierpiński gasket groups, whose nuclei are the generators with their inverses; the lamplighter group, which is not contracting; and the iterated monodromy groups of sub-hyperbolic rational maps, which are contracting and whose limit spaces are the Julia sets. The Schreier graphs, the limit space and the limit dynamical system are the subject of Limit Spaces and Schreier Graphs; the spectra and the measures are Part III's, and the fractal dimension of the limit space is Part IV's.
