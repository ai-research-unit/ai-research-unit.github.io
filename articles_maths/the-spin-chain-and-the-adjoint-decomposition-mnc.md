# __The Spin Chain and the Adjoint Decomposition__

## Introduction

**The chain of simple nilpotents is the ladder of an $\mathfrak{sl}_2$, the carrier is $\mathbb{C}^{n}=\operatorname{Sym}^{n-1}(\mathbb{C}^{2})$ of spin $(n-1)/2$, and the adjoint decomposes as $1+3+\cdots+(2n-1)$.** This is the last step of the construction: spin is the weight of the Peirce chain, not a separate structure imposed on the algebra.

The article defines the $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ of the frame, proves the commutation relations with the ladder coefficients $\sqrt{k(n-k)}$, identifies the carrier with the symmetric power, proves the adjoint decomposition by weight multiplicities, and reads the chirality at even $n$. It closes with the warning that governs the whole corpus: for $n>2$ the carrier is not a spinor module, and only the case $n=2$ produces spinors.

The frame and the nilpotents come from *The Frame, the Simple Nilpotents and the Gauge*; the idempotents and the grading from *Roots of Unity and the Lagrange Idempotents*.

## The $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ of the Frame

**The diagonal weights and the two ladder operators.** Let $E_{kl}$ be the matrix units of an adapted frame, $k,l=0,\dots,n-1$. Put

$$
j_k=\frac{n-1}{2}-k,\qquad
J_3=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}j_kE_{kk},
$$

$$
J_+=\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}c_kE_{k,k+1},\qquad
J_-=\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}c_kE_{k+1,k},\qquad
c_k=\sqrt{(k+1)(n-1-k)}.
$$

The numbers $c_k$ are the **ladder coefficients**. In the other indexing they are $c_{k-1}=\sqrt{k(n-k)}$ for $k=1,\dots,n-1$, which is the form in which they appear in the menu.

**Proposition.** The three elements satisfy

$$
[J_3,J_\pm]=\pm J_\pm,\qquad [J_+,J_-]=2J_3,
$$

so they span a Lie algebra isomorphic to $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$.

*Proof.* On the matrix units,

$$
[J_3,E_{kl}]=(j_k-j_l)E_{kl}=(l-k)E_{kl},
$$

so $[J_3,E_{k,k+1}]=E_{k,k+1}$ and $[J_3,E_{k+1,k}]=-E_{k+1,k}$. For the second relation, $[E_{k,k+1},E_{l+1,l}]=\delta_{k,l}\big(E_{kk}-E_{k+1,k+1}\big)$, hence

$$
[J_+,J_-]=\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}c_k^{2}\big(E_{kk}-E_{k+1,k+1}\big).
$$

The coefficient of $E_{kk}$ is $c_k^{2}-c_{k-1}^{2}$ with $c_{-1}=c_{n-1}=0$, and

$$
(k+1)(n-1-k)-k(n-k)=n-1-2k=2j_k,
$$

so the sum is $2J_3$. $\square$

**The coefficients are forced.** If $J_\pm=\sum a_kE_{k,k\pm1}$ and $[J_+,J_-]=2J_3$, then $a_k^{2}-a_{k-1}^{2}=2j_k=n-1-2k$, so $a_k^{2}=(k+1)(n-1-k)$ and $a_k$ is determined up to sign. The symmetric choice $a_k>0$ is the one taken here; the sign is a convention on the phase of each basis vector.

**The real form.** With the trace form of *The Matrix Algebra $M_n(\mathbb{C})$*, $J_3^{*}=J_3$ and $J_+^{*}=J_-$, so the real span of $iJ_3$, $i(J_++J_-)$ and $J_+-J_-$ is $\mathfrak{su}(2)$. The complex span is $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$.

## The Carrier

**The algebra acts on $\mathbb{C}^{n}$, and the chain acts as the ladder of a highest weight module.** Let $e_k=v_k$ be the frame vectors, so $E_{kl}e_m=\delta_{lm}e_k$. Then

$$
J_3e_k=j_ke_k,\qquad J_+e_k=c_{k-1}e_{k-1},\qquad J_-e_k=c_ke_{k+1},
$$

with the convention that the coefficients vanish at the ends. The vector $e_0$ is annihilated by $J_+$ and has the largest weight $j_0=(n-1)/2$.

**Proposition.** $\mathbb{C}^{n}$ is irreducible under this action, and is isomorphic to $\operatorname{Sym}^{n-1}(\mathbb{C}^{2})$ as a module of spin $(n-1)/2$.

*Proof.* From $e_0$, the operator $J_-$ reaches $e_1,e_2,\dots,e_{n-1}$ with nonzero coefficients $c_0,c_1,\dots,c_{n-2}$, so the submodule generated by $e_0$ is the whole space; a highest weight module generated by a vector of weight $(n-1)/2$ with all ladder coefficients nonzero is irreducible. The identification with $\operatorname{Sym}^{n-1}(\mathbb{C}^{2})$ is the standard one: on the monomial $x^{n-1-k}y^{k}$,

$$
J_3=\tfrac12(x\partial_x-y\partial_y),\qquad J_+=x\partial_y,\qquad J_-=y\partial_x
$$

give the same weights $j_k$ and the same coefficients $\sqrt{k(n-k)}$ after normalising the monomials to unit length. $\square$

**The dimension check.** $\dim\operatorname{Sym}^{n-1}(\mathbb{C}^{2})=n$, and the weights are $j_0,j_1,\dots,j_{n-1}$, equally spaced by $1$. This is the spin-$(n-1)/2$ module.

**The carrier is not the algebra.** The carrier is $\mathbb{C}^{n}$, of dimension $n$; the algebra is $\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})$, of dimension $n^{2}$. The Peirce corners have dimension $1$ each. The carrier is the module on which the chain acts, not a corner and not a subspace of the algebra.

## The Adjoint Decomposition

**The adjoint representation of the $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ on the algebra is the commutator.** For $X\in M_n(\mathbb{C})$,

$$
\operatorname{ad}_{J_3}(X)=[J_3,X],\qquad \operatorname{ad}_{J_\pm}(X)=[J_\pm,X].
$$

On a matrix unit,

$$
\operatorname{ad}_{J_3}(E_{kl})=(l-k)E_{kl},
$$

so the weights are the integers $m=l-k\in\{-(n-1),\dots,n-1\}$, and the weight $m$ occurs with multiplicity $n-|m|$: the pairs $(k,l)$ with $l-k=m$ are $(0,m),\dots,(n-1-m,n-1)$.

**Proposition.** As a module of the $\mathfrak{sl}_2$, the algebra decomposes as

$$
M_n(\mathbb{C})\cong\bigoplus_{r=0}^{n-1}V_{2r},
$$

where $V_{2r}$ is the irreducible module of dimension $2r+1$ and spin $r$. Hence

$$
M_n(\mathbb{C})\cong 1\oplus3\oplus5\oplus\cdots\oplus(2n-1),
$$

of total dimension $\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}(2r+1)=n^{2}$.

*Proof.* The irreducible module $V_{2r}$ has weights $-r,-r+1,\dots,r$, each once. In the direct sum, the weight $m$ occurs once for each $r$ with $|m|\le r\le n-1$, that is $n-|m|$ times. This equals the multiplicity of $m$ in $M_n(\mathbb{C})$. A finite-dimensional $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-module is determined up to isomorphism by its weight multiplicities, and $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ is semisimple, so the two modules are isomorphic. $\square$

**The summands.** The summand $r=0$ is the centre $\mathbb{C}I$, of dimension $1$. The summands $r=1,\dots,n-1$ span the traceless part $\mathfrak{sl}_n$, of dimension $n^{2}-1$. The summand $r=n-1$ is the largest, of dimension $2n-1$, and contains the extreme matrix units $E_{0,n-1}$ and $E_{n-1,0}$.

**The case $n=3$ explicitly.** The decomposition is $1+3+5$. A highest weight vector for the singlet is $I$; for the triplet, $E_{01}+E_{12}$; for the quintet, $E_{02}$. The last is annihilated by $\operatorname{ad}_{J_+}$ because $J_+E_{02}=E_{02}J_+=0$, and it has weight $2$, so it generates the five-dimensional summand.

**The case $n=2$.** The decomposition is $1+3$: the centre and $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ itself. The biquaternion algebra, read as $M_2(\mathbb{C})$, is the adjoint module of its own $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ plus the centre. The carrier is the two-dimensional spin-$\tfrac12$ module, which is the spinor module of the biquaternion article.

## Chirality at Even $n$

**For even $n$ the root has a canonical involution, and it splits the carrier in two.** Let $n=2m$ and put

$$
\Gamma=H^{m}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\zeta_k^{m}E_{kk}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{k}E_{kk}.
$$

Then $\Gamma^{2}=I$, $\Gamma$ commutes with $H$, and $\Gamma$ is the chirality involution. It splits

$$
\mathbb{C}^{n}=\mathbb{C}^{m}_{+}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{m}_{-},\qquad
\mathbb{C}^{m}_{\pm}=\operatorname{span}\{e_k:\pm(-1)^{k}=1\}.
$$

**The Peirce chain mixes the two halves.** The ladder $J_+$ maps $e_k$ to $e_{k-1}$, so it changes $(-1)^{k}$ and therefore exchanges the two halves. The carrier is irreducible under the Peirce $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ and does not split. This is the precise sense in which the Peirce spin and the chirality are different structures: the first is irreducible on $\mathbb{C}^{n}$, the second is a $\mathbb{Z}/2$ grading that the first does not preserve.

**The chiral algebra preserves the halves.** The subalgebra of $M_n(\mathbb{C})$ that commutes with $\Gamma$ is $M_m(\mathbb{C})\oplus M_m(\mathbb{C})$, acting on $\mathbb{C}^{m}_{+}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{m}_{-}$. Its principal $\mathfrak{sl}_2\oplus\mathfrak{sl}_2$ is the **chiral algebra**. On the carrier,

$$
\mathbb{C}^{n}\cong V_{m}\oplus V_{m}
$$

under the chiral pair, where $V_{m}$ is the spin-$(m-1)/2$ module of dimension $m$: for $n=4$, two spin-$\tfrac12$ modules; for $n=6$, two spin-$1$ modules. The mass block of the chiral decomposition is the off-diagonal part, which is precisely the part the Peirce chain uses to move between the halves.

**At $n=2$.** $m=1$, $\Gamma=H=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$, the two halves are the two lines, and the chiral algebra is $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ acting on each line trivially. This degenerate case is the biquaternion one, where the two chiralities are the two minimal left ideals.

## Spin, Carrier, Spinor

**The word spin in this construction names the weight of the Peirce chain.** The chain of simple nilpotents generates an $\mathfrak{sl}_2$, the carrier is its irreducible module of highest weight $(n-1)/2$, and the adjoint decomposes into the modules of integer spin $0,1,\dots,n-1$. This is an algebraic statement about the frame; it uses no metric and no Clifford algebra.

**The carrier is a spinor module only at $n=2$.** At $n=2$ the carrier is $\mathbb{C}^{2}=\operatorname{Sym}^{1}(\mathbb{C}^{2})$, of spin $\tfrac12$, and a spin-$\tfrac12$ module is what the word spinor denotes. At $n=4$ the chiral halves are spin-$\tfrac12$ modules and may be called spinors; the full carrier is spin $\tfrac32$ and is not. At $n=3,5$ the spin is $1,2$ and there is no spinor at all. At $n=6$ the chiral halves are spin $1$ and the full carrier is spin $\tfrac52$.

**The carrier is not a Peirce corner and not a minimal left ideal.** A minimal left ideal of $M_n(\mathbb{C})$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{C}^{n}$ as a vector space, so at $n=2$ the carrier and the minimal left ideal have the same dimension and are isomorphic as modules. At $n\ge3$ the isomorphism fails: the minimal left ideal is the standard module of $M_n$ on which the whole algebra acts, while the carrier is the module of the Peirce $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ only. The two are different objects even when they have the same dimension, because they are modules over different algebras.

**Do not define a spinor as an element of the algebra.** The elements of $M_n(\mathbb{C})$ are operators. A spinor, where the word applies, is a vector of the carrier. The confusion between the two is the defect that the corpus rejects: a Peirce element is a map between two lines of a flag, and a minimal left ideal is a space of such maps, not a spinor.

## Summary

The frame carries an $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ with $J_3=\sum_k\big(\tfrac{n-1}{2}-k\big)E_{kk}$ and $J_\pm=\sum_k\sqrt{(k+1)(n-1-k)}\,E_{k,k\pm1}$, with $[J_+,J_-]=2J_3$. The carrier is $\mathbb{C}^{n}=\operatorname{Sym}^{n-1}(\mathbb{C}^{2})$, irreducible of spin $(n-1)/2$, with ladder coefficients $\sqrt{k(n-k)}$. The adjoint decomposes as $1+3+\cdots+(2n-1)$: the centre plus the traceless part, one irreducible summand per weight. At even $n$ the chirality involution $\Gamma=H^{n/2}$ splits the carrier into two halves of dimension $n/2$, which the Peirce chain exchanges and the chiral algebra preserves. The carrier is a spinor module only at $n=2$, and at the chiral halves of $n=4$; otherwise it is the spin-$(n-1)/2$ module and should not be called a spinor.

## Summary of Notation

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $j_k=\tfrac{n-1}{2}-k$ | the weight of $e_k$ |
| $J_3,J_\pm$ | the generators of the Peirce $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ |
| $c_k=\sqrt{(k+1)(n-1-k)}$ | the ladder coefficient, $k=0,\dots,n-2$ |
| $\mathbb{C}^{n}=\operatorname{Sym}^{n-1}(\mathbb{C}^{2})$ | the carrier, of spin $(n-1)/2$ |
| $V_{d}$ | the irreducible module of dimension $d$ |
| $\Gamma=H^{n/2}$ | the chirality involution for even $n$ |
| $\mathbb{C}^{m}_{\pm}$ | the two chiral halves, $m=n/2$ |
