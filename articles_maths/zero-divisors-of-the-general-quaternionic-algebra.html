Zero Divisors of the General Quaternionic Algebra

Introduction

This article studies the zero divisors of the biquaternion algebra $\mathbb{B}$ read through the general quaternionic bilinear product, and it complements the algebraic norm of the product by describing the set on which the algebraic norm vanishes.

A divisor is relative to a product, and the definition is stated once in Definitions for the Study of the Algebraic Structures, §The Zero Divisor and the Annihilator; the unqualified "zero divisor" means the zero divisor of the general quaternionic product throughout, and every other product is named. The article establishes four things. First, the criterion: a nonzero $\tilde Q$ is a zero divisor of $\star$ exactly when its algebraic norm vanishes, so the zero divisors of the quaternionic product are the nonzero solutions of $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=0$. Second, the set is the zero-divisor cone, a real cone of dimension six in the eight-dimensional real space that is not a subspace, and it is the union of the pure and the non-pure families of the element theory of the algebra. Third, the two annihilators of an element, taken with respect to $\star$, are each of complex dimension two for a zero divisor and vanish for a unit, and the element lies in its own annihilators. Fourth, and the sharpest fact of the article, the two annihilators coincide for every element, unit or zero divisor alike, because the natural conjugation is a $\mathbb{C}$-linear anti-automorphism of order two; the quaternionic product is the one product of the block whose annihilators are not exchanged by the conjugation.

The results. A nonzero biquaternion is a zero divisor of the general quaternionic product exactly when its algebraic norm vanishes, $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=\sum_\mu Q_\mu^2=Q_t^2+(\vec Q,\vec Q)=0$, so the zero divisors are the nonzero points of the zero-divisor cone $C=\{N=0\}$, a real cone of real dimension $6$ that is not a subspace and is stable under the product. The cone splits into the pure family, the plain nilpotents with $Q_t=0$ and $(\vec Q,\vec Q)=0$ forming an isotropic cone of real dimension $4$, and the non-pure family, the complex multiples $\tilde Q=2Q_t\tilde\Pi$ of the nontrivial plain idempotents, on which $\tilde Q\star\tilde Q=0$ while the plain square is the nonzero multiple $2Q_t\tilde Q$. The two annihilators of an element coincide, are $\{0\}$ off the cone and a complex plane on it, and the four general products select one and the same zero-divisor set. Each section below states its result first and then proves it.

Boundaries. The zero divisors of the algebra, the two families, the criterion in the plain product and the structure of the cone are Zero Divisors of the Biquaternion Algebra; the plain nilpotents are Nilpotents of the General Plain Algebra, and the plain idempotents Idempotents of the General Plain Algebra. The fact that the four general products select one and the same zero-divisor set, with its proof, and the one-sided inverse equations, are Zero Divisors in the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternioid $\mathbb{C}$ Space; the article quotes that coincidence and does not reprove it. The algebraic norm and the one-sided equations of this product are Norm and Invertibility in the General Quaternionic Algebra; the nilpotents of the product are Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents of the General Quaternionic Algebra; and the form on the remarkable subspaces is Remarkable Subspaces under the General Quaternionic Algebra of Biquaternions.

Conventions. $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$ has basis $e_t,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z$, with $\vec e_x\vec e_y=\vec e_z$ and central scalar imaginary $i$, $i^2=-1$. Juxtaposition is the plain product and $\star$ the general quaternionic bilinear product,

$$ \tilde P\star\tilde Q=\tilde P^{\natural}\tilde Q , $$

with $\natural$ the natural conjugation $\tilde Q^{\natural}=Q_t-\vec Q$. An element is $\tilde Q=Q_te_t+\vec Q$ with $\vec Q=\sum_{k=1}^{3}Q_k\vec e_k$, the algebraic norm is $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=\tilde Q^{\natural}\tilde Q=\sum_\mu Q_\mu^2$, and $(\vec P,\vec Q)=\sum_kP_kQ_k$ is the complex bilinear form of the vector parts. An element is pure when $Q_t=0$ and non-pure when $Q_t\neq0$.

Definition

The word names a product, so the definition is stated for a general product and then read on $\star$.

Definition. Let $\star$ be a product on $\mathbb{B}$ and let $\tilde Q\in\mathbb{B}$. The element $\tilde Q$ is a left zero divisor of $\star$ when $\tilde Q\neq0$ and there is a nonzero $\tilde R$ with $\tilde Q\star\tilde R=0$; a right zero divisor of $\star$ when $\tilde Q\neq0$ and there is a nonzero $\tilde R$ with $\tilde R\star\tilde Q=0$; and a zero divisor of $\star$ when it is one or the other. It is a two-sided zero divisor of $\star$ when it is both. The definition is Definitions for the Study of the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternioid $\mathbb{C}$ Space, §The Zero Divisor and the Annihilator, and the origin is not a zero divisor, both factors being required to be nonzero.

Throughout this article the product is the general quaternionic bilinear product $\tilde P\star\tilde Q=\tilde P^{\natural}\tilde Q$. On it the two defining equations are

$$ \tilde Q^{\natural}\tilde R=0 \quad\text{(left)},\qquad \tilde R^{\natural}\tilde Q=0 \quad\text{(right)} , $$

which are plain-product equations in the conjugated first factor.

The Criterion

A nonzero $\tilde Q$ is a zero divisor of $\star$ if and only if its algebraic norm vanishes, $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=\sum_\mu Q_\mu^2=0$.

Theorem (the criterion for the general quaternionic product). For a nonzero $\tilde Q\in\mathbb{B}$, $\tilde Q$ is a zero divisor of $\star$ if and only if

$$ \langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=\sum_{\mu\in\{t,x,y,z\}}Q_\mu^2=0 . $$

Proof. Suppose $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=0$ and $\tilde Q\neq0$. Then

$$ \tilde Q\star\tilde Q=\tilde Q^{\natural}\tilde Q=\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}e_t=0 , $$

so $\tilde R=\tilde Q$ is a nonzero element with $\tilde Q\star\tilde R=0$ and $\tilde Q$ is a left zero divisor; the mirror computation with the factors exchanged gives the right statement. Conversely, suppose $\tilde Q$ is a zero divisor of $\star$: there is a nonzero $\tilde R$ with $\tilde Q\star\tilde R=0$, that is $\tilde Q^{\natural}\tilde R=0$. If $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}\neq0$ then $\tilde Q^{\natural}$ is a unit of the plain product by §The Criterion of Norm and Invertibility in the General Quaternionic Algebra, and multiplying the equation by its inverse gives $\tilde R=0$, a contradiction. Hence $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=0$.

The criterion is the criterion of the plain product read on this product; the two are the same equation because the algebraic norm is the same quadratic form, and the two products are distinguished by the object that turns the equation into a statement — the plain inverse there, the conjugated one-sided solution here.

The Three-Way Classification

Every element of $\mathbb{B}$ is exactly one of three: a unit of the plain product with $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}\neq0$, the zero element, or a zero divisor of $\star$ with $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=0$ and $\tilde Q\neq0$.

Combining the criterion with the invertibility theory of the algebraic norm, the elements of $\mathbb{B}$ are partitioned into three classes, and the classification is the one of Zero Divisors of the General Plain Algebra with the inverse column read for this product.

condition on $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}$ condition on $\tilde Q$ conclusion
$\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}\neq0$ none a unit of the plain product, not a zero divisor of $\star$
$\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=0$ $\tilde Q=0$ the zero element, not a zero divisor
$\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=0$ $\tilde Q\neq0$ a zero divisor of $\star$

The zero divisors are exactly the nonzero elements on which the algebraic norm vanishes. The classification is a classification of the elements by their behaviour under $\star$, and it does not describe them under the other products.

The Algebra Is Not a Division Algebra

The biquaternion algebra is not a division algebra, because it has zero divisors; the statement does not depend on the product, the same elements being the zero divisors of both.

A division algebra is an algebra in which every nonzero element is invertible; for a finite-dimensional algebra this is equivalent to the absence of zero divisors. The biquaternion algebra contains zero divisors, so it is not a division algebra. The statement is the one of Zero Divisors of the Biquaternion Algebra, and it is independent of the product: the same elements are the zero divisors of both products, by the criterion above and the coincidence quoted below.

The Two Families of Zero Divisors

The zero divisors split into the pure ones, $Q_t=0$ with $(\vec Q,\vec Q)=0$, and the non-pure ones, $Q_t\neq0$ with $(\vec Q,\vec Q)=-Q_t^2$, and the split does not depend on the product.

The zero divisors split into two families according to the value of the scalar part, and the split does not depend on the product, because $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=Q_t^2+(\vec Q,\vec Q)$ separates the two cases on the element. A biquaternion is pure when $Q_t=0$ and non-pure when $Q_t\neq0$; on a pure element the criterion is $(\vec Q,\vec Q)=0$, and on a non-pure one it is $(\vec Q,\vec Q)=-Q_t^2$, which can be met by the vector part cancelling the scalar contribution. The two families are developed in Zero Divisors of the Biquaternion Algebra; in this product they are separated not by the square, which vanishes on both, but by the plain square, as the two sections below compute.

Pure Zero Divisors

A nonzero pure element is a zero divisor of $\star$ exactly when $(\vec Q,\vec Q)=0$, and then it is a nilpotent of the plain product, $\tilde Q\tilde Q=0$; these are the isotropic bivectors $\vec\rho+i\vec\rho'$ with $\vec\rho\perp\vec\rho'$ and $\lvert\vec\rho\rvert=\lvert\vec\rho'\rvert$.

Proposition (the square of a pure element). Let $\tilde Q=\vec Q$ be a pure element. Then

$$ \tilde Q\star\tilde Q=(\vec Q,\vec Q)\,e_t , \qquad \tilde Q\tilde Q=-(\vec Q,\vec Q)\,e_t , \qquad \tilde Q^{\natural}=-\tilde Q . $$

Proof. The scalar–vector form of the product at a pure pair is $(\vec Q,\vec Q)e_t$ for the quaternionic product and $-(\vec Q,\vec Q)e_t$ for the plain one, and the natural conjugation negates a pure element.

Theorem (the criterion in the pure case). For a nonzero pure $\tilde Q$ the following are equivalent:

  1. $\tilde Q$ is a zero divisor of $\star$; 2. $(\vec Q,\vec Q)=0$; 3. $\tilde Q\star\tilde Q=0$; 4. $\tilde Q$ is a nilpotent of the plain product, $\tilde Q\tilde Q=0$.

Proof. The equivalence of (1) and (2) is the criterion specialised to $Q_t=0$. The equivalence of (2) with (3) and with (4) is the proposition above, both squares being the same scalar up to sign.

The pure zero divisors are therefore the nilpotents of the plain product: on them the two products have the same square, up to the sign, and in particular a pure zero divisor vanishes when squared in either product. In the older vocabulary they are the isotropic bivectors $\vec\rho+i\vec\rho'$ with $\vec\rho\perp\vec\rho'$ and $|\vec\rho|=|\vec\rho'|$, the parameterisation of Zero Divisors of the General Plain Algebra, §The Bivector Form.

Non-Pure Zero Divisors

A zero divisor with $Q_t\neq0$ has $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=0$ and $\tilde Q\star\tilde Q=0$ while its plain square is the nonzero element $\tilde Q\tilde Q=2Q_t\tilde Q$, and it is a complex multiple $\tilde Q=2Q_t\tilde\Pi$ of a nontrivial plain idempotent.

Proposition (the square of a non-pure element). Let $\tilde Q=Q_te_t+\vec Q$ with $Q_t\neq0$ and $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=0$. Then

$$ \tilde Q\star\tilde Q=0 , \qquad \tilde Q\tilde Q=2Q_t\tilde Q\neq0 . $$

Proof. The quaternionic square is $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}e_t$ and the algebraic norm vanishes by the criterion. The plain square is $(Q_t^2-(\vec Q,\vec Q))e_t+2Q_t\vec Q$, and the criterion $(\vec Q,\vec Q)=-Q_t^2$ turns it into $2Q_t^2e_t+2Q_t\vec Q=2Q_t\tilde Q$, which is nonzero because $Q_t\neq0$ and $\tilde Q\neq0$.

Theorem (the non-pure family is the idempotent family). A non-pure zero divisor is a complex multiple of a nontrivial idempotent of the plain product,

$$ \tilde Q=2Q_t\tilde\Pi , \qquad \tilde\Pi=\frac{\tilde Q}{2Q_t} , $$

and conversely every complex multiple of a nontrivial plain idempotent is a non-pure zero divisor of $\star$.

Proof. The relation $\tilde Q\tilde Q=2Q_t\tilde Q$ of the proposition makes $\tilde\Pi=\tilde Q/(2Q_t)$ an idempotent of the plain product, nontrivial because $\tilde Q\neq0$ and $Q_t\neq0$; the converse is the statement of Idempotents of the General Plain Algebra, §Idempotents and the Non-Pure Zero Divisors, read with the algebraic norm.

Remark (the displacement). The non-pure zero divisor has vanishing quaternionic square, so it is a nilpotent of the quaternionic product, while it is the opposite of a nilpotent in the plain product: its plain square is the nonzero element $2Q_t\tilde Q$. In particular the Hermitian idempotents $\tilde\Pi_+(\tilde\Xi)=\tfrac12(e_t+\tilde\Xi)$ over the real unit vectors $\vec u$ satisfy

$$ \tilde\Pi_+(\tilde\Xi)\star\tilde\Pi_+(\tilde\Xi)=\langle\tilde\Pi_+(\tilde\Xi)\rangle_{\natural}e_t=\tfrac14\bigl(1-(\vec u,\vec u)\bigr)e_t=0 , \qquad \tilde\Pi_+(\tilde\Xi)\tilde\Pi_+(\tilde\Xi)=\tilde\Pi_+(\tilde\Xi) , $$

so the two products see the same element as an idempotent and as a square-zero element. This displacement is the subject of Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the General Quaternionic Algebra.

The Zero Divisor Set

The zero-divisor cone $C=\{\tilde Q\star\tilde Q=0\}=\{\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=0\}$ is a real cone of real dimension $6$ that is not a subspace and is stable under the product; it is the union of the origin, the pure family and the non-pure family.

Definition. The zero-divisor cone of the quaternionic product is the vanishing set of the algebraic norm,

$$ C=\{\tilde Q\in\mathbb{B}:\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=0\}=\{\tilde Q:\tilde Q\star\tilde Q=0\} . $$

Proposition (shape). The set $C$ is a real cone of real dimension $6$ in the real eight-dimensional space $\mathbb{B}$, and it is not a real subspace.

Proof. With $Q_\mu=q_\mu+iq'_\mu$ the real and the imaginary parts, the equation $N=\sum_\mu(q_\mu^2-q'_\mu{}^2)+2i\sum_\mu q_\mu q'_\mu=0$ is one complex equation, hence two real equations, and the set is a cone because $\langle\cdot\rangle_{\natural}$ is homogeneous of degree two; the two equations are independent off the origin, so the real dimension is $8-2=6$. For the failure of linearity, the elements $\vec e_x+i\vec e_y$ and $\vec e_x-i\vec e_y$ have algebraic norm $1-1=0$, while their sum $2\vec e_x$ has algebraic norm $4$.

Corollary. The cone is a multiplicative set: for every $\tilde P$ and every $\tilde Q$ with $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=0$,

$$ \langle\tilde P\star\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=\langle\tilde P\rangle_{\natural}\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=0 , $$

so the product of a cone point with any element is again in the cone, on either side.

Proof. The multiplicativity of the algebraic norm of Norm and Invertibility in the General Quaternionic Algebra, §Multiplicativity, evaluated at $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=0$.

The two families and the cone. The cone is the union of the two families and of the origin: the pure family is the isotropic cone of the vector subspace, of real dimension four, and the non-pure family is the rest, of real dimension six. On a pure element the quaternionic and the plain squares vanish together; on a non-pure one only the quaternionic square vanishes, and the plain square is the nonzero multiple $2Q_t\tilde Q$. The distinction is invisible at the level of the quaternionic square, which is blind to it, and it is read from the scalar part of the element.

The Annihilators

The annihilator of an element is the kernel of the left or the right multiplication, the two coinciding because the natural conjugation is an anti-automorphism of order two: off the cone both vanish, and on the cone each is a complex plane. The annihilators, the coincidence theorem, the operator and the reading on the remarkable subspaces are the companion article Annihilators of the General Quaternionic Algebra, below this article in this category.

The Zero Divisors and the Four General Products

The four general products give four zero-divisor equations but one and the same zero-divisor set, $\{\tilde Q\neq0:\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=0\}$; what the product changes is the equation and the annihilator, not the set.

The four general products of The Four General Products of the Biquaternioid $\mathbb{C}$ Space give four definitions of a zero divisor, hence four zero-divisor sets. For the biquaternion algebra the four sets are one and the same set,

$$ \{\tilde Q\neq0:\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=0\} , $$

and this is a theorem of Zero Divisors in the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternioid $\mathbb{C}$ Space, §The Four General Products Coincide, which this article quotes and does not reprove. What the product changes is the equation and the annihilator: the four left zero-divisor equations are $\tilde Q\tilde R=0$, $\tilde Q^{\natural}\tilde R=0$, $\tilde Q\tilde R^{*}=0$ and $\tilde Q^{\natural}\tilde R^{*}=0$, four different equations with the same solution set, and the annihilators of one element depend on the product, as the coincidence theorem above shows for this one. The one-sided inverse equations and their solutions for the four products are the same article's §The One-Sided Inverse Equations, and the plain reading of the criterion is Zero Divisors of the Biquaternion Algebra.

The Zero Divisors and the Remarkable Subspaces

The centre, the quaternion subspace and the anti-quaternion subspace carry no zero divisor; the vector subspace carries the whole pure family as an isotropic cone of real dimension $4$, the two Hermitian subspaces carry the non-pure family in the star-fixed form, and a general non-pure zero divisor lies in neither.

Since the zero divisors are the nonzero elements of algebraic norm zero, their distribution among the remarkable subspaces is the distribution of the zeros of the quadratic form $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\natural}$, and the signatures are Remarkable Subspaces under the General Quaternionic Algebra.

$\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$, the quaternion subspace none
$i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$, the anti-quaternion subspace none
$\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{B})$, the vector subspace the pure family, an isotropic cone of real dimension four
$\mathbb{M}_{+}$, the Hermitian subspace the non-pure Hermitian elements with $Q_t^2=-(\vec Q,\vec Q)$
$\mathbb{M}_{-}$, the anti-Hermitian subspace the non-pure anti-Hermitian elements with the same condition

The centre, the quaternion subspace and the anti-quaternion subspace carry no zero divisor: the algebraic norm is definite on the last two and vanishes on the first only at the origin. The vector subspace carries the whole pure family. The two Hermitian subspaces carry the non-pure family in the form in which its elements are fixed by the star; a general non-pure zero divisor lies in neither, and the elements $\vec e_x+i\vec e_y$, of the vector subspace, and $e_t+i\vec e_x$, of the Hermitian subspace, are the two standard witnesses.

Summary

For the general quaternionic bilinear product $\tilde P\star\tilde Q=\tilde P^{\natural}\tilde Q$, a nonzero element is a zero divisor exactly when its algebraic norm vanishes, $\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=\sum_\mu Q_\mu^2=0$, and the zero divisors are the nonzero points of the zero-divisor cone, a real cone of dimension six that is not a subspace and is stable under the product. The cone is the union of the pure family, the plain nilpotents $\vec\rho+i\vec\rho'$ with $\vec\rho\perp\vec\rho'$ and equal lengths, on which the quaternionic and the plain squares vanish together, and the non-pure family, the complex multiples of the nontrivial plain idempotents, on which the quaternionic square vanishes while the plain square is the nonzero multiple $2Q_t\tilde Q$. The left and the right annihilator of a zero divisor are each a complex plane containing the element, and both are $0$ for a unit; the two annihilators coincide for every element, because the natural conjugation is a $\mathbb{C}$-linear anti-automorphism of order two that turns the one equation into the other. The zero-divisor set is the same set as for the plain product, a coincidence of the algebra proved in Zero Divisors in the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternioid $\mathbb{C}$ Space; what this product changes is the equation, the cone-shaped square, and the annihilator, which is symmetric here where the sesquilinear products make it conjugate-symmetric.
