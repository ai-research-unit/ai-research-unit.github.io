The Left Ideals and the Image of a Left Multiplication

Introduction

The subject. A left multiplication $L_a$ has an image and a kernel, and the image is a left ideal of the algebra: the set of the multiples $Xa$ of the parameter on the left. The article computes the two. On the sector where the multiplicative norm does not vanish the parameter is invertible, the image is the whole algebra, the kernel is the zero subspace, and the operator has rank four. Off that sector the image is a minimal left ideal, of complex dimension two, and the kernel is again trivial. The two pictures together describe every left multiplication.

The order of the article. The image and its ideal property come first, then the kernel. The invertible parameters are treated second, the minimal ideals third, and the list of all left ideals fourth.

It assumes Operators Acting on One Side, The Minimal Left Ideals of the Biquaternion Algebra and Zero Divisors and Invertibles in the Biquaternion Algebra. The row of the elements is where the minimal ideals are read; here the same ideals are read as the images of the operators, and the operator adds the kernel and the rank to the picture.

The result in one line. The image of $L_a$ is the left ideal generated by $a$; it is the whole algebra when $a$ is invertible, a minimal ideal when $a$ is a primitive idempotent, and the operator is always injective.

Boundaries. The two families and their intersection are The One-Sided Operators; the two-sided images, where the rank drops on the products of degenerate parameters, are The Two-Sided Operators and the Segre Cone; the Peirce reading of the minimal ideals is The Operators of the Peirce Decomposition.

The Image as a Left Ideal

Definition. For an element $a$, the image and the kernel of the left multiplication are $$ \operatorname{im}L_a=\{aX:X\in\mathbb{B}\}=\mathbb{B}a,\qquad \ker L_a=\{X:aX=0\}. $$

Proposition. The image is a left ideal: it is closed under addition and under multiplication on the left by any element of the algebra.

Proof. Two multiples add to $a(X+Y)$; and $Y(aX)=(Ya)X$ is again a left multiple of $a$, by the element $Ya$. The closure on the right is not claimed and does not hold in general.

Corollary. The image is the smallest left ideal containing $a$, so the left ideal generated by an element is exactly the image of its left multiplication.

Proof. Every left ideal containing $a$ contains $Xa$ for every $X$, hence contains the image; and the image is itself such an ideal.

Corollary. Two elements generate the same left ideal exactly when each is a left multiple of the other.

Proof. The two inclusions $\mathbb{B}a\subseteq\mathbb{B}b$ and $\mathbb{B}b\subseteq\mathbb{B}a$ say that $a$ is a left multiple of $b$ and conversely.

The reading. The image of a left multiplication is not a subspace chosen by the operator; it is the ideal that the parameter carries with it. Every statement about the image is therefore a statement about the element, and the operator is only the vehicle.

The Kernel and the Injectivity

Theorem. Every left multiplication is injective: $\ker L_a=\{0\}$ for every nonzero $a$.

Proof. If $aX=0$ with $a\neq0$, multiply on the left by $a^{-1}$, which exists on the sector of the row where the multiplicative norm does not vanish; then $X=0$. Off that sector the conclusion is the same, since a nonzero element of the algebra never annihilates on one side alone.

Corollary. A left multiplication has rank four or rank two according to whether its parameter is invertible, and it never has rank one or three.

Proof. Injectivity gives $\dim\operatorname{im}L_a=4$ when the image is the whole algebra; when the image is a minimal left ideal its dimension is two; the intermediate values would require a kernel of dimension one or three, which injectivity forbids.

Corollary. The kernel of a left multiplication is never a nontrivial subspace, so the operator is never a projection onto a proper subspace along a nonzero complement.

Proof. The theorem.

The reading. Injectivity is the one-sided form of the absence of one-sided zero divisors. Reading the algebra from the left, no element annihilates anything; the zero divisors of the algebra are two-sided phenomena, and they appear in the image and not in the kernel.

The Invertible Parameters

Theorem. For an element $a$ with $N(a)\neq0$ the left multiplication is a bijection and its image is the whole algebra.

Proof. An element is invertible exactly when its multiplicative norm does not vanish; the inverse gives $X=a^{-1}(aX)$, so every $X$ is in the image; injectivity is the preceding theorem. A bijection of a four-dimensional space onto itself has rank four.

Corollary. On the sector of the row all left multiplications are invertible operators, and their inverses are the left multiplications by the inverses of their parameters.

Proof. $L_aL_{a^{-1}}=L_{aa^{-1}}=L_{e_t}=\mathrm{I}$.

Corollary. The image of a left multiplication is the whole algebra for every invertible parameter, so the only proper images come from the degenerate parameters.

Proof. The theorem, and the definition of the degenerate elements as those of vanishing norm.

The reading. The one-sided operators of this row are almost all bijections: the exceptional parameters form the vanishing cone of the multiplicative norm, of real codimension two, and even there the image is as large as a left ideal can be. The rank of a one-sided operator therefore carries no information beyond the dichotomy invertible or idempotent.

The Minimal Left Ideals

Definition. A left ideal is minimal when it contains no nonzero left ideal other than itself. For a primitive idempotent $\tilde\Pi$ the ideal generated by it is $$ S(\tilde\Pi)=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi=\operatorname{im}L_{\tilde\Pi}. $$

Theorem. The image of the left multiplication by a primitive idempotent is a minimal left ideal of complex dimension two.

Proof. The idempotent satisfies $\tilde\Pi^2=\tilde\Pi$, so the image consists of the elements $X\tilde\Pi$ and is closed on the left. A nonzero element $Y\tilde\Pi$ of the ideal generates it, because multiplication by $Y$ on the left carries $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi$ onto itself and is surjective there. Hence no proper nonzero left ideal lies inside. The dimension is two, since the idempotent is a zero divisor and its left multiplication has rank two.

Corollary. The primitive idempotents are parametrised by the unit real axes, $\tilde\Pi_{\pm}(\vec u)=\tfrac12(e_t\pm i\vec u)$, and the minimal left ideals by the lines of the plane of the spinor space.

Proof. The idempotents solve $\tilde\Pi^2=\tilde\Pi$ with $\tilde\Pi\neq0,e_t$, which forces the vector part to be $\pm\tfrac12 i\vec u$ with $\vec u$ a unit real vector; the two signs give the two idempotents of a pair, and a pair depends on the axis, a point of the real projective plane, which in the complex reading is a projective line.

Theorem. The group of unit real quaternions carries the set of minimal left ideals transitively onto itself, and the carrier is left multiplication by the unit.

Proof. A unit $u$ satisfies $u\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{B}$, so $u\,\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi=\mathbb{B}(u\tilde\Pi)$; the element $u\tilde\Pi u^{-1}$ is again a primitive idempotent, and the unit quaternions act transitively on the axes. Hence every minimal ideal is carried to every other.

The reading. The left ideals of the algebra are the lines of a plane: one minimal ideal for each axis, and the axes form a projective line. The family is a single orbit of the unit group, and the operator that carries one member to another is a left multiplication by a unit.

The Lattice of the Left Ideals

Theorem. The left ideals of the algebra are the zero ideal, the minimal ideals $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi$, and the algebra itself.

Proof. Let $I$ be a nonzero left ideal and $a\in I$ nonzero. If $a$ is invertible then $I$ contains $e_t$ and is the whole algebra. If $a$ is a zero divisor of rank one, then $I$ contains the minimal ideal generated by $a$; a larger left ideal contains an element outside that ideal, and that element is either invertible, giving the whole algebra, or generates a second minimal ideal whose sum with the first is the whole algebra, distinct minimal ideals intersecting in zero with dimensions adding to four.

Corollary. The left ideals form a lattice of height two, with a projective line of atoms, and the sum of two distinct atoms is the whole algebra.

Proof. The theorem, and the dimension count $2+2=4$.

Corollary. The image of a left multiplication is never an ideal of dimension three or of dimension one.

Proof. The list, with the dimensions $0,2,4$.

The reading. The one-sided images are as coarse as they can be. There are three kinds of left ideal and no more, and the operator distinguishes them by the nature of its parameter alone. The finer stratification of the row is not here; it is on the two-sided side, where a product of two parameters can fail to be invertible in more than one way.

Summary of Notation

Symbol Meaning
$\operatorname{im}L_a=\mathbb{B}a$ the image, a left ideal
$\ker L_a=\{0\}$ the kernel, always trivial
$N(a)\neq0$ the invertible parameters
$S(\tilde\Pi)=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi$ the minimal left ideal of an idempotent, dimension two
$\tilde\Pi_{\pm}(\vec u)=\tfrac12(e_t\pm i\vec u)$ the primitive idempotents, one pair per axis
$\{0\},\ \mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi,\ \mathbb{B}$ the three kinds of left ideal
