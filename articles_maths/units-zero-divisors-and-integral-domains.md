# __Units, Zero Divisors and Integral Domains__

## Introduction

This article develops the **multiplicative** theory of a ring: which elements can be inverted, which products vanish, and which rings are free enough of vanishing products to admit cancellation. The additive theory and the theory of ideals are assumed from *Rings*; the two are joined here by the observation that an element is a unit exactly when the ideal it generates is the whole ring, and that a ring has no zero divisors exactly when the product of two nonzero elements cannot be zero.

Throughout, unless stated otherwise, $R$ denotes a commutative ring with identity $1 \neq 0$. The corpus takes the commutative ring as its default base, and this article marks each point at which commutativity, the absence of zero divisors, characteristic zero, or the invertibility of $2$ is actually used.

Three classes are separated. A **field** inverts every nonzero element; a **division ring** does the same without commutativity; an **integral domain** inverts too little to be a field but has no zero divisors, so that cancellation holds. The vocabulary fixed here — unit, associate, zero divisor, nilpotent, idempotent, characteristic — is used throughout the rest of the category, in particular in *Factorization: PID, UFD and Euclidean Domains*, where irreducibility and primality are defined against the notion of a unit, and in *Localization and the Fraction Field*, where the multiplicative sets inverted are built from the elements that are not zero divisors.

## Units

### The Group of Units

**Definition.** An element $u \in R$ is a **unit** if there exists $v \in R$ with

$$
u v = 1.
$$

The element $v$ is then unique: if also $u v' = 1$, then $v = v(uv') = (vu)v' = v'$. It is written $u^{-1}$ and called the **inverse** of $u$. The set of units of $R$ is written $R^\times$.

**Proposition.** Let $R$ be a commutative ring with $1 \neq 0$.

**(a)** $R^\times$ is an abelian group under multiplication, with identity $1$.

**(b)** If $u, v \in R^\times$, then $(uv)^{-1} = u^{-1} v^{-1}$.

**(c)** If $u \in R^\times$, then $-u \in R^\times$, with $(-u)^{-1} = -u^{-1}$.

**(d)** $u \in R^\times$ if and only if the principal ideal $(u)$ equals $R$.

**Proof.** (a) Multiplication is associative and commutative in $R$, and $1$ is a unit since $1 \cdot 1 = 1$; the inverse of a unit is a unit since $u u^{-1} = 1$ exhibits $u$ as the inverse of $u^{-1}$. (b) $(uv)(u^{-1}v^{-1}) = (u u^{-1})(v v^{-1}) = 1$. (c) $(-u)(-u^{-1}) = u u^{-1} = 1$. (d) If $u v = 1$ then $R = (1) \subseteq (u) \subseteq R$, so $(u) = R$. Conversely if $(u) = R$ then $1 \in (u)$, so $1 = v u$ for some $v$, and $u$ is a unit. $\square$

The functorial reading of (a) is that $R \mapsto R^\times$ is a group-valued invariant of the ring, and much of what follows measures a ring by how large or small this group is.

**Remark.** The ideal generated by a unit is the whole ring, so **no proper ideal contains a unit**. Consequently every element of a proper ideal is a non-unit. The converse fails in general: in $\mathbb{Z}$ the element $2$ is a non-unit, but $-2 + 3 = 1$ shows that the non-units are not closed under addition, so they do not form an ideal. A ring in which the non-units do form an ideal is called **local**; this is taken up in *Localization and the Fraction Field*.

### Associates

**Definition.** Two elements $a, b \in R$ are **associates**, written $a \sim b$, if there is a unit $u \in R^\times$ with

$$
a = u b.
$$

**Proposition.** Association is an equivalence relation on $R$; if $a \sim b$ then $a \mid b$ and $b \mid a$; and conversely, if $a \mid b$ and $b \mid a$ and $a$ is not a zero divisor, then $a \sim b$.

**Proof.** Reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity follow from $1 \in R^\times$, from the closure of $R^\times$ under inverses, and from its closure under products. If $a = ub$ then $b \mid a$; and $b = u^{-1}a$, so $a \mid b$. Conversely if $b = a x$ and $a = b y$ then $a = a x y$, that is, $a(1 - xy) = 0$. If $a$ is not a zero divisor then $1 - xy = 0$, so $xy = 1$ and $x$ is a unit; if $a = 0$ then $b = 0$ and $a = 1 \cdot b$. $\square$

**Corollary.** In an integral domain, $a \sim b$ if and only if $a \mid b$ and $b \mid a$, for all $a, b \in R$.

The hypothesis on $a$ in the proposition is exactly what the argument needs: it produces only $a(1 - xy) = 0$, which forces $xy = 1$ precisely when no nonzero element annihilates $a$. Taking $a = 0$ shows the exceptional case: $0$ is divisible by every element, so $a \mid 0$ always holds, while $0 \mid a$ holds only for $a = 0$. Divisibility is therefore a partial order on the associate classes of an integral domain, and it is used in that setting throughout the factorization theory.

### Units in Examples

| Ring | Group of units | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{Z}$ | $\{\pm 1\}$ | $uv = 1$ forces $\lvert u \rvert = 1$ |
| $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ | $\{a + n\mathbb{Z} : \gcd(a,n) = 1\}$, of order $\varphi(n)$ | Bézout's identity |
| $F[x]$, $F$ a field | $F^\times$, the nonzero constants | $\deg(fg) = \deg f + \deg g$ |
| $F[[x]]$ | series with nonzero constant term | recursive inversion of coefficients |
| $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}]$ | $\{\pm(1+\sqrt{2})^n : n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ | units of Pell's equation $a^2 - 2b^2 = \pm 1$ |
| $\mathbb{F}_p$ | $\mathbb{F}_p^\times$, cyclic of order $p-1$ | finite subgroup of a field's multiplicative group |

The third row is the model for the Euclidean domains of *Factorization: PID, UFD and Euclidean Domains*: a nonconstant polynomial is never a unit, and degree is additive, which is exactly what a Euclidean function records.

**Example (a local ring).** In the dual numbers $\mathbb{D}'_R = R[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$ of *Dual Numbers Algebra*, an element $a + b\varepsilon$ is a unit exactly when $a$ is a unit of $R$, with

$$
(a + b\varepsilon)^{-1} = a^{-1} - a^{-1} b a^{-1} \varepsilon,
$$

which for commutative $R$ reads $(a + b\varepsilon)^{-1} = a^{-1} - b a^{-2} \varepsilon$. When $R$ is a field the non-units are exactly the elements of the ideal $(\varepsilon)$, so $\mathbb{D}'_R$ is then a local ring with maximal ideal $(\varepsilon)$ — the prototype of a local ring, and the reason the dual numbers carry an infinitesimal calculus on a single chart.

**Example (zero divisors among non-units; non-units among zero divisors).** In the split complex numbers $\mathbb{D} = \mathbb{R}[j]/(j^2 - 1)$ of *Split-Complex Algebra*, an element $a + bj$ has norm $N(a+bj) = (a+bj)(a-bj) = a^2 - b^2$, and

$$
(a + bj)(a - bj) = a^2 - b^2.
$$

Hence $a + bj$ is a unit precisely when $a^2 \neq b^2$, with inverse $(a - bj)/(a^2 - b^2)$, whereas it is a nonzero zero divisor precisely when $a^2 = b^2$ with $(a,b) \neq (0,0)$. So $\mathbb{D}$ has nonzero zero divisors and is not an integral domain, and its units are exactly the elements of nonzero norm. Since $a^2 = b^2$ for a nonzero element describes the two lines $a = \pm b$, the zero divisors of $\mathbb{D}$ are exactly the nonzero multiples of the idempotents $e_\pm = \tfrac{1}{2}(1 \pm j)$.

## Zero Divisors

### Definition and First Properties

**Definition.** A nonzero element $a \in R$ is a **zero divisor** if there is a nonzero $b \in R$ with

$$
a b = 0.
$$

The condition $a \neq 0$ is part of the definition, as in *Rings*, §8; without it $0$ would be a zero divisor in every nonzero ring. In a commutative ring the relation is symmetric in $a$ and $b$, so a product of two nonzero elements is zero exactly when each factor is a zero divisor.

**Proposition.** Let $a, b \in R$.

**(a)** If $a$ is a zero divisor and $ab \neq 0$, then $ab$ is a zero divisor.

**(b)** If $a$ is a zero divisor then $a$ is not a unit.

**(c)** The set $S$ of elements of $R$ that are not zero divisors is multiplicatively closed and contains $1$.

**Proof.** (a) If $ac = 0$ with $c \neq 0$ then $(ab)c = 0$ and $c \neq 0$, while $ab \neq 0$ by hypothesis. (b) If $u$ is a unit and $ua = 0$ then $a = u^{-1}ua = 0$, so no nonzero element annihilates $u$. (c) $1$ is not a zero divisor since $1 \cdot b = b$. Let $s, t \in S$. If either is $0$ then $st = 0$, which is not a zero divisor; otherwise both are nonzero, and $st \neq 0$, since $st = 0$ would make $s$ a zero divisor by annihilating the nonzero element $t$. If $c \neq 0$ then $tc \neq 0$, because $t$ is not a zero divisor, and $(st)c = s(tc) = 0$ would then make $s$ a zero divisor; so $st$ annihilates no nonzero element and lies in $S$. $\square$

The set $S$ of non-zero-divisors is multiplicatively closed, and because the definition above excludes $0$, the element $0$ itself belongs to $S$; localizing at a set containing $0$ gives the zero ring. The multiplicative set used for the **total ring of fractions** of $R$ is accordingly the set of *nonzero* non-zero-divisors, which for a domain is $R \setminus \{0\}$ and for which the construction yields the fraction field (*Localization and the Fraction Field*).

**Example.** In $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ with $n \geq 2$, an element $a + n\mathbb{Z}$ is a unit exactly when $\gcd(a,n) = 1$ and a zero divisor exactly when $1 < \gcd(a,n) < n$; the only remaining element is $0$. The dichotomy is a special case of the following general fact about finite rings.

**Theorem.** In a finite commutative ring $R$ with $1 \neq 0$, every nonzero element is either a unit or a zero divisor.

**Proof.** Let $a \in R$ be nonzero. The map $x \mapsto ax$ is an endomorphism of the additive group, and it fails to be injective exactly when $ax = ay$ for distinct $x, y$, that is, when $a(x - y) = 0$ for a nonzero $x - y$: that is, exactly when $a$ is a zero divisor. Since $R$ is finite, injectivity is equivalent to surjectivity, so $a$ is not a zero divisor if and only if $ax = 1$ for some $x$, that is, if and only if $a$ is a unit. $\square$

### Nilpotent Elements

**Definition.** An element $a \in R$ is **nilpotent** if $a^n = 0$ for some integer $n \geq 1$. The least such $n$ is the **nilpotency index** of $a$. The set of all nilpotent elements of $R$ is written $\operatorname{nil}(R)$ and called the **nilradical** of $R$.

**Proposition.** Let $a, b \in R$ and let $u \in R^\times$.

**(a)** If $a \neq 0$ is nilpotent, then $a$ is a zero divisor.

**(b)** If $a$ is nilpotent then $u - a$ is a unit.

**(c)** $\operatorname{nil}(R)$ is an ideal of $R$, and $R/\operatorname{nil}(R)$ has no nonzero nilpotent elements.

**(d)** The only nilpotent idempotent is $0$.

**Proof.** (a) If $a^n = 0$ with $n$ minimal, then $a \neq 0$ and $a \cdot a^{n-1} = 0$ with $a^{n-1} \neq 0$. (b) It suffices to show that $1 - x$ is a unit for nilpotent $x$, since then $u - a = u(1 - u^{-1}a)$ is a product of units. If $x^n = 0$ then

$$
(1 - x)(1 + x + x^2 + \cdots + x^{n-1}) = 1 - x^n = 1.
$$

(c) If $a^m = 0$ and $b^n = 0$, then every term of the binomial expansion of $(a + b)^{m+n-1}$ contains a power $a^i b^{\,m+n-1-i}$ with either $i \geq m$ or $m+n-1-i \geq n$, so $(a+b)^{m+n-1} = 0$; and if $a$ is nilpotent then $ra$ is nilpotent for every $r$, since $(ra)^m = r^m a^m$. This uses commutativity, so that $a$ and $b$ commute. Finally, if $r + \operatorname{nil}(R)$ is nilpotent in the quotient then $r^n \in \operatorname{nil}(R)$, so $r^{nm} = 0$ for some $m$ and $r$ is itself nilpotent. (d) If $e^2 = e$ and $e^n = 0$, then $e = e^n = 0$. $\square$

**Remark.** The converse of (a) is false: in $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ the class of $2$ is a zero divisor, since $2 \cdot 3 = 0$, but $2^n$ is $2$ or $4$ for every $n \geq 1$ and is never $0$. Nilpotence is therefore strictly stronger than being a zero divisor.

**Example (nilpotents without zero divisors of the same shape).** In $\mathbb{Z}/p^2\mathbb{Z}$ the nilradical is the ideal $(p) = \{0, p, 2p, \ldots, (p-1)p\}$, of index $p$; the element $p$ has nilpotency index $2$. The quotient $\mathbb{Z}/p^2\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{F}_p$ discards precisely the nilpotents, as part (c) predicts. In $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ the nilradical is $0$, so $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ is **reduced** (has no nonzero nilpotent) even though it has zero divisors.

### Idempotents and Product Decompositions

**Definition.** An element $e \in R$ is **idempotent** if $e^2 = e$. The idempotents $0$ and $1$ are the **trivial** idempotents; a ring with no others is **connected**.

Idempotents detect product decompositions. If $e$ is idempotent then so is $1 - e$, and

$$
e(1 - e) = e - e^2 = 0,
$$

so the two idempotents annihilate each other. Multiplicatively, $e$ behaves as the identity of the piece $Re$ and $1 - e$ as the identity of the piece $R(1-e)$.

**Theorem.** Let $e \in R$ be idempotent and put $f = 1 - e$. Then the map

$$
\varphi : R \to Re \times Rf, \qquad \varphi(r) = (re, rf),
$$

is a ring isomorphism. Conversely, if $R \cong R_1 \times R_2$ with both factors nonzero, then $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ are nontrivial idempotents of $R$, so $R$ is disconnected.

**Proof.** The map $\varphi$ is a ring homomorphism since $e$ and $f$ are central and idempotent, and $\varphi$ is injective because $r = re + rf = 0$ whenever $re = rf = 0$. It is surjective because an element $(r_1 e, r_2 f)$ is the image of $r_1 e + r_2 f$. For the converse, the idempotents $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ are nonzero and neither is $1$, since $R_1$ and $R_2$ are nonzero. $\square$

**Corollary (Chinese remainder theorem).** Let $I, J$ be ideals of $R$ with $I + J = R$. Then

$$
R/(I \cap J) \cong R/I \times R/J, \qquad I \cap J = IJ.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Write $1 = i + j$ with $i \in I$, $j \in J$. The class $e = j + I \cap J$ is idempotent, and the theorem applies to $e$; the identification of $I \cap J$ with $IJ$ uses that $IJ \subseteq I \cap J$ always, while $xy = x(i+j)y = xiy + xjy \in IJ$ for $x \in I$, $y \in J$. $\square$

**Example.** In $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ the idempotents are the classes of $0, 1, 4, 9$. Since $12 = 4 \cdot 3$ with $\gcd(4,3) = 1$, the Chinese remainder theorem gives

$$
\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z},
$$

and under this isomorphism the idempotent $4$ corresponds to $(0,1)$ and $9$ to $(1,0)$. There are $2^2 = 4$ idempotents, one for each choice of a trivial idempotent in each factor.

**Remark.** The idempotent decomposition of a ring into two factors can be iterated: when an idempotent of one factor splits further, the ring decomposes into more **indecomposable** pieces, and a connected ring is one that admits no such splitting at all. The extreme case is a domain, in which the only idempotents are $0$ and $1$, so a domain is connected: if $e \neq 0$ is idempotent then $e(1-e) = 0$ forces $1 - e = 0$.

### Zero Divisors in Examples

| Ring | Zero divisors | Nilpotents | Idempotents |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{Z}$ | none | $0$ | $0, 1$ |
| $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ | classes of $2, 3, 4$ | $0$ | $0, 1, 3, 4$ |
| $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ | classes of $2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 10$ | $0, 6$ | $0, 1, 4, 9$ |
| $\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$ | classes of $2, 4, 6$ | $2$ (index $3$), $4$ (index $2$), $6$ (index $3$) | $0, 1$ |
| $F[x]$, $F$ a field | none | $0$ | $0, 1$ |
| $\mathbb{D}'_R$ | all $b\varepsilon$, $b \neq 0$ | all $b\varepsilon$ | $0, 1$ |
| $\mathbb{D}$ | $a \pm aj$, $a \neq 0$ | $0$ | $0, 1, e_+, e_-$ |

In $\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$ the idempotents are trivial although the ring is not a domain, so the converse of the remark above fails: connectedness does not imply that there are no zero divisors. In $\mathbb{D}$ the idempotents $e_\pm$ are exactly the zero divisors up to scaling, and the isomorphism $\mathbb{D} \cong \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}$ of *Split-Complex Algebra* is the idempotent decomposition of the theorem.

## Cancellation and Integral Domains

### Integral Domains

**Definition.** An **integral domain** is a commutative ring $R$ with $1 \neq 0$ and no zero divisors: for all $a, b \in R$,

$$
a b = 0 \implies a = 0 \text{ or } b = 0.
$$

**Proposition (cancellation).** Let $R$ be an integral domain and $a \in R$ with $a \neq 0$. If $ab = ac$ then $b = c$.

**Proof.** $a(b - c) = 0$ and $a \neq 0$, so $b - c = 0$. $\square$

Cancellation is the property that makes the notions of divisibility of *Factorization: PID, UFD and Euclidean Domains* well behaved: $a \mid b$ and $b \mid a$ then force $a \sim b$, as shown in the section on associates.

**Proposition.** Let $R$ be an integral domain.

**(a)** Every subring of $R$ containing $1$ is an integral domain.

**(b)** The polynomial ring $R[x]$ is an integral domain, and $\deg(fg) = \deg f + \deg g$ for nonzero $f, g$. In particular $(R[x])^\times = R^\times$.

**(c)** If $R$ is a field then $R$ is an integral domain.

**Proof.** (a) A subring with the same $1$ inherits the absence of zero divisors. (b) If $f$ and $g$ are nonzero with leading coefficients $a$ and $b$, then the coefficient of $x^{\deg f + \deg g}$ in $fg$ is $ab \neq 0$, so $fg \neq 0$ and the degree formula holds. If $fg = 1$ then $\deg f + \deg g = 0$, so both are constants, and the constants that are units of $R[x]$ are exactly the units of $R$. (c) A field has no zero divisors since $ab = 0$ with $a \neq 0$ gives $b = a^{-1}ab = 0$. $\square$

**Proposition.** Every finite integral domain is a field.

**Proof.** Let $R$ be a finite domain and $a \in R$ nonzero. The map $x \mapsto ax$ is injective by cancellation, hence surjective as $R$ is finite, so $ax = 1$ for some $x$. Hence $a$ is a unit. $\square$

**Corollary.** $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a field if and only if $n$ is prime; for composite $n$ it has zero divisors.

**Proof.** For prime $n$ the ring $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a finite integral domain, hence a field. For composite $n = ab$ with $1 < a, b < n$, the classes of $a$ and $b$ are nonzero and multiply to zero. $\square$

This is the first appearance of the general principle that a finite commutative ring is a field exactly when it has no zero divisors, which underlies the classification of finite fields in *Finite Fields*.

### Domains and the Invertibility of 2

Several standard constructions require that $2$ be invertible, that is, that $1 + 1 \in R^\times$. This holds in every field of characteristic not $2$, fails in every ring of characteristic $2$, and fails in $\mathbb{Z}$, where $2$ is not a unit even though the characteristic is $0$.

**Proposition.** Let $R$ be a commutative ring with $1 \neq 0$. Then $2 \in R^\times$ if and only if there is an element $\tfrac{1}{2} \in R$ with $2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = 1$, and in that case $\operatorname{char} R \neq 2$.

**Proof.** The first statement is the definition. If $2$ were a unit and $\operatorname{char} R = 2$ then $2 = 0$, so $1 = 2 \cdot 2^{-1} = 0$, contradicting $1 \neq 0$. $\square$

The converse of the second statement fails in $\mathbb{Z}$. Where $2$ is invertible, an idempotent $e$ produces the idempotents $\tfrac{1}{2}(1 + s)$ and $\tfrac{1}{2}(1-s)$ for a square root $s$ of $1$; without it, those expressions are unavailable. This is the arithmetic content of the split-complex versus complex distinction, where $\tfrac{1}{2}(1 \pm j)$ exists over $\mathbb{R}$ because $\operatorname{char} \mathbb{R} = 0$.

## Characteristic

### Definition

The **characteristic** of a unital ring $R$ is the least positive integer $n$ with $n \cdot 1 = 0$, if such an $n$ exists, and $0$ otherwise. Equivalently, $\operatorname{char} R$ is the order of the element $1$ in the additive group $(R, +)$, and it is the non-negative generator of the kernel of the unique unital ring homomorphism

$$
\chi : \mathbb{Z} \to R, \qquad \chi(n) = n \cdot 1,
$$

whose image is the **prime subring** of $R$, the smallest subring of $R$ containing $1$. Thus $\operatorname{char} R = n$ means that the prime subring of $R$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, and $R$ carries a copy of $\mathbb{F}_p$ exactly when $\operatorname{char} R = p$.

**Examples.** The rings $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$ have characteristic $0$. The ring $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has characteristic $n$ and prime subring $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$; in particular $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ has characteristic $6$, so its prime subring is the whole ring, and its characteristic is composite.

### Characteristic of a Domain

**Theorem.** Let $R$ be an integral domain. Then $\operatorname{char} R$ is either $0$ or a prime number.

**Proof.** Let $n = \operatorname{char} R$ be finite and suppose $n = ab$ with $a, b > 1$. Then

$$
(a \cdot 1)(b \cdot 1) = (ab) \cdot 1 = n \cdot 1 = 0,
$$

and since $R$ is a domain one factor is $0$, so $a \cdot 1 = 0$ or $b \cdot 1 = 0$. Either contradicts the minimality of $n$. Hence $n$ has no proper factorisation and $n$ is prime. $\square$

**Corollary.** A ring of composite characteristic, such as $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, is never an integral domain. A domain of prime characteristic $p$ contains a copy of $\mathbb{F}_p$, and a domain of characteristic $0$ contains a copy of $\mathbb{Z}$.

The prime subfield of a **field** was treated in *Fields*, §3: it is $\mathbb{Q}$ in characteristic $0$ and $\mathbb{F}_p$ in characteristic $p$. That statement is special to fields, because $\mathbb{Z}$ is not a field; for a general domain of characteristic $0$ the prime subring is only $\mathbb{Z}$, and the domain embeds in a field only after passing to its fraction field.

**Proposition (freshman's dream).** Let $R$ be a commutative ring of characteristic $p$, prime. Then for all $x, y \in R$ and all $n \geq 1$,

$$
(x + y)^{p^n} = x^{p^n} + y^{p^n}, \qquad (xy)^{p^n} = x^{p^n} y^{p^n}.
$$

**Proof.** For $n = 1$, expand $(x+y)^p$; each binomial coefficient $\binom{p}{k}$ with $0 < k < p$ is divisible by $p$ and hence vanishes in $R$. The general case follows by iteration. $\square$

This identity is the basis of the Frobenius endomorphism of *Finite Fields* and *Ring and Field Automorphisms*; it fails in characteristic $0$, where $(x+y)^2 \neq x^2 + y^2$ already over $\mathbb{Z}$.

### Characteristic and Subrings

**Proposition.** Let $S$ be a subring of $R$ containing $1$. Then $\operatorname{char} S = \operatorname{char} R$, and the prime subring of $S$ is the prime subring of $R$.

**Proof.** The element $1$ is the same in both rings by the definition of a subring of a unital ring, so the additive order of $1$ is the same, and the subring generated by $1$ is the same. $\square$

**Proposition.** For commutative rings $R$ and $S$ with identity,

$$
\operatorname{char}(R \times S) = \operatorname{lcm}(\operatorname{char} R, \operatorname{char} S),
$$

where the least common multiple is $0$ if either characteristic is $0$, and the product $R \times S$ is unital with identity $(1,1)$.

**Proof.** The element $n(1,1) = (n \cdot 1, n \cdot 1)$ vanishes exactly when $n$ is a multiple of both characteristics. $\square$

## Division Rings and Fields

### Division Rings

**Definition.** A **division ring** is a ring $R$ with $1 \neq 0$ in which every nonzero element is a unit. A division ring need not be commutative.

The quaternions $\mathbb{H}$ of *Quaternion Algebra* are the standard division ring that is not a field: every nonzero quaternion $Q$ has inverse $\bar{Q}/N(Q)$ with $N(Q) = Q\bar{Q} \neq 0$, and multiplication is not commutative. The failure of commutativity is the only difference from a field:

**Proposition.** A commutative division ring is a field.

**Proof.** This is immediate from the definitions. $\square$

**Theorem (Wedderburn's little theorem).** Every finite division ring is a field.

**Proof.** This is a standard theorem of ring theory; the standard proof examines the conjugacy class equation of the finite multiplicative group and shows it cannot be satisfied in a noncommutative division ring. $\square$

Thus no finite division ring is noncommutative, which is why every finite example of a division ring that is not a field, in particular $\mathbb{H}$, is infinite.

### Fields Viewed as Rings

**Definition.** A **field** is a commutative ring $F$ with $1 \neq 0$ in which every nonzero element is a unit.

**Proposition.** Let $F$ be a commutative ring with $1 \neq 0$. Then $F$ is a field if and only if its only ideals are $0$ and $F$.

**Proof.** If $F$ is a field and $I \neq 0$ is an ideal, choose $a \in I$ nonzero; then $1 = a^{-1} a \in I$, so $I = F$. Conversely, if the only ideals are $0$ and $F$ and $a \neq 0$, the ideal $(a)$ is nonzero, hence $F$, so $1 \in (a)$, and $a$ is a unit. $\square$

**Corollary.** A unital ring homomorphism $\varphi : F \to S$ from a field $F$ to a nonzero ring $S$ is injective.

**Proof.** The kernel is an ideal of $F$, hence $0$ or $F$. It cannot be $F$, since $\varphi(1) = 1 \neq 0$ in $S$. Hence $\ker \varphi = 0$. $\square$

This injectivity is used constantly: it is why the prime field of a field embeds uniquely, and why a field has no nontrivial quotient.

### The Hierarchy of Classes

The following table classifies the rings of this article by the four properties that separate them. A "no" entry means that a ring of the class need not have the property, not that it never does.

| Class | Commutative | $1 \neq 0$ | No zero divisors | Every nonzero element a unit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ring | no | yes | no | no |
| Commutative ring | yes | yes | no | no |
| Integral domain | yes | yes | yes | no |
| Field | yes | yes | yes | yes |
| Division ring | no | yes | yes | yes |

The two marked "no" in the last column, integral domains and commutative rings, are separated from the fields by the existence of a nonzero non-unit; for $\mathbb{Z}$ the element $2$ is such, and the general construction that repairs this is the fraction field of *Localization and the Fraction Field*.

**Theorem.** Let $R$ be a commutative ring with $1 \neq 0$. The following are equivalent: (a) $R$ is a field; (b) every nonzero element is a unit; (c) the only ideals are $0$ and $R$; (d) every unital homomorphism from $R$ to a nonzero ring is injective.

**Proof.** (a) $\Leftrightarrow$ (b) is the definition, and (b) $\Leftrightarrow$ (c) is the proposition above. For (c) $\Leftrightarrow$ (d): if $R$ is a field, the kernel of a unital homomorphism $\varphi : R \to S$ with $S \neq 0$ is an ideal of $R$, hence $0$ or $R$, and it is not $R$ because $\varphi(1) = 1 \neq 0$; conversely, if (c) fails there is an ideal $I$ with $0 \neq I \neq R$, and the quotient map $R \to R/I$ is a unital homomorphism to the nonzero ring $R/I$ that is not injective. $\square$

## Summary

The multiplicative structure of a ring is governed by the partition of its elements into units, zero divisors, and the remaining non-units that are neither. A unit generates the whole ring, $R^\times$ is an abelian group, and associates are the equivalence classes under multiplication by units, the classes on which divisibility is a partial order. An element is a zero divisor when it annihilates a nonzero element; nilpotents are those zero divisors some power of which vanishes, idempotents cut the ring into a product of two factors, and in a finite commutative ring every element is a unit or a zero divisor.

An integral domain is a commutative ring with $1 \neq 0$ and no zero divisors; there cancellation holds, $a \mid b$ and $b \mid a$ force $a \sim b$, polynomial rings over domains remain domains, and every finite domain is a field. The characteristic of a domain is $0$ or prime; a domain of characteristic $0$ contains $\mathbb{Z}$, and a domain of prime characteristic $p$ contains $\mathbb{F}_p$. A field is a commutative ring in which every nonzero element is a unit, equivalently a commutative ring whose only ideals are $0$ and itself; division rings satisfy the same elementwise condition without commutativity, and by Wedderburn's little theorem a finite division ring is a field.

| Class | Defining property | Characteristic | Contains |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commutative ring | commutative, unital | any $n$ or $0$ | — |
| Integral domain | commutative, unital, no zero divisors | $0$ or prime | $\mathbb{Z}$ or $\mathbb{F}_p$ |
| Field | domain with every nonzero element a unit | $0$ or prime | $\mathbb{Q}$ or $\mathbb{F}_p$ |
| Division ring | every nonzero element a unit | $0$ or prime | $\mathbb{Q}$ or $\mathbb{F}_p$ |

The vocabulary fixed here — $R^\times$, associates, zero divisor, nilpotent, idempotent, $\operatorname{char} R$ — is used without restatement in *Factorization: PID, UFD and Euclidean Domains*, where irreducibles are the nonzero non-units that cannot be factored, in *Localization and the Fraction Field*, where a multiplicative set is inverted and the map $R \to S^{-1}R$ is injective exactly when $S$ consists of nonzero-divisors, and in the applications *The Rational Numbers* and *The Real Numbers*, which construct $\mathbb{Q}$ as the fraction field of $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{R}$ as one of its completions.
