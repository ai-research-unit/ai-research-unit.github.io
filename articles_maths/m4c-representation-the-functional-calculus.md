# __$M_4(\mathbb{C})$ Representation : The Functional Calculus__

## Introduction

The exponential, the logarithm and the square roots of a regular matrix are three values of one construction. A holomorphic function defined on a neighbourhood of the spectrum is applied to the matrix by the Cauchy integral, and the result is computed on the spectral data. On the regular image the construction is the image of the biquaternion functional calculus: the regular model doubles the eigenvalues and squares the determinants, but it does not multiply the branches.

This article develops the calculus; the exponential series, its determinant identity, its closed forms, the one-parameter subgroups and the kernel; the logarithm equation, its branch families, the principal branch and its derivative; and the square-root equation, solved in the relative commutant and classified by spectral type. It is the $4\times4$ counterpart of *$M_2(\mathbb{C})$ Representation : The Functional Calculus*.

**Conventions.** The algebra and the model are those of *$M_4(\mathbb{C})$ Model : The Product, the Trace and the Determinant*. The image of $\tilde Q$ is $X=\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde Q)$. The vector part has complex square $B^2=Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2$, the eigenvalues of $X$ are $Q_t\pm iB$, each double, and $\operatorname{tr}X=4Q_t$, $\det X=\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}^2$. In the square-root sections the roots considered lie in the regular image unless the ambient case is named explicitly.

**Boundaries.** The general holomorphic calculus is *Holomorphic Functional Calculus*; the biquaternion closed form and the logarithm are *Biquaternion Elementary Functions*; the Lie algebra and the correspondence are *Biquaternion Lie Group and Exponential Structure*; the algebra classification of square roots is *Square Roots of an Element*; the roots of $\pm1$ and of $0$ are *Idempotents* and *Nilpotents*; the polar decomposition is *$M_4(\mathbb{C})$ Representation : The Polar Decomposition of a Matrix*; the $2\times2$ version is *$M_2(\mathbb{C})$ Representation : The Functional Calculus*.

**Frame.** Unless a basis is named, matrices are written in the coefficient basis $(e_t,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z)$. The canonical frame of *Conventions in Mathematics*, §*The Matrix Models*, is the Peirce frame reached by the change $S$ of *The Product, the Trace and the Determinant*: $S^{-1}\mathsf M_4^{L}(\tilde Q)S=I_2\otimes\mathsf M_2(\tilde Q)$.

## The Functional Calculus

**Definition.** Let $X\in M_4(\mathbb{C})$ and let $f$ be holomorphic on an open set containing the spectrum of $X$. Let $\gamma$ be a finite union of positively oriented closed contours that encloses the spectrum and lies in the domain of $f$. The **functional calculus** is
$$
f(X)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma} f(z)\,(z\mathrm I_4-X)^{-1}\,dz .
$$
The integral does not depend on the contour, by Cauchy's theorem applied to the resolvent, and it defines a matrix.

**The spectral form.** If $X$ is diagonalisable, $X=C\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_4)C^{-1}$, then
$$
f(X)=C\operatorname{diag}\bigl(f(\lambda_1),\dots,f(\lambda_4)\bigr)C^{-1}.
$$
If $X=\lambda\mathrm I_4+N$ with $N$ nilpotent, then
$$
f(X)=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{f^{(k)}(\lambda)}{k!}N^k .
$$
The two formulas are the residue theorem read on the spectral types. They show that $f(X)$ depends only on the values of $f$ on the spectrum, and on the derivatives at a repeated eigenvalue.

**The regular identity.** If $X=\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde Q)$ and $f$ is holomorphic on a neighbourhood of the spectrum of $\tilde Q$, the resolvent identity
$$
(z\mathrm I_4-\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde Q))^{-1}=\mathsf{M}_4\bigl((z e_t-\tilde Q)^{-1}\bigr)
$$
and the contour integral give
$$
f\bigl(\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde Q)\bigr)=\mathsf{M}_4\bigl(f(\tilde Q)\bigr).
$$
Every function of a regular matrix in the image therefore lies in the image, and the calculus on the image is the biquaternion calculus read through $\mathsf{M}_4^{L}$.

**Proposition (the calculus is a homomorphism).** For two functions holomorphic on a common neighbourhood of the spectrum,
$$
(fg)(X)=f(X)g(X),\qquad 1(X)=\mathrm I_4,\qquad f(X)g(X)=g(X)f(X).
$$
The map is therefore an algebra homomorphism from the holomorphic functions to the commutative algebra generated by $X$. It sends a polynomial to the same polynomial in $X$, and a rational function to the same rational function where the denominator is invertible on the spectrum.

**The spectral mapping theorem.** The spectrum of $f(X)$ is the image of the spectrum:
$$
\operatorname{spec}f(X)=f\bigl(\operatorname{spec}X\bigr).
$$
For the exponential this is the statement that the eigenvalues of $\exp X$ are the exponentials of the eigenvalues of $X$; for the logarithm and the roots it is the branch rule.

**The instances.** The calculus contains the three objects of this article.

| Function | Domain | Result |
|---|---|---|
| $f(z)=e^z$ | entire | the exponential $\exp X$ |
| $f(z)=\log z$ | a branch on $\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]$ | a logarithm |
| $f(z)=z^{1/2}$ | a branch of the square root | a square root |
| $f(z)=z^k$, $k\in\mathbb{Z}$ | $0$ excluded when $k<0$ | the powers and the inverse |
| $f(z)=(w-z)^{-1}$ | $w\notin\operatorname{spec}X$ | the resolvent $(w\mathrm I_4-X)^{-1}$ |

The exponential is the entire case, and it is the only one of the three that is defined on the whole matrix algebra. The logarithm and the roots need a branch, and the branch is the source of every multiplicity below.

## The Exponential Series

For $X\in M_4(\mathbb{C})$ the **matrix exponential** is
$$
\exp X=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{X^m}{m!}
=\mathrm I_4+X+\frac{X^2}{2!}+\frac{X^3}{3!}+\cdots
$$
The series converges for every $X$, uniformly on bounded sets, by comparison with $e^{\lVert X\rVert}$. The series is the Cauchy integral of $e^z$ written as a power series at the origin.

**The algebra identity.** Since $\mathsf{M}_4^{L}$ is an algebra homomorphism and the series is an infinite sum of products,
$$
\exp\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde Q)=\mathsf{M}_4\bigl(\exp\tilde Q\bigr),
$$
where the exponential on the right is the biquaternion exponential of *Biquaternion Elementary Functions*. The identity is the reason every formula below is the image of an algebra formula.

**The basic identities.** The exponential sends $0$ to $\mathrm I_4$, and $\exp X$ is invertible with inverse $\exp(-X)$. The derivative along the exponential line is
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\exp(tX)=X\exp(tX)=\exp(tX)X,
$$
so the differential at the origin is the identity and $\exp$ is a local diffeomorphism there.

## The Determinant and the Trace

**Theorem.** For every $X$,
$$
\boxed{\;\det(\exp X)=\exp(\operatorname{tr}X)\;}
$$
In the regular image this reads
$$
\det\exp\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde Q)=\exp(4Q_t)=\bigl(e^{2Q_t}\bigr)^2.
$$

*Proof.* Triangularise $X$, exponentiate the triangular form, and multiply the diagonal entries $e^{\lambda_i}$; the product is $e^{\sum\lambda_i}=e^{\operatorname{tr}X}$. For the image, $\operatorname{tr}\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde Q)=4Q_t$.

**The norm-one consequence.** If $Q_t=0$, then $\det\exp X=1$. Since the exponential is connected and starts at the identity, its image lies in the component $N=1$ of the determinant-one set, so
$$
\exp\bigl(\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{B})\bigr)\subseteq SL_2(\mathbb{C}).
$$
The inclusion is an equality, because every element of $SL_2(\mathbb{C})$ has a logarithm in the trace-free part.

**The norm.** The quaternionic norm of the exponential is
$$
\langle\exp\tilde Q\rangle_{\natural}=e^{2Q_t},
$$
and the determinant of the regular matrix is its square, $e^{4Q_t}$. The norm is nonzero but not necessarily real: it is positive real exactly when $Q_t$ is real.

**The image.** Every exponential is invertible, so the image lies in the unit group; every invertible element of the image has a logarithm in the image, so the image of the exponential is exactly the unit group $\mathbb{B}^{\times}$. The zero matrix is not in the image, and the exponential is neither injective nor a homomorphism.

## The Group Law

**Proposition.** If $XY=YX$, then
$$
\exp(X+Y)=\exp X\,\exp Y.
$$

*Proof.* The binomial theorem applies to the commuting pair, and the two series multiply term by term to the series of $X+Y$.

**Corollary.** $\exp(-X)=(\exp X)^{-1}$, and $\exp(mX)=(\exp X)^m$ for every integer $m$.

**Failure in general.** For non-commuting $X$ and $Y$ the identity fails, and the correction is the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff series
$$
\exp X\,\exp Y=\exp\Bigl(X+Y+\tfrac12[X,Y]+\tfrac1{12}\bigl([X,[X,Y]]-[Y,[X,Y]]\bigr)+\cdots\Bigr).
$$
The exponential is a local isomorphism from the Lie algebra to the group, not a global homomorphism. The defect is the Lie bracket, whose trace form is the subject of *$M_4(\mathbb{C})$ Representation : The Killing Form*.

## The Closed Form of the Exponential

**Theorem.** Let $\tilde Q=Q_te_t+\vec Q$ and let $B$ be a square root of $Q_x^2+Q_y^2+Q_z^2$. Then
$$
\boxed{\;
\exp\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde Q)
=e^{Q_t}\left(\cos B\,\mathrm I_4+\frac{\sin B}{B}\,\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\vec Q)\right)
\;}
$$
with $\sin B/B$ read as $1$ when $B=0$.

*Proof.* The images $\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\vec e_k)$ satisfy $\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\vec e_k)^2=-\mathrm I_4$ and anticommute in pairs, so $\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\vec Q)^2=-B^2\mathrm I_4$. The even and odd powers sum to the two trigonometric series. At $B=0$ the square vanishes and the series truncates to $\mathrm I_4+\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\vec Q)$.

| Regime | Condition | $\exp\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde Q)$ |
|---|---|---|
| Oscillatory | $B\neq0$ | $e^{Q_t}\bigl(\cos B\,\mathrm I_4+\frac{\sin B}{B}\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\vec Q)\bigr)$ |
| Scalar | $B=0$, $\vec Q=0$ | $e^{Q_t}\mathrm I_4$ |
| Nilpotent | $B=0$, $\vec Q\neq0$ | $e^{Q_t}\bigl(\mathrm I_4+\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\vec Q)\bigr)$ |

**The spectral reading.** The eigenvalues of $\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde Q)$ are $Q_t\pm iB$, each double, and those of the exponential are $e^{Q_t\pm iB}$, each double. The formula is the spectral theorem written with the two rank-two eigenprojections. It is also the functional calculus of $e^z$ read on the two distinct eigenvalues.

## One-Parameter Subgroups

A **one-parameter subgroup** of the regular group is a Lie-group homomorphism $\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\mathbb{B}^{\times})$. For $X$ in the image, the **exponential line**
$$
\varphi_X(t)=\exp(tX)
$$
is one, by the group law. Its **infinitesimal generator** is $\varphi_X'(0)=X$.

**Theorem.** Every one-parameter subgroup of the regular group is $\varphi_X$ for a unique $X$ in the image.

*Proof.* Let $\varphi$ be a one-parameter subgroup and put $X=\varphi'(0)$, which lies in the Lie algebra of the image, that is in the image. Differentiating $\varphi(s+t)=\varphi(s)\varphi(t)$ in $s$ at $s=0$ gives $\varphi'(t)=X\varphi(t)$. The product $\psi(t)=\exp(-tX)\varphi(t)$ has derivative zero and $\psi(0)=\mathrm I_4$, so $\varphi(t)=\exp(tX)$. The generator is unique.

**The subgroup depends on the line.** Since $t$ ranges over $\mathbb{R}$, the exponential lines of $X$ and of $\lambda X$ have the same image for every real $\lambda\neq0$. The subgroup is the image of the real line $\mathbb{R}X$; the generator is fixed only once the parametrisation is fixed.

**The four elementary subgroups.**

| Generator | Subgroup | Formula | Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| $i e_t$ | central phase | $e^{it}\mathrm I_4$ | circle |
| $\vec e_k$ | rotor | $\cos t\,\mathrm I_4+\sin t\,\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\vec e_k)$ | compact, in $SO(4)$ |
| $i\vec e_k$ | boost | $\cosh t\,\mathrm I_4+\sinh t\,\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(i\vec e_k)$ | non-compact |
| $\vec P$, $\vec P^2=0$ | unipotent | $\mathrm I_4+t\,\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\vec P)$ | parabolic |

The phase is the centre, the rotor is the compact direction, the boost is the non-compact direction, and the unipotent line is the exponential of a nilpotent. The four are the basic real types; a general exponential line is their product after a conjugation and a complex scaling.

**The centraliser.** Every $\exp(tX)$ commutes with $X$, so the image of $\varphi_X$ lies in the group of units of the centraliser $C(X)$. For a non-scalar $X$ the relative centraliser has dimension two and the full centraliser has dimension eight; the one-parameter subgroup is a real curve in the eight-dimensional commutant.

## The Kernel of the Exponential

**Theorem.** $\exp X=\mathrm I_4$ if and only if $X$ is diagonalisable and every eigenvalue of $X$ lies in $2\pi i\mathbb{Z}$.

*Proof.* If $X$ is diagonalisable with eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_4$, then $\exp X$ is diagonalisable with eigenvalues $e^{\lambda_i}$, and it is the identity exactly when all are $1$. Conversely, put $X$ in Jordan form $\lambda\mathrm I_4+N$ with $N$ nilpotent; then $\exp X=e^{\lambda}(\mathrm I_4+N+\cdots)$, which is the identity only if $N=0$ and $e^{\lambda}=1$.

**In the model.** A matrix of the image lies in the kernel exactly when it is conjugate, in the image, to
$$
\operatorname{diag}(2\pi im,2\pi im,2\pi in,2\pi in),
\qquad m,n\in\mathbb{Z}.
$$
The kernel is the union of these conjugacy classes. In the algebra, for $m\neq n$ each is a complex surface; for $m=n$ it is the single scalar $2\pi im\,\mathrm I_4$. It is not a discrete subgroup.

**In the algebra.** With the eigenvalues $Q_t\pm iB$, the condition is
$$
Q_t=\pi im,\qquad B=\pi n,\qquad m,n\in\mathbb{Z},\quad m\equiv n\pmod 2.
$$
The regular model doubles the eigenvalues but does not change the condition, because $\mathsf{M}_4^{L}$ is injective.

**Example.** The element
$$
\tilde Q=\pi i\,e_t+\pi\,\vec e_z
$$
has $\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde Q)$ with eigenvalues $0,0,2\pi i,2\pi i$, so $\exp\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde Q)=\mathrm I_4$, while $\det\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde Q)=0$: a zero divisor of rank two lies in the kernel. The exponential therefore does not detect invertibility of the exponent.

## The Logarithm Equation

A **logarithm** of $X$ in the image is a matrix $Y$ in the image with
$$
\exp Y=X.
$$

**Theorem.** A matrix of the image has a logarithm in the image if and only if it is invertible.

*Proof.* If $X=\exp Y$ then $\det X=e^{\operatorname{tr}Y}\neq0$. Conversely, if $X=\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde Q)$ is invertible, then $\tilde Q$ is a unit and has a biquaternion logarithm $\tilde Y$; its image is a logarithm of $X$.

**The algebra identity.**
$$
\log\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde Q)=\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\log\tilde Q),
$$
for every branch of the biquaternion logarithm. The identity is the reason the branch structure of the regular model is the branch structure of the algebra.

**The elementary identities.** From the functional equation,
$$
\log(X_1X_2)=\log X_1+\log X_2
$$
holds when $X_1$ and $X_2$ commute and the branches are chosen consistently; it fails in general. The determinant identity gives
$$
\operatorname{tr}\log X=\log\det X\pmod{2\pi i}.
$$
In the regular model $\operatorname{tr}\log X=4\mu$ and $\log\det X=2\log N$, with $\mu=(\log\lambda_++\log\lambda_-)/2$.

## The Branches of the Logarithm

**Theorem.** Let $X=\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde Q)$ be diagonalisable with distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_+\neq\lambda_-$, and let $X=C\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_+,\lambda_+,\lambda_-,\lambda_-)C^{-1}$. Then the logarithms in the image are exactly
$$
Y=C\operatorname{diag}(\mu_+,\mu_+,\mu_-,\mu_-)C^{-1},
\qquad
e^{\mu_+}=\lambda_+,\quad e^{\mu_-}=\lambda_-.
$$

*Proof.* If $\exp Y=X$ and $Y$ is in the image, then $Y$ commutes with $X$, so $Y$ lies in the relative centraliser $C_L(X)$, which is the image of the centraliser of $\tilde Q$. The centraliser is the algebra generated by $\tilde Q$, of dimension two, so $Y$ is a polynomial in $X$ and acts on the two copies with the same eigenvalues. Hence $Y$ is diagonal in the eigenbasis with two eigenvalues $\mu_\pm$, each repeated twice, and exponentiating gives the two equations.

**The branches.** Each equation $e^{\mu}=\lambda$ with $\lambda\neq0$ has the solutions
$$
\mu=\log|\lambda|+i\arg\lambda+2\pi ik,\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}.
$$
The logarithm of a matrix with distinct eigenvalues therefore depends on two integers, one per eigenvalue, and the set of logarithms in the image is a $\mathbb{Z}^2$-family. It is not a $\mathbb{Z}^4$-family: the two copies share the branch.

**The regular form.**
$$
\log X=\bigl(\log\lambda_++2\pi ik_+\bigr)\rho_+
+\bigl(\log\lambda_-+2\pi ik_-\bigr)\rho_-,
$$
with $\rho_\pm$ the rank-two spectral projectors. The formula is the spectral decomposition of the logarithm, and it is the image of the $2\times2$ formula.

**Degenerate eigenvalues.** If $\tilde Q=\lambda e_t+\tilde N$ with $\tilde N^2=0$ and $\tilde N\neq0$, then a logarithm must commute with $\tilde Q$, hence has the form $\tilde Y=a e_t+b\tilde N$, and
$$
\exp\tilde Y=e^a(e_t+b\tilde N)=\lambda e_t+\tilde N
$$
forces $e^a=\lambda$ and $b=1/\lambda$. The logarithm is
$$
\tilde Y=\bigl(\log\lambda+2\pi ik\bigr)e_t+\frac{\tilde N}{\lambda},
$$
a $\mathbb{Z}$-family: the nilpotent part is fixed, and only the scalar shifts. In the regular model the nilpotent is doubled, and the formula is the image.

**The scalar case.** For $\tilde Q=\lambda e_t$, every element commutes with $\tilde Q$, and the logarithms are exactly the diagonalisable elements whose eigenvalues lie in the log-set of $\lambda$. The set is
$$
\log\lambda\,e_t+\ker\exp,
$$
because $\log\lambda\,e_t$ is a logarithm and the kernel is the set of diagonalisable elements with eigenvalues in $2\pi i\mathbb{Z}$. Besides the scalar logs, the family contains the translates by the non-scalar kernel elements. In the regular model the same set is the image, and the kernel is the same.

The three cases are collected in one table.

| Type of $\tilde Q$ | Eigenvalues | Logarithms in the image | Parameters |
|---|---|---|---|
| diagonalisable, distinct eigenvalues | $\lambda_+\neq\lambda_-$ | $C\operatorname{diag}(\mu_+,\mu_+,\mu_-,\mu_-)C^{-1}$, $e^{\mu_\pm}=\lambda_\pm$ | $\mathbb{Z}^2$ |
| non-scalar, repeated eigenvalue | $\lambda$ double, $\tilde N\neq0$ | $(\log\lambda+2\pi ik)e_t+\tilde N/\lambda$ | $\mathbb{Z}$ |
| scalar | $\lambda$ double | diagonalisable $\tilde Y$ with spectrum in the log-set | $\mathbb{Z}^2$ modulo conjugacy |

## The Principal Logarithm

**The principal branch.** Let $\operatorname{Log}$ be the branch of the complex logarithm with imaginary part in $(-\pi,\pi)$, defined on $\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]$. The **principal logarithm** of a matrix in the image is the logarithm whose eigenvalues are the principal logarithms of the eigenvalues of $X$. It is defined when no eigenvalue lies on the closed negative real axis, that is, on the **principal domain**
$$
\Omega=\bigl\{X\in\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\mathbb{B}^{\times}):\ \operatorname{spec}X\cap(-\infty,0]=\varnothing\bigr\}.
$$
In the algebra the condition is the same on $\lambda_\pm$.

**The series.** Near the identity the principal logarithm is
$$
\operatorname{Log}(\mathrm I_4+A)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m+1}}{m}A^m,
$$
convergent for $\lVert A\rVert<1$ in any submultiplicative norm. On the principal domain it is the holomorphic functional calculus of the scalar branch, applied to the image.

**The two compositions.** On the principal domain,
$$
\exp\operatorname{Log}X=X,
$$
and on the strip $\{Y:|\operatorname{Im}\mu|<\pi\text{ for every eigenvalue }\mu\text{ of }Y\}$,
$$
\operatorname{Log}\exp Y=Y.
$$
The two identities are the image of the biquaternion identities.

**The cut is not removable.** For $X=-\mathrm I_4$ the eigenvalues lie on the cut and there is no principal logarithm; the logarithms are the images of the diagonalisable elements whose eigenvalues lie in $(2\mathbb{Z}+1)\pi i$, and none of those eigenvalues has imaginary part in $(-\pi,\pi)$. The principal domain is a genuine restriction and not a convention.

## The Derivative and the Local Inverse

**Proposition.** The principal logarithm is holomorphic on $\Omega$, and its differential at the identity is the identity:
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\operatorname{Log}(\mathrm I_4+tA)\Big|_{t=0}=A.
$$

*Proof.* The series gives $\operatorname{Log}(\mathrm I_4+tA)=tA+O(t^2)$.

**Corollary.** The exponential and the principal logarithm are inverse biholomorphisms between a neighbourhood of $0$ and a neighbourhood of $\mathrm I_4$. The exponential is therefore a local chart of the unit group at the identity, and the pair is the local form of the Lie correspondence of *Biquaternion Lie Group and Exponential Structure*.

**The derivative in general.** Differentiating $\exp Y=X$ along a path gives
$$
\dot X=\int_0^1 e^{sY}\dot Y e^{(1-s)Y}\,ds,
$$
the matrix form of the fact that the derivative of the exponential is the average of the two adjoint actions. The formula is the same as in the $2\times2$ model, read on the two identical blocks.

**The derivative of the logarithm.** Inverting this relation gives, for $X$ with distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_\pm$ and rank-two eigenprojections $\rho_\pm$,
$$
d\operatorname{Log}_X(H)
=\frac{\rho_+H\rho_+}{\lambda_+}+\frac{\rho_-H\rho_-}{\lambda_-}
+\frac{\log\lambda_+-\log\lambda_-}{\lambda_+-\lambda_-}
\bigl(\rho_+H\rho_-+\rho_-H\rho_+\bigr).
$$
The formula is the $2\times2$ formula with the rank-one projectors replaced by the rank-two ones, because each eigenvalue is repeated. When $[H,X]=0$ the cross terms vanish and $d\operatorname{Log}_X(H)=X^{-1}H$; in general the derivative is not a multiplication by $X^{-1}$ on either side.

## From the Logarithm to the Square Root

**The determinant and the branches.** The determinant of the exponential of a logarithm is
$$
\det X=e^{\operatorname{tr}\log X}=e^{4\mu}=N^2,
$$
the square of the norm. The branch shift by $2\pi i(k_+,k_-)$ changes $\operatorname{tr}\log X$ by $4\pi i(k_++k_-)$, which is invisible in the determinant. The exponential is surjective onto the unit group; the logarithm is a right inverse on the principal domain and a multivalued inverse everywhere, and the differences between the branches are the kernel translates.

**Half a logarithm.** If $Y$ is a logarithm of $X$, then $S=\exp(Y/2)$ satisfies $S^2=X$, and $S$ commutes with $X$ because $Y$ does. Conversely, if $S$ is a square root of an invertible $X$, then $S$ is invertible, and any logarithm $T$ of $S$ gives the logarithm $2T$ of $X$. Hence every square root of an invertible matrix in the image is the exponential of half a logarithm. The two signs are the shift
$$
\exp\!\left(\frac{Y+2\pi i\,\mathrm I_4}{2}\right)=-S.
$$

**The principal choice.** If the spectrum of $X$ avoids the negative real axis, the principal logarithm gives the principal square root
$$
X^{1/2}=\exp\!\left(\tfrac12\operatorname{Log}X\right),
$$
the one whose eigenvalues lie in the right half-plane. In the distinct case the two distinct eigenvalues give the four sign choices, and the principal square root is the one with the principal signs. The next sections solve the equation in the image, give the closed form on each spectral type and collect the classification.

## The Square-Root Equation

**Proposition.** If $Y^2=X$ and $Y$ lies in the image, then $Y$ commutes with $X$.

*Proof.* $YX=Y^3=XY$.

**Consequence.** Every root in the image lies in the relative commutant $C_L(X)$, which is the image of the centraliser of $\tilde Q$. The root is therefore $\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde R)$ with $\tilde R^2=\tilde Q$, and the problem reduces to the biquaternion equation.

**The ambient roots.** A $4\times4$ matrix $X$ can have roots outside the image. For example, a scalar $X=\lambda\mathrm I_4$ has roots that act with different signs on the two copies, and those roots are not of the form $\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde R)$. The classification below is the classification in the image; the ambient classification is the classification of the $4\times4$ Jordan types and is larger.

## The Spectral Cases

### Distinct Eigenvalues

**Theorem.** Let $X=C\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_+,\lambda_+,\lambda_-,\lambda_-)C^{-1}$ with $\lambda_+\neq\lambda_-$. The roots in the image are exactly
$$
Y=C\operatorname{diag}(\mu_+,\mu_+,\mu_-,\mu_-)C^{-1},
\qquad
\mu_+^2=\lambda_+,\quad\mu_-^2=\lambda_-.
$$
There are four roots when $\lambda_+\lambda_-\neq0$, and two roots when one eigenvalue is zero.

*Proof.* By the reduction $Y$ lies in the relative commutant, which is the algebra generated by $X$; it is diagonal in the eigenbasis and acts with the same eigenvalue on the two copies. Squaring is entrywise. A nonzero complex number has two square roots and $0$ has one.

**The count.** The generic regular matrix has four roots in the image, the four sign choices of the two distinct eigenvalues. The multiplicity of each eigenvalue does not multiply the count, because the root must act identically on the two copies. When one eigenvalue vanishes, the corresponding sign choice collapses and two roots remain.

### Repeated Eigenvalue

**Theorem.** Let $\tilde Q=\lambda e_t+\tilde N$ with $\tilde N\neq0$ and $\tilde N^2=0$.

If $\lambda\neq0$, the roots in the image are the two matrices
$$
Y=\pm\left(\sqrt{\lambda}\,\mathrm I_4+\frac{\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde N)}{2\sqrt{\lambda}}\right).
$$
If $\lambda=0$, there is no root in the image.

*Proof.* By the reduction $Y=a\mathrm I_4+b\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde N)$, and
$$
Y^2=a^2\mathrm I_4+2ab\,\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde N).
$$
For $\lambda\neq0$ the conditions are $a^2=\lambda$ and $2ab=1$, which give the two roots. For $\lambda=0$ they are $a^2=0$ and $2ab=1$, which are incompatible.

**The nilpotent case.** A nonzero nilpotent regular matrix has no root in the image. The ambient matrix can have a root, because a $4\times4$ nilpotent of rank two can be the square of a nilpotent of rank three; that root is not regular.

### Scalar Matrices

**Theorem.** Let $\tilde Q=\lambda e_t$.

If $\lambda\neq0$, the roots in the image are the two scalars $\pm\sqrt{\lambda}\,\mathrm I_4$ together with the image of the complex surface
$$
\mathcal S_\lambda=\{\tilde Y:\operatorname{tr}\tilde Y=0,\ \tilde Y^2=\lambda e_t\},
$$
which has complex dimension two.

If $\lambda=0$, the roots in the image are the image of the nilpotent cone
$$
\mathcal N=\{\tilde Y:\tilde Y^2=0\},
$$
of complex dimension two.

*Proof.* For $\lambda\neq0$ a root satisfies the polynomial $t^2-\lambda$ with distinct roots, hence is diagonalisable with eigenvalues $\pm\sqrt{\lambda}$. The scalar roots are the two constant choices, and the mixed choices form the conjugacy class of $\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda},-\sqrt{\lambda})$ in the algebra. For $\lambda=0$ the equation is $\tilde Y^2=0$, which is the nilpotent cone.

**The dimension.** The image surfaces have complex dimension two, hence four real parameters, matching the algebra. The ambient root set is larger: a $4\times4$ matrix with $Y^2=\lambda\mathrm I_4$ is diagonalisable with eigenvalues $\pm\sqrt{\lambda}$, and the largest conjugacy class, with two eigenvalues of each sign, has complex dimension eight. The image is the two-dimensional subfamily that acts identically on the two copies.

**The involution.** The root sets are stable under $Y\mapsto-Y$, the sign symmetry of the square-root equation.

## The Principal Square Root in Closed Form

**The formula.** For a regular matrix $X$ whose eigenvalues avoid the negative real axis and are not both zero,
$$
\boxed{\;
X^{1/2}=\frac{X+\sqrt N\,\mathrm I_4}
{\sqrt{\tfrac12\operatorname{Tr}X+2\sqrt N}}
\;}
$$
where $\sqrt N$ is the square root of the norm fixed by $\sqrt N=\sqrt{\lambda_+}\sqrt{\lambda_-}$, the product of the chosen eigenvalue roots.

*Proof.* The eigenvalues of $X$ are $\lambda_+,\lambda_+,\lambda_-,\lambda_-$, so the minimal polynomial has degree at most two. The root is $\alpha X+\beta\mathrm I_4$ with
$$
\alpha=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_+}+\sqrt{\lambda_-}},
\qquad
\beta=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_+}\sqrt{\lambda_-}}{\sqrt{\lambda_+}+\sqrt{\lambda_-}}
=\frac{\sqrt N}{\sqrt{\lambda_+}+\sqrt{\lambda_-}}.
$$
Since $\operatorname{Tr}X=2(\lambda_++\lambda_-)$, the denominator is $\sqrt{\tfrac12\operatorname{Tr}X+2\sqrt N}$.

**The check.** On the $\lambda_+$-eigenspace the numerator is $\lambda_++\sqrt N=\lambda_++\sqrt{\lambda_+}\sqrt{\lambda_-}=\sqrt{\lambda_+}(\sqrt{\lambda_+}+\sqrt{\lambda_-})$, and the quotient is $\sqrt{\lambda_+}$. On the $\lambda_-$-eigenspace it is $\sqrt{\lambda_-}$.

**The $2\times2$ comparison.** In the $2\times2$ model the eigenvalues are $\lambda_+,\lambda_-$, $\operatorname{Tr}X=\lambda_++\lambda_-$, and $\sqrt N=\sqrt{\det X}$. The denominator is $\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}X+2\sqrt N}$, and the formula is the $2\times2$ one. In the regular model the trace is doubled, and the half-trace appears.

**The special cases.** For a scalar $X=\lambda\mathrm I_4$ the formula, with the consistent choice $\sqrt N=\lambda$, gives $\sqrt{\lambda}\,\mathrm I_4$. For a Jordan block $X=\lambda\mathrm I_4+\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde N)$ with $\lambda\neq0$ it gives $\sqrt{\lambda}\,\mathrm I_4+\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde N)/(2\sqrt{\lambda})$, the root of the repeated-eigenvalue theorem. For a singular matrix with one nonzero eigenvalue it gives the root with eigenvalues $\sqrt{\lambda_+},0$, each repeated.

## The Classification

| Condition on $\tilde Q$ | Eigenvalues of $X$ | Roots in the image |
|---|---|---|
| distinct eigenvalues, both nonzero | $\lambda_+\neq\lambda_-$, $\lambda_+\lambda_-\neq0$ | four |
| distinct eigenvalues, one zero | $\lambda_+\neq\lambda_-$, $\lambda_+\lambda_-=0$ | two |
| non-scalar, repeated eigenvalue $\lambda\neq0$ | $\lambda$ double, $\tilde N\neq0$ | two |
| nonzero nilpotent, repeated eigenvalue $0$ | $0$ double, $\tilde N\neq0$ | none |
| scalar $\lambda e_t$, $\lambda\neq0$ | $\lambda$ double | two points and a surface |
| zero | $0$ double | the nilpotent cone |

**The dictionary with the algebra.** The rows are exactly the rows of the algebra classification. The matrix eigenvalues are doubled, but the count of roots in the image is not: the root must act identically on the two copies, and the two copies share the sign choice.

## Worked Examples

**A regular element.** Take $\tilde Q=2e_t+\vec e_x$. Then $Q_t=2$, $B=1$, $\lambda_\pm=2\pm i$, $N=5$. The matrix $X$ has eigenvalues $2+i,2+i,2-i,2-i$, and the four roots in the image have eigenvalues $\pm\sqrt{2+i}$ and $\pm\sqrt{2-i}$, each repeated. The principal root is the one with the principal signs; the closed form gives it directly.

**A singular element.** Take $\tilde Q=e_t+i\vec e_x$. Then $Q_t=1$, $B^2=-1$, $\lambda_+=0$, $\lambda_-=2$, $N=0$. The eigenvalues are distinct and one is zero, so there are two roots in the image; the principal root is $X/\sqrt2$, with eigenvalues $0$ and $\sqrt2$, each repeated. The determinant of the root is $0$, so the root is singular, as it must be.

**A nilpotent.** Take $\tilde Q=\vec e_x+i\vec e_y$. Then $Q_t=0$ and $B^2=0$, so $\lambda_\pm=0$ and $\tilde Q^2=0$. There is no root in the image. The ambient $4\times4$ nilpotent $\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde Q)$ has rank two and does have a root in $M_4(\mathbb{C})$, but that root is not regular.

## Summary

The functional calculus applies a holomorphic function to a matrix by the Cauchy integral. On the regular image it is the image of the biquaternion calculus, $\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(f(\tilde Q))=f(\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde Q))$; the eigenvalues are doubled, and the branches are not.

The exponential is the entire case, and it is the image of the biquaternion exponential, $\exp\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\tilde Q)=\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\exp\tilde Q)$. Its determinant is $\exp(4Q_t)$, and its norm is $e^{2Q_t}$. The closed form is $e^{Q_t}(\cos B\,\mathrm I_4+(\sin B/B)\mathsf{M}_4^{L}(\vec Q))$, with the nilpotent limit at $B=0$. The one-parameter subgroups are the exponential lines, with the four elementary types phase, rotor, boost and unipotent. The kernel consists of the diagonalisable matrices with eigenvalues in $2\pi i\mathbb{Z}$, a union of conjugacy classes that contains zero divisors.

The logarithms of a regular matrix in the image are the images of the biquaternion logarithms. For distinct eigenvalues they form a $\mathbb{Z}^2$-family, one integer per eigenvalue repeated twice; for a non-scalar repeated eigenvalue they form a $\mathbb{Z}$-family; for a scalar they are the translates of a scalar logarithm by the kernel. The principal logarithm is defined when the spectrum avoids the closed negative real axis.

The square roots in the image are the images of the biquaternion roots: four for distinct nonzero eigenvalues, two when one eigenvalue vanishes, two for a non-scalar repeated nonzero eigenvalue, none for a nonzero nilpotent, and a surface or the nilpotent cone for a scalar. The principal root is $(X+\sqrt N\mathrm I_4)/\sqrt{\tfrac12\operatorname{Tr}X+2\sqrt N}$. The ambient $4\times4$ matrix has more logarithms and more roots, but they do not lie in the image.
