Topological Clifford Algebras

Introduction

Part I built the quadratic space, its form and the Clifford algebra over a field, and it used no distance anywhere: the form is a pairing with values in the base, its quadratic form is the same datum read on the diagonal, and the Clifford algebra is a quotient of the tensor algebra. This article opens the layer in which the same objects carry a topology and the operations are required to be continuous. It is the entry of the category Topology on Algebras with a degree-2 form, and it stands to Topological Algebras and Banach Algebras as Clifford Algebras stands to Algebras: the base is a topological field, the space is a topological module, the form is continuous, and the product of the algebra is continuous.

The one new ingredient with respect to the bilinear layer is the form. A topological algebra is a topological module with a continuous product, and its structure is read off the product alone; here the algebra is generated by a space $V$ whose quadratic form is a second continuous datum, and the whole algebra is determined by the pair $(V,q)$ up to isometry. Two facts follow and organise the article. The first is that the continuity of $q$ is exactly what is needed for the defining relation $v^{2}=q(v)\cdot 1$ to survive the passage to the quotient: the ideal of the tensor algebra is generated by continuous elements, the quotient topology exists, and the relation is an identity of continuous maps. The second is that in finite dimension over a complete valued field this is automatic — a finite-dimensional Hausdorff vector space carries a unique topology, so every form is continuous and the product is jointly continuous — while in infinite dimension both the continuity of the form and the continuity of the product are genuine hypotheses, and the interesting objects are the ones where they are imposed and not deduced.

The article defines the layer and its standing hypotheses, reads the basic structure as continuous — the product, the two anti-involutions of the algebra, the grade involution and the two canonical filtrations — treats the closure of zero and the Hausdorff quotient, states the topological collapse at the degenerate form, and closes with the examples. It owns no form of the base algebra beyond the quadratic form: the alternating and the symplectic forms belong to The Continuous Symplectic Form and the Poisson Bracket, the quadratic form and its polar form in the general quadratic space to The Continuous Quadratic Form and the Polar Form, the grading and the filtration to The Continuous Grade Decomposition and the Filtration, the duality of the top grade to The Continuous Volume Element, Duality and the Hodge Star, the completion to The Completion of a Clifford Algebra, the classical groups to The Topological Orthogonal Group and the Spin Group, the classification to The Topological Witt Group and the Brauer–Wall Group, and the operators to the - Operator Theory group of this category. Throughout, $F$ is a topological field, $V$ a Hausdorff topological $F$-module in which the scalars act continuously, $q : V \to F$ a continuous quadratic form, $B$ its polar form, and $\mathrm{Cl}(V,q)$ the Clifford algebra of Clifford Algebras, §Construction and Universal Property.

The Layer

The Standing Hypotheses

Definition. Let $F$ be a topological field, let $V$ be a topological $F$-module, and let $q : V \to F$ be a continuous quadratic form with polar form $B$ in the sense of Quadratic Forms and Polarisation, §Quadratic Forms. The topological Clifford algebra of $(V,q)$ is the Clifford algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(V,q)$ carrying the finest topology for which the canonical projection $\pi : T(V) \to \mathrm{Cl}(V,q)$ is continuous when $T(V)$ carries the tensor topology built from that of $V$, together with the requirement that the product of the algebra be continuous in each variable separately.

The definition is that of Clifford Algebras, §Construction and Universal Property, read with three topological hypotheses: the base is a topological field, $V$ is a topological module whose scalar action is continuous, and $q$ is continuous. The algebra is the quotient of the tensor algebra by the two-sided ideal generated by the elements $v\otimes v - q(v)\cdot 1$, and the quotient topology is the one displayed. Nothing else is assumed. The form need not be non-degenerate, the product need not be jointly continuous, and $V$ need not be complete or finite-dimensional.

Remark (the tensor topology). The tensor algebra $T(V)=\bigoplus_{k\ge0}V^{\otimes k}$ carries the coproduct topology of the tensor powers, and each tensor power carries the finest topology for which the tensor map $V^{\times k}\to V^{\otimes k}$ is continuous; this is the topological tensor product of Topological Tensor Products in its simplest form, and it is what makes $\pi$ a continuous map. The quotient topology is then the finest topology on $\mathrm{Cl}(V,q)$ making $\pi$ continuous, and the product of the quotient is continuous in each variable separately because the product of $T(V)$ is and the quotient map is open and multiplicative.

Remark (continuity of the form is the hypothesis of the relation). The defining relation of the Clifford algebra is $v^{2}=q(v)\cdot 1$ for $v\in V$, and it is a statement about three continuous maps: the inclusion $V\hookrightarrow \mathrm{Cl}(V,q)$, the squaring map on the algebra, and the scalar multiplication by the continuous function $q$. The relation holds in the algebra because it holds in the tensor algebra and the ideal is generated by the differences; the continuity of the form is what makes the two sides continuous functions of $v$, so that the relation is an identity of continuous maps and the quadratic structure is visible in the topology and not only in the algebra. When $q$ is not continuous, the algebra still exists, but the relation is not a statement about the topology and none of the continuity statements of the next section survive.

The Conventions of the Category

The corpus writes the Clifford algebra of a quadratic form $q$ on a finite-dimensional space $V$ over a field $F$ of characteristic not $2$ as $\mathrm{Cl}(V,q)$, with the convention $v^{2}=q(v)\cdot 1$ and with $B$ the polar form of $q$, as in Clifford Algebras, §The Conventions of the Category. The intrinsic anti-involutions are the grade involution $\alpha$, the algebra automorphism with $\alpha(v)=-v$; reversion $x\mapsto x^{r}$, the anti-automorphism reversing every product; and the Clifford conjugation $x^{\natural}=\alpha(x^{r})$, the composite. Their signs on a $k$-blade are $(-1)^{k}$ for $\alpha$, $(-1)^{k(k-1)/2}$ for the reversion and $(-1)^{k(k+1)/2}$ for the conjugation, and they are computed in Clifford Algebras in Finite Dimensions. The present article adds the topology and does not change a symbol.

Remark (the degenerate case is not excluded). The definition admits a degenerate $q$. The radical $\mathrm{rad}(q)=\{v : B(v,w)=0\ \text{for every } w\}$ of Degenerate Clifford Algebras and the Radical is then a nonzero subspace, the algebra contains nilpotent elements, and the topological statements below are unaffected. The radical is closed when $q$ is continuous and $F$ is Hausdorff: the polar form is continuous, being the composite $B(x,y)=\tfrac12\bigl(q(x+y)-q(x)-q(y)\bigr)$ of the continuous form $q$ with the continuous operations of the module, so for each $w$ the map $\varphi_{w}(v)=B(v,w)$ is continuous, and the radical is the intersection of the closed kernels $\ker\varphi_{w}$. Everything that uses non-degeneracy says so when it does.

The Continuity of the Structure

The Product and the Two Anti-Involutions

Proposition. Let $\mathrm{Cl}(V,q)$ be a topological Clifford algebra. Then the product is separately continuous, and both the grade involution $\alpha$ and the reversion $x\mapsto x^{r}$ are continuous.

Proof. The product is separately continuous by the definition of the layer. For the grade involution, $\alpha$ is the algebra automorphism induced by $-1_V : V\to V$ under the universal property of Clifford Algebras, §The Parity Grading; the map $-1_V$ is continuous, being the composite of the continuous negation of the module with the continuous action of $-1\in F$, so by the universal property of the quotient it induces a continuous map $\mathrm{Cl}(V,q)\to\mathrm{Cl}(V,q)$, additive and multiplicative, which is $\alpha$. Reversion is the unique anti-automorphism with $x^{r}=x$ on $V$, and it is the composite of the continuous reversal of the tensor words on $T(V)$ with the passage to the quotient, whence continuous. $\square$

Remark. The three anti-involutions are homeomorphisms: each is an involution of the algebra and is continuous, and its inverse is itself. The Clifford conjugation $\natural=\alpha\circ{}^{r}$ is therefore continuous as a composite, and the map $N(x)=x\,x^{\natural}$ of Quadratic Forms over Algebras and Norms is continuous as a composite of continuous maps. This is the topological form of the statement that the intrinsic structure of the algebra is compatible with its topology, and it is used wherever a completion or a norm is introduced later in the category.

The Grade Involution and the Topological Grading

Proposition. The algebra is the topological direct sum of the images of the even and the odd parts of the tensor algebra, and the grade involution is a topological involution with those images as its $\pm1$-eigenspaces.

Proof. Algebraically, $\mathrm{Cl}(V,q)=\mathrm{Cl}^{0}\oplus\mathrm{Cl}^{1}$ by Clifford Algebras, §The Parity Grading, and the sum is direct because $\alpha$ is an automorphism of order two and the two parts are its eigenspaces; the projections $\tfrac12(1\pm\alpha)$ are continuous, since $\alpha$ is, and the field has characteristic not $2$, so a continuous linear map splitting the identity exhibits the sum as topological as well. $\square$

The proposition is the first instance of the general principle of The Continuous Grade Decomposition and the Filtration: every algebraic decomposition built from a finite group of continuous operators is a topological decomposition.

The Filtration and the Two canonical Topologies

Definition. The length filtration of the algebra is the increasing chain $F_{k}$ of images of $\bigoplus_{j\le k}V^{\otimes j}$, as in The Filtration and the Associated Graded Algebra, §The Length Filtration. Its topology is the coproduct topology, and the filtration topology of the algebra is the finest linear topology for which the inclusions $F_{k}\hookrightarrow \mathrm{Cl}(V,q)$ are continuous.

Proposition. The filtration is an increasing chain of closed subspaces when $F$ is Hausdorff and each $F_{k}$ is closed in $F_{k+1}$, and $F_{j}F_{l}\subseteq F_{j+l}$; consequently the filtration topology is a linear topology for which the product is continuous, and the two topologies coincide in finite dimension.

Proof. The multiplicative property is the algebraic one of The Filtration and the Associated Graded Algebra, §The Length Filtration, and holds for every form, degenerate included. The filtration topology is linear by construction, and its product is continuous because $F_{j}\times F_{l}\to F_{j+l}$ is continuous for each pair, the algebraic product being a continuous map out of the tensor product by the definition of the tensor topology. In finite dimension over a complete valued field $F$ the algebra is a finite-dimensional Hausdorff vector space, which carries a unique topology, so the two linear topologies agree. $\square$

Remark (why the distinction matters). In infinite dimension the two topologies differ in general, and the filtration topology is the one that sees the algebra as a union of finite-rank pieces; the completion of the article The Completion of a Clifford Algebra is taken for it, and the difference from the quotient topology is the reason the completion is introduced there and not deduced from the algebraic structure.

The Closure of Zero and the Hausdorff Quotient

The Ideal of the Closure

Proposition. Let $\mathrm{Cl}(V,q)$ be a topological Clifford algebra and let $N=\overline{\{0\}}$. Then $N$ is a closed two-sided ideal of the algebra, and the algebra is Hausdorff if and only if $N=\{0\}$.

Proof. The additive group of the algebra is a topological group, so Topological Groups, §Separation identifies $\overline{\{0\}}$ as the intersection of the neighbourhoods of $0$ and proves it is a subgroup, trivial exactly when the group is Hausdorff. For the ideal property, the multiplications $L_{a}$ and $R_{a}$ are separately continuous by the layer and carry $0$ to $0$, so $aN\subseteq N$ and $Na\subseteq N$; and the scalar maps are continuous for the same reason, so $N$ is a submodule. $\square$

The Quotient

Theorem. The quotient $\mathrm{Cl}(V,q)/N$ with the quotient topology is a Hausdorff topological Clifford algebra for the same space, its product being the descended map, and the quotient map is continuous, open, additive and multiplicative.

Proof. The quotient is a topological $F$-module and is Hausdorff because $N$ is closed in a topological group. The product descends because $N$ is a two-sided ideal: if $x'=x+n$ and $y'=y+m$ with $n,m\in N$, then $x'y'=xy+xm+ny+nm$ and the three correction terms lie in $N$, since $N$ is a submodule and a two-sided ideal. It is separately continuous by the universal property of the quotient topology, and the relation $v^{2}=q(v)\cdot1$ descends because it holds on representatives, the scalar $q(v)$ being untouched by the quotient. $\square$

The quotient is the largest Hausdorff quotient of the algebra and it is again a Clifford algebra, of the same pair $(V,q)$; the form is what the quotient preserves, and the ideal it kills is not visible in $(V,q)$ because the closure of zero is smaller than the radical in general. The two are compared in The Continuous Quadratic Form and the Polar Form, §The Radical and the Closure of Zero.

The Collapse and the Boundary

The Collapse at the Trivial Form

Theorem (the collapse). Let $q=0$. Then $\mathrm{Cl}(V,0)=\Lambda(V)$ with the wedge product as multiplication, the product is the alternating one of The Exterior Algebra, and the topological Clifford algebra of $(V,0)$ is the topological exterior algebra of $V$: the object is fixed by the grading, $\alpha=\mathrm{id}$, and the two parts of the decomposition coincide with the image of the even respectively the odd exterior powers.

Proof. For $q=0$ the defining relation is $v^{2}=0$, that is $v\wedge v=0$, and the universal property of $\mathrm{Cl}(V,0)$ identifies it with the exterior algebra with the wedge product, by The Filtration and the Associated Graded Algebra, §The Associated Graded Algebra. The grade involution negates the vectors and is the identity on the even part and the sign on the odd part; the product is alternating, and the topological statements are the layer's. $\square$

Remark. The collapse is the topological form of the statement that the exterior algebra is the Clifford algebra of the zero form, and it is the boundary case of the whole category: the form contributes nothing, the algebra is graded rather than filtered, and the structure is that of Anti-symmetric Algebras read topologically. It is the reason the articles of this category state non-degeneracy when they need it and not before.

The Relation to the Neighbouring Categories

Remark (the boundary with the bilinear layer). The category Topology on Algebras owns the topological algebra read with its product alone; this category owns the same object together with a continuous quadratic form, which is what generates the Clifford structure. A topological Clifford algebra is a topological algebra, so every statement of Topological Algebras and Banach Algebras applies to it, and what is added here is the form, the relation $v^{2}=q(v)$ and the anti-involutions it defines. In finite dimension over a complete valued field the object is a finite-dimensional topological algebra and the layer adds nothing to the algebraic content; in infinite dimension the form is a genuine extra datum and the Clifford algebra is not a topological algebra in general, its product being only separately continuous.

Remark (the boundary with the Hermitian layer). When the base carries an involution and the form is sesquilinear rather than bilinear, the object belongs to Topology on Sesqualgebras with a degree-2 form, whose entry is Topological Sesqualgebras with a Form. The present category is the bilinear one, the collapse $\varsigma=\mathrm{id}$ of that layer, and the two are kept apart throughout the corpus: the bilinear form has no conjugate slot, and the Hermitian adjoint ${}^{\dagger}$ of the Hermitian layer is the transpose read with the bar, which is not available here. The dagger appears in the operator articles of this category only in the sense of Hermitian Algebras, named and not developed.

Examples

The Finite-Dimensional Real Algebras

Example. Let $F=\mathbb{R}$ with its usual topology, let $V=\mathbb{R}^{n}$ with the Euclidean topology, and let $q$ be any quadratic form, represented by a symmetric matrix. Then $q$ is a polynomial in the coordinates, hence continuous; $\mathrm{Cl}(V,q)$ is finite-dimensional over $\mathbb{R}$, hence a Hausdorff topological vector space for a unique topology; and its product, being bilinear on a finite-dimensional space, is jointly continuous, hence separately continuous. The objects $\mathrm{Cl}_{p,q}$ of Clifford Algebras in Finite Dimensions, with the Euclidean topology, are therefore topological Clifford algebras, and the classification article Bott Periodicity and the Classification is a classification of objects of the layer, with the topology carried along.

Example (the complex case). Let $F=\mathbb{C}$ with the topology of $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ and let $q$ be a complex quadratic form on $\mathbb{C}^{n}$, represented by a symmetric complex matrix. The form is a polynomial in the coordinates, hence continuous; the algebra is a finite-dimensional complex vector space, so its topology is unique and its product is jointly continuous. The same argument covers $F=\mathbb{R}$ and every complete valued field of characteristic not $2$.

The Degenerate Real Cases

Example (the dual numbers). Let $V=\mathbb{R}$ with the form $q(x)=0$, so that $\mathrm{Cl}(V,q)=\mathbb{R}[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^{2})$ with $\varepsilon$ the class of the basis vector, the dual numbers of the category Dual Numbers. The product is jointly continuous, the radical is all of $V$, the algebra is the topological exterior algebra of the line, and the collapse theorem applies. It is the smallest object of the layer that is not semisimple, and it shows that the non-degeneracy of the form is not needed for the layer to exist.

Example (the split cases). Let $V=\mathbb{R}^{2}$ with $q(x,y)=x^{2}-y^{2}$. Then $\mathrm{Cl}(V,q)\cong M_{2}(\mathbb{R})$ by The Low-Dimensional Classification, the form is continuous, and the algebra with the Euclidean topology is a topological Clifford algebra. The zero divisors of the algebra are the images of the isotropic vectors of the form; the isotropic set is the pair of lines $x=\pm y$, closed in $V$, and the collapse of the algebra that they cause is a statement about the form, not about the topology: the closure of zero of the algebra is trivial, since the algebra is Hausdorff.

A Non-Discrete and an Indiscrete Topology

Example (an infinite-dimensional object). Let $F=\mathbb{R}$ and let $V$ be a Hilbert space with its norm topology and $q(v)=\lVert v\rVert^{2}$, the quadratic form of the inner product. Then $q$ is continuous, and the Clifford algebra carries the quotient topology of the tensor algebra; this is the object of Infinite-Dimensional Clifford Algebras and CAR with Inner Conjugation, whose canonical anticommutation relations are the relation $v^{2}=q(v)$ read on the annihilation and creation operators of the completion. The article there owns the CAR algebra and the representations; the present one owns the definition of the layer and the continuity statements, and the completion of the algebra is in The Completion of a Clifford Algebra.

Example (the discrete and the indiscrete topologies). Let $F$ and $V$ carry the discrete topology. Then every map into $F$ is continuous, in particular $q$, and the tensor and quotient topologies are discrete, so every algebraic Clifford algebra of Part I is an object of the layer and the continuity hypotheses are vacuous. If instead $\mathrm{Cl}(V,q)$ carries the indiscrete topology, every map into it is continuous and the product is continuous, with $\overline{\{0\}}=\mathrm{Cl}(V,q)$; the quotient of the previous section is then the zero object. The two examples are the extremes of the layer, and they show that the continuity of the form is a restriction only when a topology that is not discrete is imposed.

Summary

A topological Clifford algebra is the Clifford algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(V,q)$ of a continuous quadratic form $q$ on a topological module $V$ over a topological field $F$, carrying the quotient topology of the tensor algebra and a product that is separately continuous. The layer is the bilinear layer of Topological Algebras and Banach Algebras with a continuous quadratic form as an extra datum, and the continuity of the form is exactly what makes the defining relation $v^{2}=q(v)\cdot 1$ an identity of continuous maps.

The intrinsic structure is continuous: the grade involution $\alpha$ is the automorphism induced by the continuous map $-1_{V}$, the reversion is the continuous anti-automorphism fixing $V$, the Clifford conjugation is their composite, and the algebra splits as a topological direct sum of its even and odd parts because the projections $\tfrac12(1\pm\alpha)$ are continuous. The length filtration is an increasing chain of subspaces with $F_{j}F_{l}\subseteq F_{j+l}$; the filtration topology and the quotient topology coincide in finite dimension over a complete valued field and differ in general, and the completion is taken for the filtration topology. The closure of zero is a closed two-sided ideal, the algebra is Hausdorff exactly when it is zero, and the quotient by it is a Hausdorff topological Clifford algebra of the same pair $(V,q)$.

At $q=0$ the algebra is the exterior algebra, graded rather than filtered, and the layer collapses onto the topological anti-symmetric algebras; the finite-dimensional real, complex and quaternionic cases are the objects for which the topology is unique and adds no hypothesis; the dual numbers are the smallest degenerate object; the isotropic lines of the split case show that isotropy belongs to the form and hausdorffness to the topology; the Hilbert-space case is the infinite-dimensional object whose completion carries the canonical anticommutation relations. Non-degeneracy is assumed only where it is used, and the Hermitian layer with its sesquilinear form and its dagger is a different category, adjoining through Topology on Sesqualgebras with a degree-2 form.
