Norm and Invertibility in the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra

Introduction

A norm and an inverse are read in a product, and the equations name it. The block of this article is the antisymmetric plain multiplication $$ \tilde P\wedge\tilde Q=\tfrac12\bigl(\tilde P\tilde Q-\tilde Q\tilde P\bigr)=\mathbf{P}\times\mathbf{Q}, $$ the cross product of the two vector parts, which is the antisymmetric half of the general plain bilinear product; it is $\mathbb{C}$-bilinear, alternating, vector valued and without a unit (Introduction to the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra of Biquaternions). The classical theory of the norm and the inverse belongs to a unital associative product, and the block has neither a unit nor an associativity; the present article states what that costs and what survives.

The answer is an absence and a substitute. The block has no unit on either side, hence no inverse and an empty group of units; it has no norm, its square and its conjugated square both vanishing identically. The quadratic objects it does carry are the operator trace form $K(\tilde A,\tilde B)=\operatorname{Tr}(L_{\tilde A}L_{\tilde B})$ and the scalar-part form $\varphi(\tilde P,\tilde Q)=P_0Q_0$, and neither is multiplicative. The substitute the block uses, and the form on which its whole Lie theory rests, is the operator trace form $K$, whose identification with the invariant form of the block, its radical and its rank are owned by The Killing Form of the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra and are cited here, not reproved. The article closes with the comparison against the absent norm of the parent product and against the invertibility criterion of the general plain algebra.

This article is algebraic throughout, and the word norm names the algebraic norm, an element of the algebra and not a value in the reals. The general definitions of the unit and the inverse are Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures; the product and its laws are Introduction to the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra of Biquaternions; the norm of the general plain product, to which the block is compared, is Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Algebra.

The Notion and Its Product

Definition (unit of a product). Let $\star$ be a product on $\mathbb{B}$. An element $e$ is a left unit of $\star$ when $e\star x=x$ for every $x$; a right unit of $\star$ when $x\star e=x$ for every $x$; and a two-sided unit of $\star$ when it is both (Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures, §The Unit and the Inverse).

Definition (inverse). Let $\star$ be a product with a two-sided unit $e$. An element $x$ is invertible of $\star$ when there is $y$ with $x\star y=y\star x=e$; such a $y$ is an inverse of $x$ of $\star$.

Throughout this article the product is the antisymmetric plain multiplication $\wedge$ of the block, $\tilde P\wedge\tilde Q=\tfrac12(\tilde P\tilde Q-\tilde Q\tilde P)$, and unqualified, unit and inverse mean the unit and the inverse of $\wedge$. The product is written from the general plain bilinear product $\tilde P\tilde Q$ by antisymmetrisation, the construction of The Symmetric and Antisymmetric Parts of an Algebra Product. The reader is asked to keep the reading relative: the unit $e_0$ of the algebra is a unit of the plain product and is not a unit of the block, and no equation below is transferred across the exchange of the two products.

The Absence of a Unit

Proposition (no unit). The operation $\wedge$ has no left unit and no right unit; there is no $\tilde E$ with $\tilde E\wedge\tilde X=\tilde X$ for all $\tilde X$, and none with $\tilde X\wedge\tilde E=\tilde X$ for all $\tilde X$.

Proof. Let $\tilde E$ be a left unit. Evaluating at $\tilde X=e_0$, whose vector part is zero, gives $$ \tilde E\wedge e_0=\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{0}=0, $$ which would have to equal $e_0$; the two differ, since $e_0$ is nonzero. Let $\tilde E$ be a right unit. Evaluating at $\tilde X=e_0$ gives $$ e_0\wedge\tilde E=\mathbf{0}\times\mathbf{E}=0, $$ which would again have to equal $e_0$. Hence neither one-sided unit exists.

The absence is structural and not accidental: the value of the block is a pure vector for every pair of arguments, and the equations that would name a unit require the central element $e_0$ on the right. The scalar direction is invisible to the operation, and a unit is exactly an element the operation cannot see.

The Absence of an Inverse

Corollary (no inverse). No element of $\mathbb{B}$ is invertible of the block. Equivalently, the group of units of $\wedge$, the set of elements that have an inverse of $\wedge$, is empty.

Proof. The inverse is defined by the pair of equations $\tilde X\wedge\tilde Y=e$ and $\tilde Y\wedge\tilde X=e$, with $e$ a two-sided unit of the product; the block has no two-sided unit by the proposition above, so the equations have no solution, whatever the pair $(\tilde X,\tilde Y)$. There is no one-sided notion either, the one-sided inverse being defined against a one-sided unit and no one-sided unit existing.

Remark (the equation to the centre). A direct computation reaches the same conclusion without naming a unit. The equation $$ \tilde X\wedge\tilde Y=e_0 $$ has no solution in $\mathbb{B}$, because the left side is a pure vector, of scalar part zero, for every pair $\tilde X,\tilde Y$, and the right side has scalar part one. The block cannot produce a central value at all, so the equation that defines an inverse is unsolvable even before the two-sided character of the unit is required.

The Absence of a Norm

Definition (norm of a product). The algebraic norm attached to a product $\star$ with an involution ${}^{\natural}$ is the quadratic map $q_\star(\tilde X)=\tilde X\star\tilde X^{\natural}$, read as an element of $\mathbb{B}$ and not as a real number; for the general plain bilinear product it is the central square $N(\tilde X)e_0=\tilde X\tilde X^{\natural}$ with $N(\tilde X)=\sum_\mu X_\mu^{2}$ (Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Algebra).

For the block the two quadratic maps available are the square and the conjugated square, and neither is a norm.

Proposition (the block has no norm). For every element $\tilde X$, $$ \tilde X\wedge\tilde X=0,\qquad \tilde X\wedge\tilde X^{\natural}=0 . $$ Both canonical quadratics of the block — the square and the conjugated square — vanish identically, so neither is a norm.

Proof. The first identity is alternation, $\tilde X\wedge\tilde X=0$ for every $\tilde X$, by Introduction to the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra of Biquaternions. For the second, the natural conjugate is $\tilde X^{\natural}=X_0e_0-\mathbf{X}$, and the value of the block on a pair is the cross product of the two vector parts, so $$ \tilde X\wedge\tilde X^{\natural} =\mathbf{X}\times(-\mathbf{X})=-\bigl(\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{X}\bigr)=0 , $$

The failure is total, and it is stronger than the failure of the quaternionic sibling of this block. The block removes the scalar part of the parent product; the parent's central-valued quadratic form is the norm $N(\tilde X)=X_0^{2}+(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X})$, read from the product $\tilde X\tilde X^{\natural}$ with the conjugate, and the block, whose values are pure vectors, produces no central value at all. The block inherits the square of the parent with the scalar part removed, and with the scalar part removed from a square the central quadratic form is gone. What replaces it is a pair of forms on the whole space, and an operator form, which are the subject of the next section.

The Two Forms and the Operator Form

Definition (the operator trace form). The operator trace form of the block is $$ K(\tilde A,\tilde B)=\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(L_{\tilde A}L_{\tilde B}\bigr), \qquad L_{\tilde A}\tilde R=\tilde A\wedge\tilde R , $$ the trace of the composition of the two left multiplication operators.

Definition (the scalar-part form). The scalar-part form of the block is $$ \varphi(\tilde P,\tilde Q)=P_0Q_0 , $$ the product of the two scalar parts.

Proposition (the operator trace form). For all $\tilde A,\tilde B$, $$ K(\tilde A,\tilde B)=-2\,\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}=-2\,(A_1B_1+A_2B_2+A_3B_3), $$ so on the diagonal $K(\tilde X,\tilde X)=-2(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X})$, the Gram matrix of $K$ in the basis is $\operatorname{diag}(0,-2,-2,-2)$, the rank of $K$ is three, its radical is the centre $\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{B}}=\mathbb{C}e_0$, and on the vector subspace $K$ is non-degenerate.

Proof. The operator $L_{\tilde A}$ kills the scalar direction and acts on the vector part by $\mathbf{X}\mapsto\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{X}$, so $$ L_{\tilde A}L_{\tilde B}\mathbf{X} =\mathbf{A}\times(\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{X}) =\mathbf{B}\,(\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{X})-(\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B})\,\mathbf{X} . $$ The first summand is the rank-one operator $\mathbf{X}\mapsto\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{X})$, of trace $\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}$, and the second is $-(\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B})$ times the identity of the three-dimensional vector part, of trace $-3(\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B})$; the scalar direction contributes nothing. The trace is therefore $-2\,\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}$. The Gram entries are read at the units, $\mathbf{e}_i\cdot\mathbf{e}_j=\delta_{ij}$, and the radical is the set orthogonal to every vector part, that is the centre. Computed exactly on general elements with coefficients in $\mathbb{Q}(i)$ and on the sixteen pairs of the basis.

Proposition (the invariance of the two forms). Both forms are symmetric and invariant under the block: for all $\tilde P,\tilde Q,\tilde X$, $$ K\bigl(\tilde P\wedge\tilde X,\tilde Q\bigr)+K\bigl(\tilde X,\tilde P\wedge\tilde Q\bigr)=0, \qquad \varphi\bigl(\tilde P\wedge\tilde X,\tilde Q\bigr)+\varphi\bigl(\tilde X,\tilde P\wedge\tilde Q\bigr)=0 . $$

Proof. For $K$ the invariance equation reads, by the formula of the previous proposition, $$ -2\,(\mathbf{P}\times\mathbf{X})\cdot\mathbf{Q}-2\,\mathbf{X}\cdot(\mathbf{P}\times\mathbf{Q})=0, $$ which is the alternation of the scalar triple product, $(\mathbf{P}\times\mathbf{X})\cdot\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{X}\cdot(\mathbf{P}\times\mathbf{Q})$ with a sign; the centre contributes nothing to either slot. For $\varphi$ both terms vanish outright, because $\tilde P\wedge\tilde X$ and $\tilde P\wedge\tilde Q$ are pure vectors and the scalar-part form of an element with a central argument is zero. The symmetry of $K$ is the symmetry of $\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}$, and that of $\varphi$ is the symmetry of the product of the two scalar parts.

Remark (the two invariant forms). The operator trace form $K$ and the scalar-part form $\varphi$ are independent, and they span the invariant symmetric forms of the block: a computation on the central direction forces every invariant form to annihilate the pairings of the centre with the vector part, and on the vector subspace the form is proportional to $\mathbf{P}\cdot\mathbf{Q}$, the vector subspace carrying the irreducible three-dimensional representation of the block (The Lie Algebra of the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra). The two extremes are $\varphi$, of rank one, whose radical and isotropic cone are the whole vector subspace, and $K$, non-degenerate on the vector subspace, whose radical is the centre. The combination $\varphi+\tfrac12K$ is the trace form $\operatorname{Sc}(\tilde P\tilde Q)$ of the associative parent, invariant for the same reason.

Remark (the substitute). The form the block uses is $K$: it is the invariant form of the block, identified with the Killing form of the bracket in The Killing Form of the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra, and its radical, its rank, the reading of the Cartan criteria on it and the Casimir element it produces are that article's subject. The present article only fixes the definition and the diagonal value, and cites the rest. The scalar-part form $\varphi$ is the complementary rank- one form; in the quaternionic sibling of this block the roles are exchanged, the rank-one form being there the substitute and the operator trace form failing invariance, because there the product is not a Lie bracket.

Multiplicativity

Definition (multiplicativity). Let $\star$ be a product and let $q$ be a quadratic form on $\mathbb{B}$, valued in $\mathbb{C}$ or in the centre. The form is multiplicative of $\star$ when $$ q(x\star y)=q(x)\,q(y) $$ for all $x,y$, the equation being read in the scalars.

Proposition (no multiplicativity). The square is the zero map and satisfies the multiplicativity equation only because both sides vanish, so it is a norm in no sense. Of the three nonzero quadratic forms available, none is multiplicative: the operator trace form gives $$ K(e_1\wedge e_2,e_1\wedge e_2)=K(e_3,e_3)=-2 \quad\text{against}\quad K(e_1,e_1)K(e_2,e_2)=(-2)(-2)=4 ; $$ the scalar-part form gives $$ \varphi(e_0\wedge e_0,e_0\wedge e_0)=\varphi(0,0)=0 \quad\text{against}\quad \varphi(e_0,e_0)^{2}=1 ; $$ and the norm $N$ of the parent product gives $$ N(e_1\wedge e_1)=N(0)=0 \quad\text{against}\quad N(e_1)^{2}=1 . $$

Proof. The square vanishes at every element, so both sides of its multiplicativity equation vanish and the equation carries no information. Each displayed pair separates the two sides: the values of the block are $e_1\wedge e_2=e_3$, $e_0\wedge e_0=0$ and $e_1\wedge e_1=0$, read from the table of the block, and the three right sides are computed from $K=-2\,\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}$, $\varphi=P_0Q_0$ and $N(\tilde X)=\sum_\mu X_\mu^{2}$. A single pair separates the two sides in each case, so no multiplicativity holds.

The failure at the pairs displayed is the same failure the absence of a norm records: a multiplicative quadratic form of a product that has a unit takes the value one at the unit, and the block has no unit, so the general multiplicativity equation has no reason to hold and does not.

The Comparison with the Parent Product

Remark (what the removal of the scalar part costs). The parent product, the general plain bilinear product $\tilde P\tilde Q$, is associative and unital, with unit $e_0$, and it carries the central square $N(\tilde X)e_0=\tilde X\tilde X^{\natural}$ with $N(\tilde X)=\sum_\mu X_\mu^{2}$, the norm of the general plain algebra. Its antisymmetric half, the block, is the parent with the scalar part removed, and the two losses are one loss: a norm is a central-valued quadratic map, and the block's values are pure vectors, so the block carries no central quadratic form. The scalar part of the parent contributes to the norm through its square $X_0^{2}$, which in the block survives only as the rank-one form $\varphi$.

Remark (the invertibility criterion is not transferred). For the general plain algebra the invertibility criterion is $N(\tilde Q)\neq0$, equivalently the central square nonzero (Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Algebra). The criterion names the product, and for the block the corresponding statement is the absence of the previous sections: there is no unit against which to invert, and the substitute equation $\tilde X\wedge\tilde Y=e_0$ has no solution. No element of the block is invertible, and the empty group of units is the sharp form of the statement.

Summary

The antisymmetric plain multiplication $\tilde P\wedge\tilde Q=\mathbf{P}\times\mathbf{Q}$ has no unit on either side, hence no invertible element and an empty group of units, and the inverse equation $\tilde X\wedge\tilde Y=e_0$ has no solution because every value of the block is a pure vector. The block has no norm: its square vanishes identically by alternation, and its conjugated square vanishes identically as well, $\tilde X\wedge\tilde X^{\natural}=\mathbf{X}\times(-\mathbf{X})=0$, so neither canonical quadratic is a norm, and the central-valued norm of the parent product is lost with the scalar part of the parent. The two quadratic objects that survive are the operator trace form $K(\tilde A,\tilde B)=\operatorname{Tr}(L_{\tilde A}L_{\tilde B})=-2\,\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}$, of Gram matrix $\operatorname{diag}(0,-2,-2,-2)$, rank three and radical the centre, which is the invariant form of the block and the Killing form of the bracket, and the rank-one scalar-part form $\varphi(\tilde P,\tilde Q)=P_0Q_0$, whose radical and isotropic cone are the vector subspace; the two are independent and span the invariant symmetric forms, and neither is multiplicative, the pairs $(e_1,e_2)$, $(e_0,e_0)$ and $(e_1,e_1)$ separating the two sides of the multiplicativity equation. The invertibility criterion of the general plain algebra is not transferred, and its place is taken by the absence of a unit and of an inverse.
