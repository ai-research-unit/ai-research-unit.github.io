# __The Square-Zero Elements of the Two General Products__

## Introduction

The **square-zero set** of a product is the set of elements whose square in that product vanishes, and a **square-zero element**, or **nilpotent**, is a nonzero member of it. This article determines the square-zero sets of the two general products and of the six structures they generate, and contrasts the empty class that survives with the nilpotent cone of the biquaternion chapter. The products are *The Two General Products of the Quaternion $\mathbb{R}$ Space* and the six structures are *The 6 Algebraic Structures over the Quaternion $\mathbb{R}$ Space*, above this article in this category.

## Square-Zero Elements of a Product

**Definition.** Let $\star$ be a product on $\mathbb{H}$. The **square-zero set** of $\star$ is the solution set of
$$
\tilde\nu\star\tilde\nu=0 ,
$$
and a **square-zero element**, or **nilpotent**, of the product is a nonzero member, written $\tilde\nu$ after the convention of *Conventions in Mathematics*. The set is read relative to the product, as the name *square-zero element of the general plain product* records. A nonzero square-zero element is at once a zero divisor of the product, its own annihilating partner, and a root of the central value $0$; the three classes coincide in the alternating products, and this article owns only the comparison of the class across the structures.

## The Two General Products

**Theorem.** Neither the general plain product $\tilde p\tilde q$ nor the general quaternionic product $\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde q$ has a square-zero element.

**Proof.** For the plain product, if $\tilde\nu\neq0$ then $\tilde\nu$ is invertible, and multiplying $\tilde\nu^2=0$ on the left by $\tilde\nu^{-1}$ gives $\tilde\nu=0$, a contradiction; hence the only solution is $\tilde\nu=0$. For the quaternionic product, $\tilde\nu^{\natural}\tilde\nu=N(\tilde\nu)e_0$; the norm is a sum of four real squares, so $N(\tilde\nu)=0$ forces $\tilde\nu=0$. In both products the solution set of the square-zero equation is the origin alone.

The proof is the division property, quoted from *Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Algebra*, above this article in this category.

## The Six Structures

| structure | product | square-zero set | reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| GPA | $\tilde p\tilde q$ | $\{0\}$ | division |
| GQA | $\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde q$ | $\{0\}$ | definiteness of the norm |
| SPA | $\tilde p\bullet\tilde q$ | $\{0\}$ | $\tilde x\bullet\tilde x=\tilde x^2$ |
| SQA | $\tfrac12(\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde q+\tilde q^{\natural}\tilde p)$ | $\{0\}$ | value is $N(\tilde x)e_0$ |
| APA | $\tilde p\times\tilde q$ | all of $\mathbb{H}$ | alternation |
| AQA | $\tfrac12(\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde q-\tilde q^{\natural}\tilde p)$ | all of $\mathbb{H}$ | alternation |

The symmetrised structures keep the origin alone, for the same reason that they keep the roots of $0$ at the origin: their square is the plain square or the norm times $e_0$. The two antisymmetric structures are **alternating**, $\tilde x\star\tilde x=0$ for every $\tilde x$, so their square-zero set is all of $\mathbb{H}$, the origin included; the nonzero members coincide with the zero divisors and with the roots of $0$ computed in *The Square Roots of a Central Value in the Two General Products*, above this article in this category.

## Contrast with the Biquaternion Case

In the biquaternion chapter the square-zero elements of the four general products form the **nilpotent cone**, a complex quadric of complex dimension two, and its nonzero elements are exactly the zero divisors of the general products; the four products share it, and the ideal generated by a nilpotent coincides with its annihilator. Over $\mathbb{R}$ there is no nilpotent cone: the definiteness of the norm empties the class for the two general products and for their symmetrisations, and the only products with square-zero elements are the antisymmetrised ones, where the class is the whole space. The contrast is the collapse from a cone to the empty set for the two general products, and from a cone to the whole space for the antisymmetrisations.

## Summary

Neither of the two general products has a square-zero element: their square-zero sets are the origin alone, by the division property of the plain product and the definiteness of the norm for the quaternionic one; the symmetrised structures keep the same one-point set. The two antisymmetric structures have every element square zero, by alternation, so their square-zero set is the whole space and coincides with their zero divisors and their roots of $0$. The biquaternion chapter has a shared nilpotent cone instead, and the difference is again the division property lost at the complexification.
