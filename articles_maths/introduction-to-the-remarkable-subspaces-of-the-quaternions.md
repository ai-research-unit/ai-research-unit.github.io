# __Introduction to the Remarkable Subspaces of the Quaternions__

## Introduction

The quaternion algebra $\mathbb{H}$ carries two distinguished real subspaces and no more: the **scalar subspace**, the line of real quaternions, and the **vector subspace**, the three-dimensional space of pure quaternions. This article introduces them: the involutions that define them, their coordinate conditions, their bases and dimensions, the extent to which each is closed under the product, the restriction of the quaternion norm to each, and the reason the fuller lattice of the biquaternion algebra has no counterpart here. The element, the basis and the product are those of *Quaternion Algebra*; the real vector space reading is *The Quaternions as a Vector Space over $\mathbb{R}$*, and the involutions are *The Group of Involutions of the Quaternions*.

This article is the entry point of the remarkable-subspace group of the chapter, and the counterpart of *Introduction to the Remarkable Subspaces* of the biquaternion chapter, where six subspaces sit in a lattice generated by a central imaginary unit and an indefinite form. Here there is no central unit and the quaternion norm is definite, so the lattice has two subspaces and the norm is positive. The point of the article is exactly this contrast, and it is stated at the end rather than left to the reader.

The article owns the two subspaces one by one: the defining condition, the coordinate condition, the basis, the dimension, the role, and the two closures. It owns no product theory, no form and no topology; the norm is quoted from *The 2 Algebraic Norms over the Quaternion $\mathbb{R}$ Space*, below this article in this category, and the invertibility criterion from *Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Algebra*, below this article in this category.

## The Three Involutions and the Two Subspaces

The biquaternion chapter distinguishes three commuting involutions on the element, the coefficientwise bar, the natural sign ${}^{\natural}$ and the star ${}^{*}=\bar{\cdot}\,{}^{\natural}$. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the coefficientwise bar is the identity, since the coefficients are real, and the star therefore coincides with the natural sign:
$$
\bar \tilde q=\tilde q , \qquad \tilde q^{*}=\tilde q^{\natural}\quad\text{for every }\tilde q\in\mathbb{H}.
$$
The three involutions of the biquaternion algebra thus collapse to two distinct maps, the identity and the natural sign, and the fixed-space lattice they generate collapses with them. The equation above is the whole content of the bar over $\mathbb{H}$; this is the reading fixed once and for all for the group, and it is stated again in *The Group of Involutions of the Quaternions*. On an element of $\mathbb{H}$ the article writes ${}^{\natural}$ and never a bar as a conjugation.

The natural sign is an anti-involution, $\tilde q^{\natural}=q_0-\mathbf q$ with $\tilde q=q_0e_0+q_1e_1+q_2e_2+q_3e_3$ and $\mathbf q=q_1e_1+q_2e_2+q_3e_3$, and its $\pm1$ eigenspaces are the two remarkable subspaces. There is no fourth real subspace: the only other involutions available act by inner conjugation and move the two subspaces among themselves, and the article records their action in *Comparison of the Remarkable Subspaces of the Quaternions*, below this article in this category.

## The Scalar Subspace

**Definition.** The **scalar subspace** is the fixed space of ${}^{\natural}$,
$$
\mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{H}}=\{\tilde q\in\mathbb{H}:\tilde q^{\natural}=\tilde q\}=\{\tilde q:\mathbf q=0\}.
$$
Its projection is the scalar part $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde q)=\tfrac12(\tilde q+\tilde q^{\natural})$.

**Coordinate condition.** $q_1=q_2=q_3=0$; that is, $\mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{H}}=\{q_0e_0:q_0\in\mathbb{R}\}\cong\mathbb{R}$.

**Basis and dimension.** Basis $(e_0)$, dimension $1$.

**Role.** The scalar subspace is a subalgebra of $\mathbb{H}$ isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}$; it is the **centre** of the algebra, since it commutes with every element and every central element is scalar; and it is closed under inversion, the inverse of a nonzero scalar being the scalar $1/s$. It is the unique copy of the field in $\mathbb{H}$ up to the choice of a real unit, and it is the base of the algebra as a free module, the whole of $\mathbb{H}$ being a free module of rank four over it.

**Norm.** On $\mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{H}}$ the quaternion norm is the square, $N(s)=s^2$, and it is positive definite on the line; the nonzero scalars are exactly the units of the line.

## The Vector Subspace

**Definition.** The **vector subspace** is the anti-fixed space of ${}^{\natural}$,
$$
\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})=\{\tilde q\in\mathbb{H}:\tilde q^{\natural}=-\tilde q\}=\{\tilde q:\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde q)=0\},
$$
also written $\operatorname{Im}\mathbb{H}$. Its projection is the vector part $\mathbf q=\tfrac12(\tilde q-\tilde q^{\natural})$.

**Coordinate condition.** $q_0=0$; that is, $\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})=\{q_1e_1+q_2e_2+q_3e_3\}\cong\mathbb{R}^3$.

**Basis and dimension.** Basis $(e_1,e_2,e_3)$, dimension $3$.

**Role (not a subalgebra).** The vector subspace is not closed under the product. For $\mathbf p,\mathbf q$ pure,
$$
\mathbf p\,\mathbf q=-\langle\mathbf p,\mathbf q\rangle+\mathbf p\times\mathbf q,
$$
with $\langle\mathbf p,\mathbf q\rangle=\sum_kp_kq_k$ the inner product and $\mathbf p\times\mathbf q$ the cross product; the scalar part vanishes exactly when $\mathbf p$ and $\mathbf q$ are orthogonal. The symmetric and antisymmetric parts are
$$
\mathbf p\mathbf q+\mathbf q\mathbf p=-2\langle\mathbf p,\mathbf q\rangle , \qquad \mathbf p\mathbf q-\mathbf q\mathbf p=2(\mathbf p\times\mathbf q),
$$
so the cross product is recovered by $\mathbf p\times\mathbf q=\tfrac12(\mathbf p\mathbf q-\mathbf q\mathbf p)$. The subspace is closed under inversion — for pure nonzero $\mathbf q$ the inverse is $-\mathbf q/N(\mathbf q)$, again pure — but the square $\mathbf q^2=-N(\mathbf q)$ leaves the subspace. The vector subspace is the derived part of the algebra: the products of its elements generate $\mathbb{H}$, and under the commutator bracket it is the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ of the group of unit quaternions.

**Norm.** On $\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})$ the quaternion norm is the square of the Euclidean norm, $N(\mathbf q)=|\mathbf q|^2=\langle\mathbf q,\mathbf q\rangle$, and it is positive definite on the three-space. Its unit sphere is the sphere of roots of $-1$: $N(\mathbf q)=1$ is $\mathbf q^2=-1$, the sphere of unit imaginary quaternions that carry the inner involutions of *The Group of Involutions of the Quaternions*.

Together with the decomposition $\tilde q=\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde q)+\mathbf q$ the two restrictions give the orthogonal sum
$$
N(\tilde q)=N(\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde q))+N(\mathbf q)=q_0^2+|\mathbf q|^2,
$$
the two subspaces being orthogonal for the associated inner product; this is *The 2 Algebraic Norms over the Quaternion $\mathbb{R}$ Space*, below this article in this category, read on the two blocks. Invertibility is non-vanishing norm, by *Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Algebra*, below this article in this category: every nonzero element of either subspace is a unit, the scalar line and the vector space both meeting the singular set only at zero.

## The Two-Subspace Lattice and the Absence of the Full Lattice

The biquaternion algebra carries, beside the centre and the vector subspace, the quaternion subsystem $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$, the anti-quaternion subsystem $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ and the Hermitian halves $\mathbb{M}_\pm$, and these six real subspaces form the lattice of *Comparison of the Remarkable Subspaces* of the biquaternion chapter.

**Proposition.** In the quaternion algebra the distinguished real subspaces are exactly two, the scalar line and the vector space, and they are supplementary,
$$
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{H}}\oplus\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H}) , \qquad \mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{H}}\cap\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})=0 , \qquad 1+3=4 .
$$
Every other subspace distinguished by the involutions is $0$, $\mathbb{H}$, or one of these two.

**Proof.** The only non-trivial involution of $\mathbb{H}$ available on elements is ${}^{\natural}$, whose $\pm1$ eigenspaces are the two; the inner involutions are automorphisms that permute the vector lines and do not define new fixed spaces beyond the axis plane $\mathbb{C}_\nu=\mathbb{R}e_0\oplus\mathbb{R}\nu$ and its orthogonal complement $\nu^\perp$, which depend on the chosen axis and are not invariant objects of the algebra.

**Theorem (the fuller lattice needs the complexification).** The six-subspace lattice of the biquaternion algebra has no counterpart in $\mathbb{H}$. It grows to its biquaternion size exactly when the coefficient field is enlarged to $\mathbb{C}$, which supplies a central imaginary unit and an indefinite algebraic norm; the three Hermitian-type subspaces $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}},\mathbb{M}_+,\mathbb{M}_-$ then appear beside the three that survive. The reason is one statement in two guises: the full lattice needs an **indefinite form**, and the Hermitian decomposition is meaningful only when there are elements of negative norm.

**Proof.** The three missing subspaces are defined by the maps $\tilde q\mapsto iq$, by the Hermitian conjugation ${}^{*}$ and by the anti-Hermitian conjugation, each of which uses the central unit $i$ of the complexification; over $\mathbb{R}$ there is no such central unit, and the only involution is ${}^{\natural}$. For the form: over $\mathbb{C}$, $N(\tilde Q)=Q_0^2+Q_1^2+Q_2^2+Q_3^2$ takes negative real values, and the separation of the positive from the indefinite elements is the content of $\mathbb{M}_\pm$; over $\mathbb{R}$ one has $N(\tilde q)=q_0^2+|\mathbf q|^2\ge0$, with equality only at zero, so the separation is empty.

The complexification and the lattice are the material of the biquaternion chapter, *Decompositions Along the Remarkable Subspaces* and *Biquaternions as a Vector Space over $\mathbb{C}$*; the functor is *The Complexification and the Effect of the Change of Scalars on the Quaternions*, below this article in this category. The additive decomposition carried by the two subspaces, and the polar decomposition the norm adds to it, are *Decompositions Along the Remarkable Subspaces of the Quaternions*, below this article in this category.

## Summary

The quaternion algebra has two remarkable real subspaces. The scalar line $\mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{H}}$ is the fixed space of the natural sign, of dimension one, a subalgebra and the centre, closed under inversion, on which the norm is the square. The vector space $\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})$ is the anti-fixed space, of dimension three, not a subalgebra, with product $\mathbf p\mathbf q=-\langle\mathbf p,\mathbf q\rangle+\mathbf p\times\mathbf q$; it is closed under inversion, its square is the negative norm, and its unit sphere is the sphere of roots of $-1$. The two are supplementary and orthogonal for the norm, and $N(\tilde q)=q_0^2+|\mathbf q|^2$. The biquaternion lattice of six subspaces does not occur here, because there is no central imaginary unit and the norm is definite; both arrive with the complexification, and the fuller lattice is the biquaternion chapter's.
