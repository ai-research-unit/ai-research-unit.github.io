$M_4(\mathbb{C})_L$ Representation : The Six Subspaces as Matrix Conditions

Introduction

The six remarkable subspaces of the biquaternion algebra are the fixed and anti-fixed spaces of the four conjugations and the two halves of the scalar–vector split. In the regular model each becomes an entrywise matrix condition: scalar, traceless, real, purely imaginary, Hermitian, anti-Hermitian. The conditions are exact only inside the four-dimensional image; the ambient matrix algebra is much larger, and this is the main difference from the $2\times2$ model.

This article collects the six descriptions, their dimensions, traces, norms and signatures, and the idempotents and involutions they contain. It is the $4\times4$ counterpart of $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ Representation : The Six Subspaces as Matrix Conditions.

Conventions. The algebra and the model are those of $M_4(\mathbb{C})_L$ Representation : The Matrix Product and the Algebra Structure. Write $Q_\mu=q_\mu+iq'_\mu$ with $q_\mu,q'_\mu$ real. The four involutions are $$ \tilde Q^{\natural}=Q_t-\vec Q,\qquad \overline{\tilde Q}=\overline{Q_t}+\overline{\vec Q},\qquad \tilde Q^{*}=\overline{\tilde Q^{\natural}},\qquad \tilde Q^{\flat}=-\tilde Q^{*}. $$

Boundaries. The subspaces are defined in Introduction to the Remarkable Subspaces; the four forms restricted to them are Remarkable Subspaces and the Four General Products, Forms and Norms; the trace and determinant are $M_4(\mathbb{C})_L$ Representation : Trace, Determinant and the Biquaternion Norm; the $2\times2$ version is $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ Representation : The Six Subspaces as Matrix Conditions.

The determinant of $M$ is $N^2$ on every row, so it is a quartic on the subspace. The table records the quadratic norm $N$, not the determinant, because the norm is the form whose signature is meaningful.

The warning. The conditions in the third column are not sufficient in the ambient $M_4(\mathbb{C})$. The Hermitian matrices alone form a real space of dimension $16$, not $4$; the real matrices alone form a real space of dimension $16$, not $4$. The six subspaces are the intersections of those conditions with the image, and the dimensions in the table are the dimensions of the intersections. In the $2\times2$ model the image is all of $M_2(\mathbb{C})$, so no warning is needed; in the regular model it is essential.

The Centre Subspace

The scalar matrices.

$$ \mathsf{M}_4(Q_te_t)=Q_t\mathrm I_4, \qquad \operatorname{Tr}M=4Q_t, \qquad \det M=Q_t^4. $$

The image is the complex line of scalar matrices, of real dimension two. It is the only commutative subspace and the only one of real dimension two. It meets $\mathbb{M}_+$ in the real line $\mathbb{R}\mathrm I_4$ and $\mathbb{M}_-$ in the imaginary line $i\mathbb{R}\mathrm I_4$.

The Vector Subspace

The traceless matrices.

$$ \operatorname{Tr}M=4Q_t=0 \iff Q_t=0. $$

The condition is equivalent, inside the image, to skew-symmetry: $$ \operatorname{Tr}M=0 \iff M^{\mathsf T}=-M, $$ because the transpose is quaternion conjugation and $Q^{\natural}=-Q$ exactly when $Q_t=0$. The image is the three-dimensional complex space of regular matrices with zero scalar part, of real dimension six. It is closed under the commutator and not under the product.

The two chiral halves. The three matrices $\mathsf{M}_4(\vec e_k)$ are real and skew-symmetric. They span a three-dimensional subspace of the six-dimensional space of complex skew matrices, one of the two chiral halves of $\mathfrak{so}_4(\mathbb{C})$. The other half is not in the image. This is the regular analogue of the traceless matrices being all of $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$: in $M_4(\mathbb{C})$ the traceless condition alone is fifteen-dimensional, and the image selects a three-dimensional subspace.

The Quaternion Subspace

The real matrices. $$ \mathsf{M}_4(\tilde Q)=\overline{\mathsf{M}_4(\tilde Q)} \iff Q_\mu\in\mathbb{R}\ \text{for all }\mu. $$ The image is the space of real matrices of the regular form, of real dimension four. It is closed under multiplication and is a division algebra.

The trace and the norm. On the quaternion subspace the trace is $4q_t$, real, and the norm is the positive definite sum $\sum q_\mu^2$, of signature $(4,0)$.

The contrast with $2\times2$. In the $2\times2$ model the quaternion subspace is the conjugate-linear pattern $\begin{pmatrix}z&w\\-\bar w&\bar z\end{pmatrix}$, not the real matrices. In the regular model complex conjugation is entrywise, so the pattern disappears and the condition becomes realness of all entries. The price is that the image is only four real-dimensional.

The Anti-Quaternion Subspace

The purely imaginary matrices. $$ \mathsf{M}_4(\tilde Q)=-\overline{\mathsf{M}_4(\tilde Q)} \iff Q_\mu\in i\mathbb{R}\ \text{for all }\mu. $$ The image is the space of purely imaginary matrices of the regular form, of real dimension four. It is not closed under multiplication: the product of two purely imaginary matrices is real.

The trace and the norm. On the anti-quaternion subspace the trace is $4iq'_t$, purely imaginary, and the norm is the negative definite $-\sum(q'_\mu)^2$, of signature $(0,4)$.

The Informational Subspace

The Hermitian matrices. $$ \mathsf{M}_4(\tilde Q)^{\dagger}=\mathsf{M}_4(\tilde Q) \iff \tilde Q^{*}=\tilde Q. $$ The image is the space of Hermitian matrices of the regular form, of real dimension four. The trace is real on it, and the norm has signature $(1,3)$.

The Material Subspace

The anti-Hermitian matrices. $$ \mathsf{M}_4(\tilde Q)^{\dagger}=-\mathsf{M}_4(\tilde Q) \iff \tilde Q^{*}=-\tilde Q. $$ The image is the space of anti-Hermitian matrices of the regular form, of real dimension four. The trace is purely imaginary on it, and the norm has signature $(3,1)$, the other signature of the two real forms.

The warning repeated. The ambient Hermitian matrices are sixteen real-dimensional; the image of $\mathbb{M}_+$ is the four-dimensional subspace of Hermitian matrices of the form $\mathsf{M}_4(\tilde Q)$ with $\tilde Q^{*}=\tilde Q$. The distinction is the regular analogue of the fact that a general $2\times2$ Hermitian matrix need not be of the form $\mathsf{M}_2(\tilde Q)$; there the image is everything, here it is not.

The Roots of Plus One and the Idempotents

The Hermitian idempotents. For a real unit vector $\vec u$, $$ \tilde\Pi(\vec u)=\tfrac12(e_t+i\vec u), \qquad \tilde\Pi(\vec u)^2=\tilde\Pi(\vec u), \qquad \tilde\Pi(\vec u)^{*}=\tilde\Pi(\vec u). $$ The image $\mathsf{M}_4(\tilde\Pi(\vec u))$ is an orthogonal projector of rank two, with trace two. The family is a two-sphere, and it lies in $\mathbb{M}_+$.

The general idempotents. Every idempotent of $\mathbb{B}$ is a conjugate of one of these by a unit, and the family is two complex-dimensional. The general idempotent is not Hermitian, and its image is an oblique projector of rank two. In the regular model every nontrivial idempotent has rank two and trace two, whatever its Hermitian nature.

The roots of $+1$. The involutions $X^2=\mathrm I_4$ in the image are the images of the elements $\tilde X$ with $\tilde X^2=e_t$. They are the identity, minus the identity, and the conjugates of $i\vec e_z$. The traceless Hermitian ones are $\mathsf{M}_4(i\vec u)$ with $\lvert\vec u\rvert=1$; they lie in $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}\cap\mathbb{M}_+$. The anti-Hermitian subspace contains none, because an anti-Hermitian matrix has purely imaginary eigenvalues and cannot have the real eigenvalues $\pm1$.

The nilpotents. A nonzero nilpotent has square zero, trace zero and rank two. It lies in the vector subspace and on the null cone, and in none of the other five subspaces except through the origin. The rank-two nilpotent is the regular analogue of the rank-one nilpotent of the $2\times2$ model.

How They Fit Together

The three splits.

$$ \mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{B}}\oplus\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{B}) =\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}\oplus i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}} =\mathbb{M}_+\oplus\mathbb{M}_-. $$

The first is the trace decomposition, of real dimensions two and six. The second and the third are four-plus-four, and neither refines the other.

The intersections. The centre meets $\mathbb{M}_+$ in $\mathbb{R}e_t$ and $\mathbb{M}_-$ in $i\mathbb{R}e_t$. The vector subspace meets $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ in the pure quaternions, of real dimension three, and $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ in the pure anti-quaternions. It meets $\mathbb{M}_+$ in $i$ times the real vector space, and $\mathbb{M}_-$ in the real vector space. The quaternion and anti-quaternion subspaces meet only at the origin, and so do the two sectors.

The subalgebras. The centre and the quaternion subspace are subalgebras; the vector subspace is a Lie subalgebra; the anti-quaternion subspace and the two sectors are neither. The statements are read from the matrix conditions and from the closure of the corresponding coefficient conditions.

The real structure. The regular model is a complex model, and it displays the two sectors as the Hermitian and the anti-Hermitian matrices. The two are anti-isometric, not isomorphic: the norm has opposite signatures, and the multiplication reverses the reality in the same way as in the algebra.

Summary

The six subspaces are the scalar, traceless, real, purely imaginary, Hermitian and anti-Hermitian matrices of the regular image. Their real dimensions are $2,6,4,4,4,4$, and their norms have signatures $(2,0),(6,0),(4,0),(0,4),(1,3),(3,1)$. The conditions are exact only inside the four-dimensional image; the ambient matrix algebra satisfies them on much larger sets. The nontrivial idempotents have rank two, the Hermitian ones forming a two-sphere and the general ones a two-complex-dimensional family.
