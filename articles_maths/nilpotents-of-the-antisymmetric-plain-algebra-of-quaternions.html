Nilpotents of the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra

Introduction

The nilpotent is defined by an equation in which the product occurs, so it is a class of the product and not of the space (Definitions for the Study of the 6 Algebraic Structures, §The Element Vocabulary). The block of this article is the antisymmetric plain multiplication

$$ \tilde p\times\tilde q=\tfrac12\bigl(\tilde p\tilde q-\tilde q\tilde p\bigr)=\mathbf p\times\mathbf q, $$

the cross product of the two vector parts, which is alternating (Introduction to the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra of Quaternions). The article determines its nilpotent class and states the two coincidences the vanishing square produces.

The answer is total. The square of every element of the block is zero, $\tilde p\times\tilde p=0$, so the nilpotent class is the whole space: the origin is its trivial member, of index one, and every other element is of index two, its square vanishing while it does not. The nilpotents, the nonzero elements of the class, are the punctured space; they coincide with the zero-divisor set and with the root set of $0$, and the implication nilpotent implies zero divisor holds for the block in the total form, the origin being the class's only element outside the zero divisors. Every element lies in its own annihilator on both sides, and the square detects no zero divisor here, vanishing on everything. The comparison with the parent product is the comparison of the whole space with the origin alone, the parent being a division product. The two companion articles are Zero Divisors of the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra and Square Roots of Minus One, Zero and Plus One in the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra, and the parent class is Nilpotents of the General Plain Algebra.

The Notion and Its Product

Definition (nilpotent of a product). Let $\star$ be a product on $\mathbb{H}$. An element is in the nilpotent class of $\star$ when some power of it vanishes, $\tilde\upsilon^{\star k}=0$ for some $k\geq1$, the powers being read $x^1=x$ and $x^{k+1}=x\star x^k$; the least such $k$ is the index of nilpotency. A nilpotent of $\star$ is a nonzero element of the class, a square-zero element is a nonzero element with $\tilde\upsilon\star\tilde\upsilon=0$, and a cube-zero element is a nonzero element with $f(\tilde\upsilon,f(\tilde\upsilon,\tilde\upsilon))=0$; a nilpotent of index two is exactly a square-zero element. The origin lies in the class of every product, of index one, and it is not a nilpotent (Definitions for the Study of the 6 Algebraic Structures, §The Element Vocabulary).

Throughout this article the product is the antisymmetric plain multiplication $\times$, and unqualified, nilpotent and class are read in $\times$. The nilpotent is the root of $0$ in the sense of the definitions article, and the nilpotent classes of the six structures, against which the block is read, are Nilpotents.

The Criterion

Theorem (the class is the whole space). For every element $\tilde\upsilon$,

$$ \tilde\upsilon\times\tilde\upsilon=0 , $$

so the nilpotent class of the block is the whole space, its nonzero elements, the nilpotents, are the punctured space, and every nilpotent is of index two; no element is of index three or more. The origin is the trivial member of the class, of index one.

Proof. The block is alternating, $\tilde x\times\tilde x=0$ for every $\tilde x$, by Introduction to the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra of Quaternions. Hence the second power of every element vanishes and the class, the set of elements some power of which vanishes, is the whole space. An element whose square vanishes has every higher power zero, the powers being read from the square, so its index is two when the element is nonzero and one at the origin. No element has index three or more, the square having already vanished.

The criterion is the alternation of the product read as an element statement. There is no stratification by index: the index is two on the punctured space and one at the origin, and none is of index three or more.

The Nilpotent Class

Proposition (the class and its nonzero part). The nilpotent class of the block is the whole space, and the nilpotents are the punctured space,

$$ \{\tilde\upsilon:\tilde\upsilon^{\times k}=0\text{ for some }k\}=\mathbb{H}, \qquad \{\tilde\upsilon\neq0:\tilde\upsilon\text{ nilpotent}\}=\mathbb{H}\setminus\{0\}. $$

Proof. The theorem gives the vanishing of the square at every element, so every element satisfies the defining condition with $k=2$ and lies in the class; the origin does so with $k=1$ and is the trivial member, of index one; the nonzero elements are the nilpotents, and the punctured space is their set.

The class is as large as the space allows, and it is stable under every operation of the space: the sum of two nilpotents is square zero, and so are the scalar multiple of a nilpotent and the bracket of two nilpotents, all three being elements of the space. The punctured space is therefore closed under the product, and the class is not a proper cone of the space.

The Relation to the Zero Divisors and to the Roots of Zero

Proposition (the four classes coincide). The nilpotents, the square-zero elements, the zero divisors and the roots of $0$ are one and the same set,

$$ \{\text{nilpotents}\}=\{\text{square-zero elements}\}=\{\text{zero divisors}\}=\{\text{roots of }0\}=\mathbb{H}\setminus\{0\}. $$

Proof. The nilpotents are the punctured space by the previous proposition, and every nilpotent is square zero, of index two. The zero divisors are the punctured space by Zero Divisors of the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra, because the block is alternating and every nonzero element is its own annihilating partner; and the roots of $0$ are the punctured space by Square Roots of Minus One, Zero and Plus One in the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra. The four descriptions select one set.

Corollary (the implication nilpotent implies zero divisor). For the block the implication holds in the total form: every nilpotent is a two-sided zero divisor, with itself as the annihilating partner. The origin is the class's only element that is not a zero divisor, the definition of the zero divisor requiring nonzero elements.

Proof. A nilpotent $\tilde\upsilon$ satisfies $\tilde\upsilon\times\tilde\upsilon=0$, and the element $\tilde\upsilon$ itself is a nonzero partner for both sides; the element is therefore a two-sided zero divisor. The origin lies in the class and is not a zero divisor, the definition requiring nonzero elements.

Remark (the square detects no zero divisor). The implication nilpotent implies zero divisor has no converse here: the square vanishes on everything, so the vanishing of a square carries no information about an element, and the class the square detects is the whole space rather than a proper subfamily of the zero divisors. The zero-divisor set is the punctured space as well (Zero Divisors of the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra), and the two sets agree; what fails is the discrimination, not the coincidence.

Corollary (self-annihilation). Every element lies in its own annihilator on both sides: for every $\tilde\upsilon$,

$$ \tilde\upsilon\in A_\times(\tilde\upsilon)\quad\text{and}\quad \tilde\upsilon\in B_\times(\tilde\upsilon), $$

the left and the right annihilator being read in the block.

Proof. The square vanishes, $\tilde\upsilon\times\tilde\upsilon=0$, and the left annihilator $A_\times(\tilde\upsilon)$ is the set of $\tilde x$ with $\tilde\upsilon\times\tilde x=0$; the choice $\tilde x=\tilde\upsilon$ gives the left membership. The right membership is the same display read in the other order, and the two sides coincide for the block, the product being alternating (Annihilators of the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra). Hence every element is its own annihilating partner, on the left and on the right.

The self-annihilation is the element-level form of the totality of the class. For a noncentral element the annihilator is the plane $\mathbb{R}e_0\oplus\mathbb{R}\mathbf\upsilon$ of real dimension two, which contains the element; for a central element it is the whole space. The element lies in its annihilator in every case, which is the statement that the square vanishes.

The Conjugations and the Remarkable Subspaces

Proposition (the conjugations preserve the class). The nilpotent class of the block is preserved by the bar, the natural conjugation and the star; the conjugates of an element of the class are elements of the class.

Proof. For the block the statement is immediate from the criterion, every element lying in the class, so every conjugate lies in it; the compatibility of each conjugation with the bracket is recorded here for the comparison with the other structures, where the class is proper and the preservation is a computation. The bar is the identity over $\mathbb{R}$ and so preserves the class trivially. The natural conjugation is an anti-automorphism of the parent product and it reverses the bracket, $\tilde p^{\natural}\times\tilde q^{\natural}=-(\tilde p\times\tilde q)^{\natural}$, so it carries a vanishing square to the negative of a vanishing square, which vanishes as well; the star coincides with the natural conjugation over $\mathbb{R}$.

Proposition (the restriction to the remarkable subspaces). On each of the remarkable subspaces the restriction of the block is again alternating, so every element of every subspace lies in the class of the restriction and every nonzero element of it is a nilpotent of the restriction.

Proof. The square of an element of the subspace vanishes as the square of an element of the ambient algebra vanishes, and the criterion applies to the elements in every case. The subspaces and their readings are Comparison of the Remarkable Subspaces of the Quaternions.

The Comparison with the Parent Product

Remark (the parent class). The parent general plain bilinear product is associative and unital, and it carries no nilpotent: it is a division product, so a nonzero $\tilde\upsilon$ is invertible and $\tilde\upsilon^2=0$ would give $\tilde\upsilon=0$ (Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Algebra; The Specificities of the General Plain Algebra). The parent's class is therefore the origin alone, that is the empty set off the origin. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the plain square has no isotropic direction, its isotropic cone being real only after complexification, so the pure elements that would be square zero over $\mathbb{C}$ are not available. The block is the antisymmetric half of the parent, and the comparison is the comparison of the whole space with the origin alone: the block's class strictly contains the parent's, the element $e_0$ lying in the class of the block ($e_0\times e_0=0$) and not of the parent ($e_0^2=e_0$). The enlargement is the removal of the scalar part: the parent's criterion is a condition on the square, and the block's square is identically zero, so the condition is satisfied by every element.

Remark (the reading among the six structures). The nilpotent class of the six structures is the origin alone for the general plain, the symmetric plain, the general quaternionic and the symmetric quaternionic structures, and it is the whole space for the antisymmetric plain and the antisymmetric quaternionic structures; only two sets occur, and no structure of the six has an intermediate one (Nilpotents). The block is one of the two structures whose square vanishes identically, and among the six it is the only one whose nilpotents all have index two. In the biquaternion chapter the class of the two antisymmetric biquaternion structures is likewise the whole space (Nilpotents), so the totality for an antisymmetric block is not an artefact of the real field.

Summary

For the antisymmetric plain multiplication $\tilde p\times\tilde q=\mathbf p\times\mathbf q$ the square of every element vanishes, so the nilpotent class is the whole space: the origin is its trivial member, of index one, and every other element is of index two. The nilpotents, the nonzero elements of the class, are the punctured space, and they coincide with the square-zero elements, the zero divisors and the root set of $0$, the four descriptions selecting one set. The implication nilpotent implies zero divisor holds in the total form, the origin being the only element of the class outside the zero divisors; every element lies in its own annihilator on both sides, the self-annihilation being the element-level form of the totality; and the square detects no zero divisor, vanishing on everything. The conjugations preserve the class and every remarkable subspace inherits it. The comparison with the parent product is the comparison of the whole space with the origin alone, the enlargement being the removal of the scalar part from the square.
