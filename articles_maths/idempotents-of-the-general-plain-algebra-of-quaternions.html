Idempotents of the General Plain Algebra

Introduction

The algebra article defined the quaternion algebra $\mathbb{H}$, its natural conjugation and its remarkable real subspaces. This article treats the idempotents of $\mathbb{H}$ — the elements satisfying $\tilde\Pi^2 = \tilde\Pi$ — and the projection and direct sum structure they carry.

Idempotents are the algebraic form of a projection. In the complexified block they do four separate jobs at once: they give the direct sum decompositions of the algebra into left ideals, they classify the non-pure zero divisors, they are in bijection with the roots of $-1$, and they drive the Peirce decomposition. Over $\mathbb{R}$ every one of these jobs collapses, because the only idempotents are $0$ and the unit $e_0$: there is no nontrivial decomposition, no non-pure zero divisor to attach to an idempotent, and no root of $-1$ to be in bijection with. The interest of the article is exactly this collapse, and it is proved from the division property alone.

The article is one of the last entries of the Common Theory of the block, after Square Roots of a General Element in the General Plain Algebra and before Nilpotents of the General Plain Algebra and The Specificities of the General Plain Algebra, all of them neighbours in this category. Its proofs use only the algebra article and the division property of Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Algebra, above this article in this category; the relations to the zero divisors, to the nilpotents and to the roots are pointers, and no claim of those articles is re-derived here.

Conventions

The algebra is $\mathbb{H}$, the four-dimensional real algebra with basis $e_0 = 1, e_1, e_2, e_3$, $e_k^2 = -e_0$ and $e_1e_2 = e_3 = -e_2e_1$. A general element is $\tilde q = q_0e_0 + \mathbf q$ with $q_0 \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbf q = q_1e_1 + q_2e_2 + q_3e_3$; the scalar part is $q_0 = \mathrm{Sc}(\tilde q)$ and the vector part is $\mathbf q$. The natural conjugation is $\tilde q^{\natural} = q_0e_0 - \mathbf q$, and the quaternion norm is $N(\tilde q) = \tilde q\tilde q^{\natural} = q_0^2 + |\mathbf q|^2$, positive definite over $\mathbb{R}$ by Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Algebra, above this article in this category. Every nonzero element is invertible, $\tilde q^{-1} = \tilde q^{\natural}/N(\tilde q)$, so there is no zero divisor: $\tilde p\tilde q = 0$ forces $\tilde p = 0$ or $\tilde q = 0$. The centre is the scalar line $\mathbb{R}e_0$ and the vector subspace is $\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})$.

The plain product throughout is the general plain bilinear product $\tilde p\tilde q$, the multiplication of the algebra — the only one of the six products of The 6 Products of the Quaternion Real Space that is associative with a two-sided unit, and the one the equation $\tilde\Pi^2 = \tilde\Pi$ presupposes. An idempotent is written $\tilde\Pi$ and a generic element $\tilde q$. The general definition of an idempotent is Definitions for the Study of the 6 Algebraic Structures, §The Element Vocabulary; the orthogonal, complete and primitive families and the Peirce decomposition are defined in the body of this article, and their general associative form is Unital Algebras, §Idempotents and the Peirce Decomposition. The six structures the plain product belongs to are The 6 Algebraic Structures over the Quaternion $\mathbb{R}$ Space.

Definition

An element $\tilde\Pi$ of $\mathbb{H}$ is an idempotent of the general plain algebra when

$$ \tilde\Pi^2 = \tilde\Pi , $$

the square being read in the general plain bilinear product. The equation names a product, so the notion is relative to it; the general definition of an idempotent is Definitions for the Study of the 6 Algebraic Structures, §The Element Vocabulary, and the orthogonal, complete and primitive families are defined in §Orthogonal and Complete Families below. Throughout this article the product is the general plain bilinear product, and another product is named where it is meant.

Idempotents encode direct summands of the regular module, $\mathbb{H} = \mathbb{H}\tilde\Pi \oplus \mathbb{H}(e_0 - \tilde\Pi)$ on the left and $\mathbb{H} = \tilde\Pi\mathbb{H} \oplus (e_0 - \tilde\Pi)\mathbb{H}$ on the right, and every such decomposition arises from an idempotent. A primitive idempotent — one that is not a sum of two nonzero orthogonal idempotents — generates a minimal left ideal. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the possibilities are as small as an algebra permits, and the sections below compute them.

The Idempotents

Theorem (the idempotents of the plain product). The idempotents of the general plain product are exactly $0$ and $e_0$.

Proof. If $\tilde\Pi^2 = \tilde\Pi$ then $\tilde\Pi(\tilde\Pi - e_0) = \tilde\Pi^2 - \tilde\Pi = 0$. By the absence of zero divisors (Zero Divisors of the General Plain Algebra, above this article in this category), one of the two factors vanishes: either $\tilde\Pi = 0$ or $\tilde\Pi - e_0 = 0$, that is $\tilde\Pi = e_0$. Both elements are idempotent, $0^2 = 0$ and $e_0^2 = e_0$.

The theorem is the extreme case of the classification of the complexified block, where every idempotent is $\tfrac12 e_0 \pm \tfrac12\tilde\Xi i$ over a root $\tilde\Xi$ of $-1$; over $\mathbb{R}$ the central unit $i$ is not available, the family is empty, and only the two trivial idempotents remain.

Remark (the two idempotents are as different as possible). The idempotent $0$ is not a unit, and it is not a zero divisor either, a zero divisor being required to be nonzero; the idempotent $e_0$ is the unit of the algebra. Neither is a zero divisor, and no other element of the algebra is idempotent. The complexified block places a whole manifold of nontrivial idempotents between the two, and the manifold is what disappears.

Orthogonal and Complete Families

An orthogonal family is a family of idempotents with $\tilde\Pi_i\tilde\Pi_j = 0$ for $i \neq j$; it is complete when the sum of its members is $e_0$. A nonzero idempotent is primitive when it is not the sum of two nonzero orthogonal idempotents.

Theorem (the families). In the plain product:

  • every orthogonal family contains at most one nonzero idempotent;
  • the only complete orthogonal family of nonzero idempotents is the singleton $\{e_0\}$;
  • the unit $e_0$ is the only primitive idempotent, and $0$ is not primitive.

Proof. By the theorem above every idempotent is $0$ or $e_0$. Two distinct nonzero idempotents would both be $e_0$, which is not orthogonal to itself, $e_0e_0 = e_0 \neq 0$; so no orthogonal family contains two nonzero members. A complete orthogonal family of nonzero idempotents sums to $e_0$, and since it contains at most one nonzero idempotent it is either empty, with sum $0 \neq e_0$, or the singleton $\{e_0\}$. For primitivity, $e_0$ is not a sum of two nonzero orthogonal idempotents because there are no two distinct nonzero idempotents to sum; and $0$ is not primitive under the convention that a primitive idempotent is nonzero.

Corollary (no nontrivial decomposition). The only decompositions of the algebra carried by an idempotent are the two trivial ones: for $\tilde\Pi = e_0$,

$$ \mathbb{H} = e_0\mathbb{H} \oplus (e_0 - e_0)\mathbb{H} = \mathbb{H} \oplus \{0\} , $$

and for $\tilde\Pi = 0$,

$$ \mathbb{H} = 0\cdot\mathbb{H} \oplus (e_0 - 0)\mathbb{H} = \{0\} \oplus \mathbb{H} . $$

There is no proper nonzero direct summand of the regular module in the plain product.

The Peirce Decomposition

For an idempotent $\tilde\Pi$ the Peirce decomposition is the direct sum

$$ \mathbb{H} = \tilde\Pi\mathbb{H}\tilde\Pi \;\oplus\; \tilde\Pi\mathbb{H}(e_0 - \tilde\Pi) \;\oplus\; (e_0 - \tilde\Pi)\mathbb{H}\tilde\Pi \;\oplus\; (e_0 - \tilde\Pi)\mathbb{H}(e_0 - \tilde\Pi), $$

the four Peirce spaces of the idempotent.

Theorem (the Peirce decomposition is trivial). For each of the two idempotents of the plain product one Peirce space is the whole algebra and the other three are $\{0\}$:

$$ \tilde\Pi = e_0 : \quad \mathbb{H} = \mathbb{H} \oplus \{0\} \oplus \{0\} \oplus \{0\} , \qquad \tilde\Pi = 0 : \quad \mathbb{H} = \{0\} \oplus \{0\} \oplus \{0\} \oplus \mathbb{H} . $$

Proof. For $\tilde\Pi = e_0$ the four spaces are $e_0\mathbb{H}e_0 = \mathbb{H}$, $e_0\mathbb{H}(e_0 - e_0) = 0$, $(e_0 - e_0)\mathbb{H}e_0 = 0$ and $(e_0 - e_0)\mathbb{H}(e_0 - e_0) = 0$. For $\tilde\Pi = 0$ the same computation exchanges the outer factors.

So the Peirce decomposition of the plain product is the trivial one, with Peirce spaces $\mathbb{H}$ and $\{0\}$ and no off-diagonal space. In particular the two off-diagonal Peirce units, which in the complexified block generate the minimal ideals, do not exist over $\mathbb{R}$: such a unit satisfies $\tilde\Upsilon^2 = 0$ with $\tilde\Upsilon \neq 0$, and there is none (Nilpotents of the General Plain Algebra, below this article in this category).

Idempotents and the Zero Divisors

A nontrivial idempotent is always a zero divisor, since

$$ \tilde\Pi(e_0 - \tilde\Pi) = \tilde\Pi - \tilde\Pi^2 = 0 $$

with both factors nonzero unless $\tilde\Pi$ is $0$ or $e_0$. Conversely, in the complexified block every non-pure zero divisor is a complex multiple of a nontrivial idempotent, $\tilde Q = 2Q_0\tilde\Pi$.

Over $\mathbb{R}$ both statements are vacuous, and the reason is one and the same: there are no nontrivial idempotents and no zero divisors. The only idempotents are $0$ and $e_0$; the element $0$ is not a zero divisor by the requirement that a divisor be nonzero, and $e_0$ is a unit. So no idempotent of the plain product is a zero divisor, which is the exact opposite of the complexified reading, where the nontrivial idempotents sit inside the zero-divisor cone as its non-pure family. The zero-divisor set is empty (Zero Divisors of the General Plain Algebra, above this article in this category), and the idempotent reading of it has nothing to describe.

Idempotents and the Roots of the Central Values

The idempotent equation is not a central-value equation, but the affine change of variable $\tilde\Xi = 2\tilde\Pi - e_0$ carries the one into the other: $\tilde\Pi^2 = \tilde\Pi$ holds exactly when $\tilde\Xi^2 = e_0$, since $\tilde\Xi^2 = 4\tilde\Pi^2 - 4\tilde\Pi + e_0 = e_0$. The named central values are the three $c = -1, 0, +1$ of Square Roots of Minus One, Zero and Plus One in the General Plain Algebra; the two read here are $c = +1$, whose roots the affine map ties to the idempotents, and $c = -1$, whose roots are the unit sphere of the vector subspace.

Proposition (roots of $+1$). The solutions of $\tilde x^2 = e_0$ are exactly $\pm e_0$.

Proof. $\tilde x^2 - e_0 = (\tilde x - e_0)(\tilde x + e_0) = 0$, and the absence of zero divisors forces $\tilde x = e_0$ or $\tilde x = -e_0$; both square to $e_0$.

So the idempotents and the roots of $+1$ are different sets, carried one to the other by the affine map: the first is $\{0, e_0\}$ and the second is $\{e_0, -e_0\}$, with $\tilde\Xi = 2\tilde\Pi - e_0$ sending $e_0$ to $e_0$ and $0$ to $-e_0$. They meet in the unit $e_0$ alone, and $-e_0$ is the one root of $+1$ that is not idempotent, since $(-e_0)^2 = e_0 \neq -e_0$. The roots of $-1$, the unit sphere of the vector subspace, have no counterpart in the idempotent set over $\mathbb{R}$: the bijection $\tilde\Xi \mapsto \tfrac12(e_0 + \tilde\Xi i)$ of the complexified block needs the central unit $i$, and without it the roots of $-1$ and the idempotents are two disjoint families. The root sets of the three central values are Square Roots of Minus One, Zero and Plus One in the General Plain Algebra, above this article in this category.

Idempotents and the Units

Proposition. Every idempotent of the plain product is either the zero element or a unit.

Proof. The idempotents are $0$ and $e_0$; the element $e_0$ is the unit and a nonzero element, hence invertible with $e_0^{-1} = e_0$.

This is a reading of the division property: an idempotent that is neither $0$ nor a unit would be a non-invertible nonzero element, and there is none. In the complexified block the same reading fails, the nontrivial idempotents being non-invertible and of vanishing norm.

Summary

An idempotent of the quaternion algebra is an element $\tilde\Pi$ with $\tilde\Pi^2 = \tilde\Pi$. Over the general plain product the idempotents are exactly $0$ and the unit $e_0$: the equation gives $\tilde\Pi(\tilde\Pi - e_0) = 0$, and the absence of zero divisors leaves only the two trivial solutions. There is no nontrivial idempotent, no orthogonal family of two nonzero idempotents, no complete orthogonal family but the singleton $\{e_0\}$, no primitive idempotent but $e_0$, and no nontrivial direct sum decomposition of the algebra; the Peirce decomposition is the trivial one, with Peirce spaces $\mathbb{H}$ and $\{0\}$ and no off-diagonal unit. No idempotent is a zero divisor, since $0$ is excluded from the zero divisors and $e_0$ is a unit, and the idempotents meet the roots of $+1$ in the unit $e_0$ alone.

This is the collapse of the complexified block, where the idempotents form a real four-dimensional manifold, are in bijection with the roots of $-1$, classify the non-pure zero divisors, and generate the minimal left ideals. The neighbouring classes are Zero Divisors of the General Plain Algebra, above this article in this category, and Nilpotents of the General Plain Algebra, below it: the zero-divisor set of the plain product is empty and the nilpotent class reduces to the origin.
