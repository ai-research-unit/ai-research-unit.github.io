The Closures of a Weight Vector: the Chain from $V_0$ to $V_3$

Introduction

The subject. The closure of a single weight vector under the copy is the smallest invariant subspace containing it. At order four a closure is one carrier, so the four closures are the four carriers, and there is no chain of strict inclusions between them; the only chain available is the chain of sums, of dimensions $1,4,9,16$.

The order of the article. The first section defines the closure and computes it for a weight vector. The second states the absence of a chain of inclusions. The third constructs the chain of sums. The fourth compares with the biquaternion family of closures indexed by the real axes. The fifth draws the boundary.

It assumes The Spin Decomposition of $M_4(\mathbb{C})$ for the weights and From the Spin to the Carriers and the Closures at $n=4$ for the generation of a carrier. The row at order two is The Closures of a Weight Vector.

The result in one line. The closure of a nonzero vector of weight $m$ in the carrier $V_k$ is $V_k$ itself, the four closures are independent, and the only chains are the sums of dimensions $1,4,9,16$.

Boundaries. The article treats closures under the copy; it does not treat the closure properties of the ideals, which are closed under left multiplication by the algebra.

The Closure of a Weight Vector

Definition. For a nonzero $X$ in the algebra, the closure is the span of the orbit $\{\Phi_q(X)\}$.

Proposition. If $X$ lies in $V_k$ and is nonzero, its closure is $V_k$, whatever its weight.

Proof. The carrier is irreducible, so the invariant subspace generated by $X$ is zero or all of $V_k$; it is nonzero because it contains $X$.

Corollary. The four weight vectors $E_{03},E_{02},E_{01},E_{00}$ have closures $V_3,V_2,V_1,V_0$, and no weight vector has a closure smaller than a carrier.

Proof. Each $E_{0j}$ is nonzero and lies in the carrier of its label.

Corollary. The dimension of the closure of a weight vector is $2k+1$ for a vector in $V_k$, and the weight of the vector does not enter.

Proof. The proposition; the weight determines the position in the string and not the string.

The reading. The closure forgets the weight and keeps the carrier. A weight vector and any other nonzero vector of the same carrier have the same closure.

No Chain of Inclusions

Proposition. The carriers are independent: $V_k\cap V_l=0$ for $k\neq l$, and no carrier is contained in another.

Proof. The carriers are the isotypic components of distinct labels, and isotypic components of distinct irreducible labels are disjoint; a containment of two nonzero carriers would give a nonzero intersection.

Corollary. There is no chain $V_0\subset V_1\subset V_2\subset V_3$ of strict inclusions among the closures.

Proof. The proposition.

Corollary. A chain of inclusions can only be built from sums: $V_0\subset V_0\oplus V_1\subset V_0\oplus V_1\oplus V_2\subset M_4(\mathbb{C})$.

Proof. The sums are nested by construction and the differences are carriers.

The reading. The temptation to order the four carriers by their labels is a temptation to confuse the label with a size: the label orders the strings, and the strings are independent.

The Chain of Sums

Proposition. The chain of sums has dimensions $1,4,9,16$, and each step adds a carrier of dimension $2k+1$.

Proof. $1$, $1+3=4$, $1+3+5=9$, $1+3+5+7=16$.

Corollary. The chain of sums is the chain of closures of the sets of the first $k+1$ top weight vectors, and its dimensions are the squares.

Proof. The closure of a finite set is the sum of the closures of its elements.

Corollary. The chain of sums is not a chain of closures of single vectors: a single vector closes to one carrier, and a carrier has dimension $1,3,5$ or $7$.

Proof. The closure theorem and the carrier dimensions.

The reading. The chain from $V_0$ to $V_3$ is a chain of sums and not of carriers; it is the only chain of invariant subspaces that the four labels allow.

The Biquaternion Family

Proposition. At order two the algebra is four-real-dimensional and its conjugation action gives the triplet and the scalars; the closures of nonzero vectors are the triplet or the scalars, and there are two of them.

Proof. $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{H}_{0}$ with $\mathbb{H}_0$ of dimension three; the conjugation action fixes the scalars and acts on $\mathbb{H}_0$ by the defining action, which is irreducible over the reals.

Corollary. The biquaternion closures do not form a family indexed by a continuous parameter: there are two closures, of dimensions $1$ and $3$.

Proof. The proposition.

Corollary. The family indexed by the real axes is a family of ideals and not of closures: the minimal left ideals of $\mathbb{B}$ are the lines of $\mathbb{C}^{2}$, parametrised by $\mathbb{P}^{1}$, which is the sphere of real axes.

Proof. The minimal left ideals are $M_2P$ for rank-one idempotents $P$, and the rank-one idempotents are parametrised by the lines of $\mathbb{C}^{2}$; the sphere of real axes is $\mathbb{P}^{1}$.

The reading. At order two the continuous family is the family of spinor lines, and at order four it is the family of minimal left ideals, parametrised by $\mathbb{P}^{3}$. The closures themselves are discrete in both rows.

The Boundary

Against the carriers article. That article proves the generation of each carrier from its top weight vector; this one states that no weight vector generates less than a carrier, and that the carriers are not nested.

Against the ideals article. The ideals are a continuous family and the closures are not; the two families must not be confused.

Against the general order. At order $n$ the closures are the $n$ carriers and the chain of sums has dimensions $1,4,\dots,n^{2}$.

The Orbits and Their Closures

The orbit of the highest weight vector. The highest weight vector of the module is a single vector, and its orbit under the copy is the set of unit vectors, a sphere of real dimension three; the closure of the orbit in the module is the whole module for a compact group, and in the projective space it is the whole projective space.

The orbit inside the carrier. The orbit of the highest weight vector of a carrier lies in that carrier, because the action preserves the isotypic components; its closure in the carrier is the orbit itself, since the group is compact.

The orbit closure in a nilpotent direction. For the vectors of an ideal the orbit closure is the ideal itself, because the unipotent radical of the parabolic acts transitively on the open part and the boundary is the origin.

The reading. The closures of the weight vectors are the carriers themselves, and the only proper closed invariant sets are the intersections of carriers with the ideals.

The Check of the Orbits

The dimensions. The orbit of a weight vector of weight $m$ is the set of vectors of length one in its weight space times the orbit of the phase, and its closure is the corresponding closed ball; the dimension of the orbit is the dimension of the sphere, and the closure adds the origin.

Summary

The closure of a nonzero vector of the algebra is the carrier that contains it, whatever the weight of the vector: the closure forgets the weight and keeps the label. The four closures are the four carriers, of dimensions $7,5,3,1$.

The carriers are independent and not nested, so there is no chain of strict inclusions among them. The only chain of invariant subspaces is the chain of sums, of dimensions $1,4,9,16=(k+1)^{2}$.

At order two there are two closures, of dimensions $1$ and $3$; the continuous family indexed by the real axes is the family of minimal left ideals and not of closures.
