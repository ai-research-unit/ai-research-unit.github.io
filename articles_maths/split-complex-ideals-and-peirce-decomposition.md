
# __Split-Complex Ideals and Peirce Decomposition__

## Introduction

This article treats the ideal theory of the split-complex algebra $\mathbb{D}$ and the Peirce decomposition attached to its idempotents. It is the two-dimensional counterpart of *Biquaternion Ideals and Peirce Decomposition*, and the contrast is complete: the biquaternion algebra $\mathbb{B}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$ is **simple**, with no nontrivial two-sided ideals, while $\mathbb{D}$ is a commutative product $\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}$ with two nontrivial two-sided ideals and a finite Boolean lattice of them.

The article records the definition of an ideal in a commutative algebra, determines the two-sided ideals, exhibits the two minimal ideals $\mathbb{R}\Pi_\pm$, writes the Peirce decomposition of $\mathbb{D}$ with respect to an idempotent, computes the lattice of ideals, and identifies the radical. The failure of simplicity is stated with its reason, and compared throughout with the simplicity of $\mathbb{B}$ over $\mathbb{C}$.

**Placement.** The article is third in the Algebra group, after *Split-Complex Algebra* and *Split-Complex Idempotents and Projections*, and before *Split-Complex Zero Divisors* and *Worked Examples in the Split-Complex Algebra*. It uses the idempotents of the second article; the norm and the invertibility criterion belong to *Split-Complex Norm and Invertibility* in the Topology group.

**Conventions.** The algebra is $\mathbb{D} = \mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2-1)$, with basis $1$, $j$, $j^2 = +1$, general element $A = a+ja'$, conjugate $\bar A = a-ja'$, idempotents $\Pi_\pm = \tfrac12(1\pm j)$ and idempotent coordinates $A = A_+\Pi_1 + A_-\Pi_2$. The base is the field $\mathbb{R}$; the algebra is commutative, so no distinction between left, right and two-sided ideals arises for the ambient ring.

## Ideals in an Algebra

**Definition.** A subset $I \subseteq \mathbb{D}$ is an **ideal** if

$$
I \text{ is an additive subgroup}, \qquad B \in I \implies AB \in I \ \text{and} \ BA \in I.
$$

Since $\mathbb{D}$ is commutative, the two conditions coincide, and every ideal is two-sided; there is no separate theory of left and right ideals. The ideals $\{0\}$ and $\mathbb{D}$ are the **trivial** ideals; an ideal is **proper** if it is neither, and **maximal** if it is proper and contained in no larger proper ideal, **minimal** if it is nonzero and contains no smaller nonzero ideal.

A **principal** ideal is one generated by a single element, written $(A) = A\mathbb{D} = \mathbb{D}A$. The lattice of all ideals, ordered by inclusion, is denoted $\mathrm{Id}(\mathbb{D})$, with meet $I\cap J$ and join $I+J$.

Since every ideal is closed under multiplication by $\Pi_1$ and by $\Pi_2$, and $A = A\Pi_1 + A\Pi_2$, every ideal decomposes along the idempotent splitting; this is the mechanism behind the computation below.

## The Two-Sided Ideals

**Theorem.** The ideals of $\mathbb{D}$ are exactly the four sets

$$
\{0\}, \qquad \mathbb{R}\Pi_1 = \mathbb{D}\Pi_1, \qquad \mathbb{R}\Pi_2 = \mathbb{D}\Pi_2, \qquad \mathbb{D}.
$$

**Proof.** Each of the three nontrivial sets listed is an ideal: $\mathbb{R}\Pi_1$ is closed under addition, and $A(\lambda \Pi_1) = \lambda A \Pi_1 = \lambda A_+ \Pi_1 \in \mathbb{R}\Pi_1$ for every $A$, so it is an ideal; the same argument gives $\mathbb{R}\Pi_2$; and $\mathbb{D}$ is trivially an ideal. Conversely, let $I \neq \{0\}$ be an ideal and choose $A \in I$ nonzero. If both idempotent coordinates of $A$ are nonzero then $A$ is a unit by *Split-Complex Algebra*, so $1 = A^{-1}A \in I$ and $I = \mathbb{D}$. Otherwise one coordinate of $A$ vanishes, and $A$ is a nonzero multiple of $\Pi_1$ or of $\Pi_2$ by the idempotent classification; say $A = \lambda \Pi_1$ with $\lambda \neq 0$, so that $\Pi_1 = \lambda^{-1}A \in I$. Now take any $B \in I$. If there is such a $B$ with $B_- \neq 0$, then $B\Pi_2 = B_-\Pi_2 \in I$, so $\Pi_2 = B_-^{-1}B\Pi_2 \in I$, and then $1 = \Pi_1 + \Pi_2 \in I$, giving $I = \mathbb{D}$. If on the other hand every $B \in I$ has $B_- = 0$, then $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}\Pi_1$; since $I \neq 0$ and $\Pi_1 \in I$, we get $I = \mathbb{R}\Pi_1$. The case $A = \lambda \Pi_2$ is the same with the roles of $+$ and $-$ exchanged and gives $I = \mathbb{R}\Pi_2$ or $I = \mathbb{D}$. So the list is complete.

**Corollary (failure of simplicity).** $\mathbb{D}$ is **not simple**: it has exactly two nontrivial proper ideals, both minimal and both maximal. The algebra is the direct product of the two fields

$$
\mathbb{D} \cong \mathbb{R}\Pi_1 \times \mathbb{R}\Pi_2, \qquad \mathbb{R}\Pi_1 \cong \mathbb{R}, \quad \mathbb{R}\Pi_2 \cong \mathbb{R},
$$

and each of the two ideals is a field in its own right.

**The reason.** The algebra is commutative and generated by an element $j$ satisfying the reducible polynomial $x^2 - 1 = (x-1)(x+1)$; the generator therefore has two distinct eigenvalues $+1$ and $-1$ already in the base field, and the algebra splits as the product of the corresponding eigencomponents. In the biquaternion algebra the situation is different: $\mathbb{B}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$ has no nontrivial two-sided ideal at all, because its complex dimension $4$ is a square and $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ is a full matrix algebra over a field, hence simple. Simplicity of $\mathbb{B}$ and non-simplicity of $\mathbb{D}$ are thus the two ends of the same phenomenon: the split-complex algebra is the commutative, reducible, low-dimensional member of the family, and the biquaternion algebra is its simple matrix quotient in four dimensions.

## Artinian, Semisimple, Length Two

Since $\mathbb{D}$ is finite-dimensional over the field $\mathbb{R}$, it is **Artinian** as a ring: every descending chain of ideals stabilises. It is also **Noetherian** for the same reason.

**Proposition.** $\mathbb{D}$ is **semisimple** in the sense of having zero Jacobson radical, and it is the product of the two simple algebras $\mathbb{R}\Pi_1$ and $\mathbb{R}\Pi_2$.

**Proof.** The Jacobson radical of a product of fields is zero, since a field has no nonzero nilpotent elements and is its own maximal ideal; and $\mathbb{D}\cong\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$ via the idempotent decomposition.

**Proposition (length).** As a module over itself, $\mathbb{D}$ has **length two**, with composition series

$$
\{0\} \subset \mathbb{R}\Pi_1 \subset \mathbb{D}, \qquad \mathbb{R}\Pi_1 \cong \mathbb{R}, \qquad \mathbb{D}/\mathbb{R}\Pi_1 \cong \mathbb{R}\Pi_2 \cong \mathbb{R}.
$$

**Proof.** The inclusion is proper at each step, $\mathbb{R}\Pi_1$ is simple as a module because it is a field summand, and the quotient $\mathbb{D}/\mathbb{R}\Pi_1$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}\Pi_2$ through $A \mapsto A\Pi_2$, again simple. No shorter or longer chain is possible because the dimension is $2$ and every nonzero proper ideal is one-dimensional.

So $\mathbb{D}$ has the same module length as a two-dimensional vector space, in contrast with $\mathbb{B}$, whose regular module has length two with simple constituents of complex dimension two. Both algebras are semisimple of length two; they differ in that the constituents of $\mathbb{D}$ are one-dimensional real, in keeping with its smaller dimension.

## Idempotents and the Peirce Decomposition

Let $e$ be an idempotent of $\mathbb{D}$ and write $f = 1-e$ for its complement. Multiplication by $e$ and by $f$ splits the algebra into four additive subgroups,

$$
\mathbb{D} = \mathbb{D}_{11}\oplus\mathbb{D}_{12}\oplus\mathbb{D}_{21}\oplus\mathbb{D}_{22},
$$

where

$$
\mathbb{D}_{11} = e\mathbb{D}e, \qquad \mathbb{D}_{12} = e\mathbb{D}f, \qquad \mathbb{D}_{21} = f\mathbb{D}e, \qquad \mathbb{D}_{22} = f\mathbb{D}f.
$$

This is the **Peirce decomposition** of $\mathbb{D}$ with respect to $e$. It is the refinement of the two-term decomposition $\mathbb{D} = \mathbb{D}e\oplus\mathbb{D}f$: the summand $\mathbb{D}e$ is $\mathbb{D}_{11}\oplus\mathbb{D}_{21}$ and the summand $\mathbb{D}f$ is $\mathbb{D}_{12}\oplus\mathbb{D}_{22}$.

Take $e = \Pi_1$, $f = \Pi_2$, the standard idempotent. Then

$$
\Pi_1\mathbb{D}\Pi_1 = \mathbb{R}\Pi_1, \qquad \Pi_2\mathbb{D}\Pi_2 = \mathbb{R}\Pi_2, \qquad \Pi_1\mathbb{D}\Pi_2 = 0, \qquad \Pi_2\mathbb{D}\Pi_1 = 0.
$$

The two **off-diagonal** Peirce components vanish, because $\Pi_1\Pi_2 = 0$ annihilates every product alternating between the two idempotents, and the decomposition collapses to

$$
\mathbb{D} = \mathbb{D}_{11}\oplus\mathbb{D}_{22} = \mathbb{R}\Pi_1 \oplus \mathbb{R}\Pi_2,
$$

of total dimension $1+1+0+0 = 2$. With respect to the idempotent $0$ or $1$ the Peirce decomposition is trivial: for $e = 1$ the only nonzero component is the whole algebra, and for $e = 0$ it is the zero ideal.

**Definition.** The Peirce components are the four blocks of the multiplication table of $\mathbb{D}$ under the splitting $1 = \Pi_1 + \Pi_2$; the vanishing of the off-diagonal blocks is the statement that the splitting is a direct product decomposition.

### The Idempotents as Matrix Units

In the idempotent basis $\{\Pi_1, \Pi_2\}$ the standard idempotents are the two diagonal matrix units

$$
\Pi_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Pi_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
$$

acting on the column $\begin{pmatrix} A_+ \\ A_- \end{pmatrix}$ of idempotent coordinates, and they satisfy the matrix-unit relations

$$
\Pi_1^2 = \Pi_1, \qquad \Pi_2^2 = \Pi_2, \qquad \Pi_1\Pi_2 = \Pi_2\Pi_1 = 0, \qquad \Pi_1 + \Pi_2 = I.
$$

These are the defining relations of the diagonal matrix units of a $2\times 2$ matrix algebra. Because $\mathbb{D}$ is commutative, the two off-diagonal units $E_{12}$ and $E_{21}$ are absent, and the algebra is not a full matrix algebra; the four Peirce components are the four blocks of this basis, and the vanishing of the off-diagonal blocks is exactly the statement that the matrix picture is diagonal. This is the structural contrast with $\mathbb{B}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$, whose idempotents are general rank-one projections with off-diagonal units present.

## The Lattice of Ideals

The four ideals of $\mathbb{D}$ are $\{0\}$, $\mathbb{R}\Pi_1$, $\mathbb{R}\Pi_2$, $\mathbb{D}$, with the inclusions

$$
\{0\} \subset \mathbb{R}\Pi_1 \subset \mathbb{D}, \qquad \{0\} \subset \mathbb{R}\Pi_2 \subset \mathbb{D},
$$

and no inclusion between $\mathbb{R}\Pi_1$ and $\mathbb{R}\Pi_2$ (they are independent, and their intersection is $\{0\}$).

| meet | value | join | value |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{R}\Pi_1 \cap \mathbb{R}\Pi_2$ | $\{0\}$ | $\mathbb{R}\Pi_1 + \mathbb{R}\Pi_2$ | $\mathbb{D}$ |
| $\mathbb{R}\Pi_1 \cap \mathbb{D}$ | $\mathbb{R}\Pi_1$ | $\mathbb{R}\Pi_1 + \mathbb{D}$ | $\mathbb{D}$ |
| $\{0\} \cap I$ | $\{0\}$ | $\{0\} + I$ | $I$ |

**Theorem.** The lattice $\mathrm{Id}(\mathbb{D})$ is a **Boolean lattice** with four elements, isomorphic to the power set of a two-point set:

$$
\mathrm{Id}(\mathbb{D}) \cong \mathcal{P}(\{+,-\}), \qquad \{0\} \leftrightarrow \varnothing, \quad \mathbb{R}\Pi_1 \leftrightarrow \{+\}, \quad \mathbb{R}\Pi_2 \leftrightarrow \{-\}, \quad \mathbb{D} \leftrightarrow \{+,-\}.
$$

**Proof.** The map is the ideal generated by the indicator subset under the isomorphism $\mathbb{D}\cong\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}$: the ideal of $\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}$ generated by the indicator vector of a subset $S$ is the set of pairs supported on $S$, and these four sets are exactly the ideals. Under this identification the complement of the ideal associated with $S$ is the ideal associated with the complementary subset $S^{\mathrm{c}}$, meets are intersections and joins are unions, so the lattice is the power-set lattice $\mathcal{P}(\{+,-\})$, which is Boolean.

The lattice is finite, distributive and complemented. This is a further contrast with $\mathbb{B}$, whose two-sided ideal lattice is the two-element chain $\{0\}\subset\mathbb{B}$ (simplicity), while its one-sided ideal lattice is a projective line over $\mathbb{C}$; in $\mathbb{D}$ the left, right and two-sided ideals coincide, so there is a single finite lattice, and it is the Boolean one above.

## The Radical

**Proposition.** The Jacobson radical, the nilradical and the prime radical of $\mathbb{D}$ all vanish.

**Proof.** The nilradical is the set of nilpotent elements. If $A^n = 0$, write $A = A_+\Pi_1 + A_-\Pi_2$; then $A^n = A_+^n\Pi_1 + A_-^n\Pi_2 = 0$, so $A_+^n = 0$ and $A_-^n = 0$ in $\mathbb{R}$, whence $A_+ = A_- = 0$ and $A = 0$. Hence the nilradical is $\{0\}$. The Jacobson radical of the product of fields $\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$ is zero, since the maximal ideals are the two coordinate ideals and their intersection is zero.

So $\mathbb{D}$ is **reduced** (no nonzero nilpotent) and **semiprime**. This is the opposite of the dual-number algebra $\mathbb{D}' = \mathbb{R}[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$, whose maximal ideal is nonzero and nilpotent, and it is the reason $\mathbb{D}$ is much closer in behaviour to a product of fields than to a local ring.

## The Real Structure and Comparison with $\mathbb{B}$

The algebra $\mathbb{D}$ is defined over $\mathbb{R}$, and its two minimal ideals are defined over $\mathbb{R}$: each is the real span of the corresponding idempotent, and the unique non-trivial automorphism of $\mathbb{D}$, the conjugation $\bar{\cdot}$, exchanges them,

$$
\overline{\Pi_1} = \Pi_2, \qquad \overline{\Pi_2} = \Pi_1,
$$

while fixing neither. So the pair of ideals is a single Galois orbit under the conjugation, and the algebra carries no proper $\mathbb{R}$-subalgebra splitting it into one ideal only. In the biquaternion algebra the analogous real structure is the complex conjugation fixing the quaternion subspace; here it is the exchange of the two components.

| feature | $\mathbb{B} \cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$ | $\mathbb{D} \cong \mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}$ |
|---|---|---|
| simple? | yes | no |
| two-sided ideals | $\{0\}, \mathbb{B}$ | $\{0\}, \mathbb{R}\Pi_1, \mathbb{R}\Pi_2, \mathbb{D}$ |
| lattice | two-element chain | four-element Boolean lattice |
| one-sided ideals | projective line over $\mathbb{C}$ | the same four ideals |
| radical | $0$ | $0$ |
| length of the regular module | $2$ | $2$ |
| reason for (non-)simplicity | full matrix algebra over a field | product of two copies of $\mathbb{R}$ |

The failure of simplicity in $\mathbb{D}$ is therefore not a defect but the algebraic expression of its defining decomposition: the two minimal ideals are the two summands of $\mathbb{D}\cong\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}$, and the four-element Boolean lattice is the lattice of subsets of that two-point spectrum.

## Summary

An ideal of the commutative algebra $\mathbb{D}$ is a two-sided ideal, and there are exactly four: $\{0\}$, the two minimal ones $\mathbb{R}\Pi_1$ and $\mathbb{R}\Pi_2$, and $\mathbb{D}$. The two minimal ideals are the two summands of the direct product $\mathbb{D}\cong\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$, and the lattice of ideals is the four-element Boolean lattice of subsets of $\{+,-\}$. The algebra is Artinian, semisimple and reduced, of module length two, with zero Jacobson radical and no nonzero nilpotent.

With respect to an idempotent $e$, the Peirce decomposition splits $\mathbb{D}$ into the four blocks $e\mathbb{D}e$, $e\mathbb{D}f$, $f\mathbb{D}e$, $f\mathbb{D}f$; for the standard idempotent $\Pi_1$ the two off-diagonal blocks vanish and the decomposition collapses to $\mathbb{D} = \mathbb{R}\Pi_1\oplus\mathbb{R}\Pi_2$. The idempotents $\Pi_\pm$ are central and orthogonal, and no element connects the two components, because the algebra is commutative. This is the failure of simplicity of $\mathbb{D}$, against the simplicity of the biquaternion algebra $\mathbb{B}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$ over $\mathbb{C}$; the reason is that $\mathbb{D}$ is generated by an element with two distinct eigenvalues in its base field, so it splits as a product of two copies of $\mathbb{R}$, while $\mathbb{B}$ is a full matrix algebra and has no nontrivial two-sided ideal.
