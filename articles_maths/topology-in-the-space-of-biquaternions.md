# __Topology in the Space of Biquaternions__

## Introduction

This article builds the topology of the space of biquaternions, and its only input is the linear structure of that space. The space is a real vector space of dimension eight; a **norm** is defined on it; because the dimension is finite, all its norms are equivalent, so the space carries exactly **one topological norm** and **one topology**; that topology is then built explicitly and it is the **Euclidean topology of $\mathbb{R}^{8}$**; and in it the product of biquaternions is continuous, so that the space is a topological algebra as well as a topological vector space. The article uses the linear structure and the product, and nothing else.

## Notation

The algebra is $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$, with units $e_t=1,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z$ satisfying $\vec e_k^{2}=-e_t$ and $\vec e_x\vec e_y=\vec e_z$, $\vec e_y\vec e_z=\vec e_x$, $\vec e_z\vec e_x=\vec e_y$. The central imaginary is written $i$, so that it does not collide with the quaternion units. A general element and its real coordinates are

$$
\tilde{Q}=Q_te_t+Q_x\vec e_x+Q_y\vec e_y+Q_z\vec e_z,\qquad Q_\mu=q_\mu+iq'_\mu,\qquad q_\mu,q'_\mu\in\mathbb{R},
$$

so that, as a real vector space,

$$
\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{R}\text{-span}\{e_t,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z,ie_t,i\vec e_x,i\vec e_y,i\vec e_z\},\qquad \dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{B}=8,
$$

with $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde{Q})=Q_t$ the scalar part and the product of the algebra as in *The Realification and the Effect of the Change of Scalars*.

## 1. The Norm

**Definition (norm).** Let $V$ be a real vector space. A **norm** on $V$ is a function $\lVert\cdot\rVert:V\to[0,\infty)$ such that

1. $\lVert x\rVert=0$ if and only if $x=0$ (**definiteness**);
2. $\lVert\lambda x\rVert=\lvert\lambda\rvert\,\lVert x\rVert$ for every $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$ and every $x\in V$ (**homogeneity**);
3. $\lVert x+y\rVert\leq\lVert x\rVert+\lVert y\rVert$ for all $x,y\in V$ (**triangle inequality**).

A pair $(V,\lVert\cdot\rVert)$ is a **normed space**, the function $d(x,y)=\lVert x-y\rVert$ is a metric on it, and the topology generated by that metric is the **norm topology**.

**Definition (the norm of the space).** On $\mathbb{B}$ define

$$
\lVert\tilde{Q}\rVert=\Bigl(\sum_{\mu\in\{t,x,y,z\}}\lvert Q_\mu\rvert^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}
=\Bigl(\sum_{\mu\in\{t,x,y,z\}}\bigl(q_\mu^{2}+(q'_\mu)^{2}\bigr)\Bigr)^{1/2}.
$$

**Proposition.** $\lVert\cdot\rVert$ is a norm on $\mathbb{B}$.

**Proof.** *Definiteness*: a sum of squares vanishes only when every $Q_\mu=0$, and then $\tilde{Q}=0$. *Homogeneity*: $\lvert\lambda Q_\mu\rvert=\lvert\lambda\rvert\,\lvert Q_\mu\rvert$ for real $\lambda$. *Triangle inequality*: the coordinates $Q_\mu\mapsto(q_\mu,q'_\mu)$ identify $\mathbb{B}$ with $\mathbb{R}^{8}$, the expression is the Euclidean norm of that space, and the triangle inequality there is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.

## 2. The Topological Norm: Equivalence and Uniqueness

**Definition (equivalent norms).** Two norms $\lVert\cdot\rVert_1$ and $\lVert\cdot\rVert_2$ on $V$ are **equivalent** when there are constants $c,C>0$ with

$$
c\,\lVert x\rVert_1\leq\lVert x\rVert_2\leq C\,\lVert x\rVert_1\qquad\text{for every }x\in V .
$$

Equivalent norms have the same open sets and therefore generate the same topology. The common class of a norm is called here its **topological norm**.

**Theorem (finite dimension).** Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional real vector space. Then

1. **every two norms on $V$ are equivalent**, and
2. **$V$ carries exactly one Hausdorff topology compatible with its vector-space structure**, namely the topology induced by any one of its norms.

Statement 1 is the theorem of the equivalence of norms, and statement 2 the theorem that every finite-dimensional Hausdorff topological vector space is linearly homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^{n}$. Both are in *Normed and Banach Spaces* and *Comparison of Norms and Invertibility*, and in the standard references (Rudin, *Functional Analysis*, theorems 1.21 and 1.22; Bourbaki, *Topological Vector Spaces*, chapter I).

**Corollary.** The space of biquaternions carries exactly one topological norm and exactly one topology. The choice of a norm is a matter of convenience and never a topological choice: the Euclidean norm of §1, the maximum norm $\max_\mu\lvert Q_\mu\rvert$, the sum norm $\sum_\mu(\lvert q_\mu\rvert+\lvert q'_\mu\rvert)$ and every other norm of $\mathbb{R}^{8}$ generate that one topology.

## 3. The Topology

**Definition (the topology of $\mathbb{B}$).** On $\mathbb{B}$ take the norm of §1, the metric $d(\tilde{Q}_1,\tilde{Q}_2)=\lVert\tilde{Q}_1-\tilde{Q}_2\rVert$, and the topology generated by the open balls

$$
B(\tilde{Q}_1,r)=\bigl\{\tilde{Q}_2\in\mathbb{B}:\lVert\tilde{Q}_2-\tilde{Q}_1\rVert<r\bigr\},\qquad r>0 .
$$

**Theorem.** The topology of $\mathbb{B}$ is the Euclidean topology of $\mathbb{R}^{8}$ under the coordinate isomorphism $\tilde{Q}\mapsto(q_t,q'_t,q_x,q'_x,q_y,q'_y,q_z,q'_z)$, and by §2 it is the only topology the space carries.

It is Euclidean in the precise sense that this coordinate isomorphism is a linear bijection carrying the norm of §1 to the Euclidean norm of $\mathbb{R}^{8}$ and the balls $B(\tilde{Q}_1,r)$ to the Euclidean balls, so the open sets are those of the usual metric topology of $\mathbb{R}^{8}$ and of nothing else.

The topology is the usual one of a finite-dimensional real space: Hausdorff, second countable, locally compact, complete, path connected, and with the Heine–Borel property that a subset is compact exactly when it is closed and bounded (*Metric, Uniform and Complete Spaces*). It is also the topology inherited from any faithful finite-dimensional real representation: the regular representation gives a linear injection $\mathbb{B}\hookrightarrow M_4(\mathbb{R})$ (*The Realification and the Effect of the Change of Scalars*), and the topology defined above is the subspace topology of the operator norm there, by §2.

The norm of §1 is the topological norm: it is the norm that defines the topology of §3, and by §2 every other norm of the space gives the same topology.

## 4. The Algebra Structure

**Proposition (the product is bounded).** For all $\tilde{Q}_1,\tilde{Q}_2\in\mathbb{B}$,

$$
\boxed{\; \lVert\tilde{Q}_1\tilde{Q}_2\rVert\leq\sqrt{2}\,\lVert\tilde{Q}_1\rVert\,\lVert\tilde{Q}_2\rVert \;}.
$$

**Proof.** The estimate is the sharp normed-algebra inequality of *The Euclidean Topology of the Biquaternion Algebra*, §*The Algebra as a Normed Algebra*, where it is proved from $\lVert\mathsf{M}_2(\tilde{Q})\rVert_F=\sqrt{2}\,\lVert\tilde{Q}\rVert$ and the submultiplicativity of the Frobenius norm.

**Corollary.** The product is continuous, and addition and scalar multiplication are continuous, so $\mathbb{B}$ is a **topological algebra**. The rescaled norm $\sqrt2\,\lVert\cdot\rVert$ is submultiplicative, so with that norm $\mathbb{B}$ is a **normed algebra**, and being finite-dimensional it is complete, hence a **Banach algebra** (*Topological Algebras and Banach Algebras*). The group $\mathbb{B}^{\times}$ of invertible elements is open in the topology and inversion is continuous on it.

## 5. Summary

The space of biquaternions is a real vector space of dimension eight. It carries a norm (§1), all its norms are equivalent and it carries exactly one topological norm and one topology (§2), that topology is built in §3 and is the Euclidean topology of $\mathbb{R}^{8}$, and in it the product is continuous, so the space is a topological, normed and Banach algebra with an open group of units (§4). One norm, one topological norm, one topology, and it is the Euclidean one.
