# __The Regular Module__

## Introduction

The algebra acts on itself by left and right multiplication. This article reads that action as a module, identifies its submodules with the ideals, and states the structure of the algebra as a module over itself: simple as an algebra, of length two as a left module, with zero radical.

The article assumes the Peirce frame of *The Peirce Decomposition* and the count of the zero divisors of *Zero Divisors and Units*. It is the first of the three module articles; the minimal left ideals are the second and the category of modules is the third.

The distinction on which the article turns is the one between the two-sided and the one-sided reading. A two-sided ideal is a sub-bimodule, and the algebra has only the two trivial ones. A left ideal is a left submodule, and the algebra has many; the minimal ones are the spinor spaces of the next article.

## Ideals: the Recall

**The ideals are defined before the frame, in *The Peirce Decomposition*, §*Ideals*; this article reads them as submodules.**

**Recall.** The left, right and two-sided ideals, the trivial, proper, minimal and maximal ideals, the ideal generated by a set, and the simplicity of an algebra are defined in *The Peirce Decomposition*, §*Ideals*. The definitions are not repeated here. What this article adds is the identification of these subspaces with the submodules of the regular module, and the structure of the resulting lattices.

A base-field remark will be used repeatedly. If $A I \subseteq I$, then $I$ is automatically a $k$-subspace, since $\lambda \tilde Q = (\lambda 1) \tilde Q \in I$ for $\lambda \in k$, $\tilde Q \in I$, as $\lambda 1 \in A$. So the left ideals of a unital algebra do not depend on which field of scalars inside the centre is used to view it; in particular the ideal lattice of $\mathbb{B}$ is the same in the $\mathbb{C}$-view and the $\mathbb{R}$-view (§*The Real Structure*).

For a two-sided ideal $I$, the **quotient algebra** $A/I$ is the set of cosets with the induced operations, well defined precisely because $I$ absorbs multiplication on both sides. Kernels of algebra homomorphisms are two-sided ideals, and the first isomorphism theorem gives $A/\ker\varphi \cong \operatorname{im}\varphi$. For a left ideal only, $A/I$ is still a left $A$-module but not in general an algebra. Thus the left ideals govern module theory and the two-sided ideals govern quotient algebras.

## The Two-Sided Ideals

**The only two-sided ideals are $0$ and $\mathbb{B}$: the algebra is simple and has no proper nonzero two-sided ideal.**

**Theorem.** Over $\mathbb{C}$, the only two-sided ideals of $\mathbb{B}$ are $0$ and $\mathbb{B}$. Equivalently, $\mathbb{B}$ is a **simple** $\mathbb{C}$-algebra.

| Ideal | Generator | Dimension over $\mathbb{C}$ | Minimal | Maximal | Quotient |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $0$ | none | $0$ | no | yes | $\mathbb{B}$ |
| $\mathbb{B}$ | $e_t$ | $4$ | yes | no | $0$ |

The simplicity is the absence of a third row. It makes the two ideals exchange the two roles: $0$ is the unique maximal two-sided ideal, since it is proper and the only ideal containing it is $\mathbb{B}$, and $\mathbb{B}$ is the unique minimal one, since it is nonzero and contains no nonzero proper ideal. Minimality is meant among nonzero ideals, so $0$ is not minimal.

**Proof.** Let $I \neq 0$ be a two-sided ideal. It is a $\mathbb{C}$-subspace, because multiplication by the central element $i$ is left multiplication by an element of $\mathbb{B}$. Fix $0 \neq \tilde{Q} = \sum_\mu Q_\mu e_\mu \in I$. For every unit $\tilde Q_3 \in \mathbb{B}$ the conjugate $\tilde Q_3\tilde{Q}\tilde Q_3^{-1}$ lies in $I$, since $I$ is two-sided.

Average the conjugates over the finite group $\{\pm e_t, \pm \vec e_x, \pm \vec e_y, \pm \vec e_z\}$. For $\mu = 1, 2, 3$, conjugation by $e_\mu$ fixes $e_t$ and $e_\mu$ and reverses the other two imaginary units, while conjugation by $e_t$ is the identity; in each case $e_\mu$ and $-e_\mu$ give the same conjugate, and the sum over the four representatives $e_t, \vec e_x, \vec e_y, \vec e_z$ is

$$
\tilde Q + \big(Q_t e_t + Q_x \vec e_x - Q_y \vec e_y - Q_z \vec e_z\big) + \big(Q_t e_t - Q_x \vec e_x + Q_y \vec e_y - Q_z \vec e_z\big) + \big(Q_t e_t - Q_x \vec e_x - Q_y \vec e_y + Q_z \vec e_z\big) = 4 Q_t e_t,
$$

the sum over all eight is twice this, and the average is that sum divided by $8$, giving $Q_t e_t \in I$. If $Q_t \neq 0$ then $e_t = Q_t^{-1}(Q_t e_t) \in I$ and $I = \mathbb{B}$. If $Q_t = 0$ then $\tilde{Q}$ is nonzero and pure, so some coefficient $Q_\mu \neq 0$ with $\mu \in \{1,2,3\}$. Since $I$ is a two-sided ideal, the product $\tilde{Q} \vec e_\mu$ lies in $I$, and its scalar part is $-Q_k \neq 0$; the averaging applied to $\tilde{Q} \vec e_\mu$ then gives $-Q_k e_t \in I$, so $e_t \in I$ and again $I = \mathbb{B}$.

Consequences over $\mathbb{C}$:

- The only quotient algebras of $\mathbb{B}$ are $\mathbb{B}$ and $0$.
- Every nonzero element generates $\mathbb{B}$ as a two-sided ideal.
- The centre of $\mathbb{B}$ is the scalar copy of $\mathbb{C}$, so $\mathbb{B}$ is a **central simple** $\mathbb{C}$-algebra; in particular it is not a product $A_1 \times A_2$ of nonzero algebras, since each factor would give a nontrivial two-sided ideal.
- The many one-sided ideals of *The Minimal Left Ideals*, §*The Decomposition into Minimal Left and Right Ideals*, and of *The Category of Modules*, §*The Lattice of Left Ideals*, are all non-two-sided, so they do not contradict simplicity.

## Artinian, Semisimple, and Length Two

**The algebra is Artinian, semisimple, simple, and of module length two; its radical is zero.**

| Property | Result |
|---|---|
| Artinian | yes, the complex dimension is finite |
| Noetherian | yes, the complex dimension is finite |
| Semisimple | yes, $J(\mathbb{B})=0$ |
| Simple | yes |
| Length as a left module over itself | $2$ |
| Simple modules | one, $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi$ |
| Radical | $0$ |
| Central simple over $\mathbb{C}$ | yes |

A nonzero module is **simple** if it has no submodules other than $0$ and itself. A **composition series** is a finite strictly increasing chain $0 = M_0 \subset M_1 \subset \cdots \subset M_r = M$ with simple successive quotients $M_i/M_{i-1}$; the number $r$ is the **length** of $M$. A ring is **left artinian** if every descending chain of left ideals stabilizes, and **semisimple** if its left regular module is a direct sum of simple modules.

Over $\mathbb{C}$, every descending chain of left ideals of $\mathbb{B}$ is a descending chain of $\mathbb{C}$-subspaces, so it stabilizes; thus $\mathbb{B}$ is artinian. By Wedderburn–Artin, a unital ring is semisimple if and only if it is artinian with zero Jacobson radical, and every simple artinian ring is semisimple. Hence $\mathbb{B}$ is **semisimple**, and every left ideal is a direct sum of minimal left ideals.

All simple left $\mathbb{B}$-modules are isomorphic, since a simple artinian ring has a unique simple module up to isomorphism. With the idempotents $\tilde\Pi_+, \tilde\Pi_-$ of §*Idempotents and Orthogonal Idempotents*, the algebra as a left module over itself reads $\mathbb{B} = \mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+ \oplus \mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$, with $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+$ and $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_-$ both minimal left ideals. Each of the two minimal left ideals is a complex plane. Hence $0 \subset \mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+ \subset \mathbb{B}$ is a composition series, and the **length of $\mathbb{B}$ as a left module over itself is $2$**, with both factors isomorphic. The right regular module has length $2$ as well. All of this is over $\mathbb{C}$.

## The Radical

**The radical of the algebra is $0$; the algebra is semisimple and has no nonzero nilpotent two-sided ideal.**

The **Jacobson radical** $J(A)$ is the intersection of all maximal left ideals of $A$, equivalently of all maximal right ideals; it is a two-sided ideal. For an artinian ring, $J(A)$ is the largest nilpotent ideal and $A/J(A)$ is semisimple; an artinian ring is semisimple if and only if its radical is zero.

Over $\mathbb{C}$, the radical of $\mathbb{B}$ vanishes:

$$
J(\mathbb{B}) = 0.
$$

| Radical | Value |
|---|---|
| Jacobson radical | $0$ |
| Nilradical | $0$ |
| Prime radical | $0$ |

Indeed $J(\mathbb{B})$ is a two-sided ideal, hence by simplicity is $0$ or $\mathbb{B}$; it cannot be $\mathbb{B}$, since in a unital algebra the radical is proper. This restates the semisimplicity of §*Artinian, Semisimple, and Length Two* in terms of the radical. Consequences over $\mathbb{C}$:

- $\mathbb{B}$ has no nonzero **nilpotent two-sided ideals**; the nilradical is zero.
- It has no nonzero nilpotent left or right ideals either, since such an ideal generates a nonzero nilpotent two-sided ideal.
- The absence of a radical is not the absence of nilpotent **elements**: $\vec\Upsilon_x^2 = \vec\Upsilon_y^2 = 0$ (§*The Biquaternion Case*) — nilpotents of the biquaternion layer, written $\vec\Upsilon_x$ and $\vec\Upsilon_y$ for the two particular units and $\tilde\Upsilon$ where the class is what is meant (§*The Nilpotent Convention* of *Conventions in Mathematics*) — yet the left ideal generated by $\vec\Upsilon_x$ is not nilpotent.
- Every left $\mathbb{B}$-module is semisimple.

## The Three Regular Modules

### The left regular module

**Definition.** The **left regular module** ${}_{\mathbb{B}}\mathbb{B}$ is the additive group $(\mathbb{B},+)$ with the scalar multiplication

$$
\mathbb{B}\times\mathbb{B}\longrightarrow\mathbb{B},\qquad (\tilde Q_1,\tilde Q_2)\mapsto \tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2 ,
$$

the multiplication of the algebra itself.

**Proposition (the module axioms are the ring axioms).** ${}_{\mathbb{B}}\mathbb{B}$ is a left $\mathbb{B}$-module. The four module axioms are the two distributive laws, the associativity and the unit law of $\mathbb{B}$, each read as a module axiom, and the derived identities $0\tilde Q_2=0$ and $(-\tilde Q_1)\tilde Q_2=-(\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2)$ hold because they already hold in $\mathbb{B}$. In particular $\mathbb{B}$ is a left $\mathbb{B}$-module of complex dimension four, and the same carrier with the same multiplication is a left module over $\mathbb{B}$, not a new object built from it.

**Proof.** The distributivity of the action in the scalar and in the vector is the two distributive laws of $\mathbb{B}$, the compatibility $(\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2)\tilde Q_3=\tilde Q_1(\tilde Q_2\tilde Q_3)$ is the associativity, and $e_t\tilde Q_3=\tilde Q_3$ is the unit law. The derived identities are the elementary properties of *Modules*.

### The right regular module

**Definition.** The **right regular module** $\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{B}}$ is $(\mathbb{B},+)$ with the right scalar multiplication $(\tilde Q_2,\tilde Q_1)\mapsto \tilde Q_2\tilde Q_1$, which is the same multiplication read on the right.

### The regular bimodule

**Definition.** The left action and the right action of $\mathbb{B}$ on $(\mathbb{B},+)$, taken together, make it a **bimodule** ${}_{\mathbb{B}}\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{B}}$. The two actions commute,

$$
\tilde Q_1(\tilde Q\tilde Q_4)=(\tilde Q_1\tilde Q)\tilde Q_4 \qquad (\tilde Q_1,\tilde Q_4,\tilde Q\in\mathbb{B}),
$$

which is the associativity of $\mathbb{B}$ once more: the two scalar multiplications of the bimodule are the two readings of one product.

The left regular module is **cyclic**, generated by $e_t$, since $\tilde Q=\tilde Qe_t$, and **faithful**, since $\tilde Qe_t=0$ forces $\tilde Q=0$; the right regular module is the same with the multiplication read on the right.

**Remark (why the regular module is the reference object).** Every left ideal of $\mathbb{B}$ is a submodule of ${}_{\mathbb{B}}\mathbb{B}$ and every element of $\mathbb{B}$ is a scalar of the module, so the algebra and its module are cut from the same multiplication. This is why the module theory of $\mathbb{B}$ can be read off from the single object ${}_{\mathbb{B}}\mathbb{B}$ and its two one-sided twins, and why the left ideal theory of *The Peirce Decomposition* is simultaneously the submodule theory of the regular module.

## The Submodules Are the Ideals

**Theorem.** Let $L\subseteq\mathbb{B}$ be an additive subgroup. Then $L$ is a submodule of ${}_{\mathbb{B}}\mathbb{B}$ if and only if $L$ is a left ideal of $\mathbb{B}$; $L$ is a submodule of $\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{B}}$ if and only if $L$ is a right ideal; and $L$ is a sub-bimodule of ${}_{\mathbb{B}}\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{B}}$ if and only if $L$ is a two-sided ideal.

**Proof.** A submodule of ${}_{\mathbb{B}}\mathbb{B}$ is an additive subgroup closed under $\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2$ for all $\tilde Q_1\in\mathbb{B}$ and $\tilde Q_2\in L$, and that is the definition of a left ideal. The scalar action of ${}_{\mathbb{B}}\mathbb{B}$ is by construction the multiplication of $\mathbb{B}$, so the two conditions are the same formula and not two equivalent ones. The right-handed statement is the same argument with the multiplication read on the right, and the two-sided statement applies both readings at once.

**Corollary (the lattice of ideals is the lattice of submodules).** The identity map on subsets of $\mathbb{B}$ is an isomorphism of the lattice of submodules of ${}_{\mathbb{B}}\mathbb{B}$ onto the lattice of left ideals of $\mathbb{B}$, preserving inclusion, intersection and sum; likewise for the right ideals and for the two-sided ideals of the bimodule.

**Remark (the biquaternion content).** Because $\mathbb{B}$ is simple, the two-sided ideals are only $0$ and $\mathbb{B}$, so the regular bimodule has no nontrivial sub-bimodule; this is the bimodule reading of the simplicity proved in *The Peirce Decomposition*. The left ideals are the left ideals of the algebra, classified there as $0$, the minimal left ideals $\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi$ generated by the primitive idempotents of *Idempotents*, and $\mathbb{B}$ itself; the theorem above says that the same list is the complete list of submodules of the left regular module.

## The Endomorphisms of the Regular Object

**The endomorphism ring of the regular module is the algebra itself, and the bimodule endomorphisms are the centre.**

**Proposition.** $\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{B}}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{B}})\cong\mathbb{B}$ by left multiplications $\tilde Q\mapsto L_{\tilde Q}$, and $\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{B}}({}_{\mathbb{B}}\mathbb{B})\cong\mathbb{B}^{\mathrm{opp}}$ by right multiplications $\tilde Q\mapsto R_{\tilde Q}$.

*Proof.* A right $\mathbb{B}$-linear $T$ satisfies $T(\tilde Q)=T(e_t\tilde Q)=T(e_t)\tilde Q$, so $T=L_{T(e_t)}$; conversely every left multiplication is right $\mathbb{B}$-linear. The left-handed case is the mirror, with the opposite multiplication.

**Proposition.** $\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{B}\text{-}\mathbb{B}}(\mathbb{B})\cong Z(\mathbb{B})=\mathbb{C}e_t$, of complex dimension one.

*Proof.* A bimodule endomorphism is both a left and a right multiplication, $L_{\tilde Q_1}=R_{\tilde Q_2}$; evaluating at $e_t$ gives $\tilde Q_1=\tilde Q_2$, so the multiplier is central. Conversely a central multiplier is a bimodule map, since $L_{\tilde Q}=R_{\tilde Q}$ commutes with both actions. The centre is $\mathbb{C}e_t$ by *The Biquaternion Algebra*.

The simple module $S=\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+$, its basis, its left action and its endomorphism ring are constructed in *The Minimal Left Ideals*; the regular module is the direct sum $S\oplus S$ of two copies.

## Summary

- The algebra is a left, a right and a two-sided module over itself, by left multiplication, right multiplication and the sandwich.
- The submodules of the regular module are the ideals: left, right or two-sided according to the side.
- The algebra is simple: its two-sided ideals are $0$ and $\mathbb{B}$. It is of length two as a left module, with the two minimal left ideals as its composition factors.
- The radical is zero, and the algebra is semisimple.
- The regular module is the direct sum $S\oplus S$ of two copies of the simple module, and the algebra is the endomorphism ring of the regular module.
