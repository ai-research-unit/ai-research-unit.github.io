# __Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents of the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra__

## Introduction

A root of a central value is defined by an equation in which the product occurs, so it belongs to
the product (*Definitions for the Study of the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space*, §*Powers, the Square and
the Roots*). The block of this article is the antisymmetric plain multiplication
$$
\tilde\Xi\wedge\tilde Q=\tfrac12\bigl(\tilde\Xi\tilde Q-\tilde Q\tilde\Xi\bigr)=\vec\Xi\times\vec Q,
$$
the cross product of the two vector parts, which is alternating and without a unit
(*Introduction to the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra of Biquaternions*).

The article treats two subjects, and in this order. The first is the square roots of the central
value $0$, the nonzero elements $\tilde\Xi\in\mathbb{B}$ with
$$
\tilde\Xi\wedge\tilde\Xi=0 .
$$
The second is the nilpotents of the block, the elements $\tilde\Upsilon$ some power of which
vanishes. The two subjects are one subject, because a square-zero element is exactly a nilpotent of
index two or the origin, and the root set of $0$ is the punctured space, at once the nonzero
square-zero elements, the nonzero nilpotents and the zero divisors of the block. The reduction that
produces all of it is one line, the alternation of the block.

The outcome is total. The square of every element of the block is zero,
$\tilde\Upsilon\wedge\tilde\Upsilon=0$, so **every element of the space is a nilpotent**: the origin
is the trivial one, of index one, and every other element is of index two, its square vanishing
while it does not. The nilpotent class is the whole space, and the nonzero nilpotents are the
punctured space; the implication *nilpotent implies zero divisor* holds for the block in the total
form, with the origin as its only excluded element. The conjugations preserve the class, the
remarkable subspaces inherit it, and the comparison with the parent product is the comparison of
the whole space with a proper cone.

The article is algebraic. It does not treat the roots of $+1$ and the idempotents, which are
*Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra*; the square roots of a
general right side, which is *Square Roots of a General Element in the Antisymmetric Plain
Algebra*; or the zero-divisor set in full, which is *Zero Divisors of the Antisymmetric Plain
Algebra*.

**The results.** For the antisymmetric plain multiplication $\tilde\Xi\wedge\tilde Q=\vec\Xi\times\vec Q$ the square of every element vanishes, $\tilde\Xi\wedge\tilde\Xi=0$, so **every element of the space is a nilpotent**: the origin of index one and every other element of index two. The root set of $0$ is the punctured space $\mathbb{B}\setminus\{0\}$, at once the nonzero square-zero elements, the nonzero nilpotents and the zero divisors; the implication *nilpotent implies zero divisor* therefore holds in the **total** form, with the origin as its only excluded element. The comparison with the parent product is the comparison of the whole space with the pure isotropic cone, of complex dimension two.

## The Notion and Its Product

**Definition (root of a central value).** Let $\star$ be a product on $\mathbb{B}$ and let
$\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$. A **root of $\lambda$ in $\star$** is a nonzero element $\tilde\Xi$ with
$$
\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=\lambda e_0 .
$$
The three central values the biquaternion algebra singles out are $-1$, $+1$ and $0$; this article
treats $0$, whose roots are the nonzero nilpotents of the product (*Definitions for the Study of
the Algebraic Structures*, §*Powers, the Square and the Roots*).

**Definition (nilpotent of a product).** Let $\star$ be a product on $\mathbb{B}$. An element
$\tilde\Upsilon$ is **nilpotent of $\star$** when some power of it vanishes,
$$
\tilde\Upsilon^{\star k}=0\quad\text{for some }k\geq1,
$$
the power being the left-nested reading
$\tilde\Upsilon^{\star k}=\bigl(\cdots\bigl((\tilde\Upsilon\star\tilde\Upsilon)\star\tilde\Upsilon\bigr)\cdots\star\tilde\Upsilon\bigr)$
when the product is not associative. The least such $k$ is the **index of nilpotency**, and an
element of index two, $\tilde\Upsilon\star\tilde\Upsilon=0$ with $\tilde\Upsilon\neq0$, is a
**square-zero element of $\star$**. The origin is the trivial nilpotent, of index one
(*Definitions for the Study of the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space*, §*The Nilpotent*).

Throughout this article the product is the **antisymmetric plain multiplication** $\wedge$, and
unqualified, *root* means a root of the block and *nilpotent* means a nilpotent of the block. The
nilpotent is the root of $0$ in the sense of the definitions article, and the two subjects of this
article are its two faces. The general square and the powers of a non-associative product are *The
Symmetric and Antisymmetric Parts of an Algebra Product*, and the nilpotents of the parent plain
product, to which the block is compared, are *Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents of the General Plain Algebra*.

## The Roots of Zero

**The root set of $0$ is the punctured space: $\{\tilde\Xi\neq0:\tilde\Xi\wedge\tilde\Xi=0\}=\mathbb{B}\setminus\{0\}$.**

**Proposition (the reduction).** For every element $\tilde\Xi$,
$$
\tilde\Xi\wedge\tilde\Xi=0 ,
$$
and consequently the root equation $\tilde\Xi\wedge\tilde\Xi=0$ holds at every element, so that the
root condition is exactly the condition $\tilde\Xi\neq0$.

*Proof.* The block is alternating, $\tilde X\wedge\tilde X=0$ for every $\tilde X$, by
*Introduction to the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra of Biquaternions*; read at $\tilde X=\tilde\Xi$
this is the display. Substituting the display into the root equation reduces it to a tautology,
uniformly in $\tilde\Xi$.

**Theorem (the roots of $0$ are all the nonzero elements).** The block's roots of $0$ are the
nonzero elements of $\mathbb{B}$,
$$
\{\tilde\Xi\neq0:\ \tilde\Xi\wedge\tilde\Xi=0\}=\mathbb{B}\setminus\{0\}.
$$

*Proof.* The reduction gives $\tilde\Xi\wedge\tilde\Xi=0$ for every $\tilde\Xi$, so the equation
$\tilde\Xi\wedge\tilde\Xi=0$ holds at every element and the root condition is exactly the condition
$\tilde\Xi\neq0$, which excludes the origin alone.

The root set of $0$ is therefore the whole punctured space, and it is at once the set of the nonzero
square-zero elements of the block and its zero-divisor set: an element is a root of $0$ exactly when
it is a nonzero element with vanishing square, and by *Zero Divisors of the Antisymmetric Plain
Algebra* it is then a two-sided zero divisor with itself as partner. The origin is the square-zero
element that is not a root of $0$, excluded by the definition of the root of a central value; apart
from it the three names — root of $0$, square-zero element, zero divisor — select one and the same
set, and the set is as large as the space allows. The same set is the set of the nonzero nilpotents,
which is the subject of §*The Nilpotents*.

## The Nilpotents

**Every element of the block is a nilpotent, $\tilde\Upsilon\wedge\tilde\Upsilon=0$: the origin is of index one and every other element is of index two.**

### The Criterion

**Theorem (every element is a nilpotent).** For every element $\tilde\Upsilon$,
$$
\tilde\Upsilon\wedge\tilde\Upsilon=0 ,
$$
and consequently every element of $\mathbb{B}$ is a nilpotent of the block: the origin is of index
one and every other element is of index two.

*Proof.* The reduction of §*The Roots of Zero* gives the vanishing of the square at every element.
Hence the second power of every element vanishes; an element whose square vanishes has every higher
power zero, the powers being read from the square, so the index of a nonzero element is exactly
two; and the origin is the only element whose first power vanishes, so its index is one.

The criterion is the alternation of the product read as an element statement. There is no
stratification by index: the index is one at the origin and two everywhere else, and no element of
the block is of index three or more, because the square has already vanished.

### The Nilpotent Class

**The nilpotent class of the block is the whole space, $\mathbb{B}$, and the nonzero nilpotents are the punctured space $\mathbb{B}\setminus\{0\}$.**

**Proposition (the class is the whole space).** The nilpotent set of the block is the whole space,
and the nonzero nilpotents are the punctured space:
$$
\{\tilde\Upsilon:\tilde\Upsilon\text{ nilpotent of }\wedge\}=\mathbb{B},\qquad
\{\tilde\Upsilon\neq0:\tilde\Upsilon\text{ nilpotent}\}=\mathbb{B}\setminus\{0\}.
$$

*Proof.* The theorem gives the vanishing of the square at every element, so every element satisfies
the defining condition with $k=2$ (the origin with $k=1$). The nonzero nilpotents are obtained by
excluding the origin.

The class is as large as the space allows. The sum of two elements of the space is an element of the
space, and likewise the scalar multiple and the bracket, and every element of the space is a
nilpotent, so the class is closed under the three operations; in particular it is a subspace of
$\mathbb{B}$, and being all of $\mathbb{B}$ it is the whole space and not a proper subset of it.

### The Relation to the Zero Divisors and to the Roots of Zero

**The nonzero nilpotents, the zero divisors and the roots of $0$ are one and the same set, the punctured space $\mathbb{B}\setminus\{0\}$; the implication *nilpotent implies zero divisor* holds in the total form, with the origin as its only excluded element.**

**Proposition (the three classes coincide).** The nonzero nilpotents, the zero divisors and the
roots of $0$ are one and the same set,
$$
\{\text{nonzero nilpotents}\}=\{\text{zero divisors}\}=\{\text{roots of }0\}=\mathbb{B}\setminus\{0\}.
$$

*Proof.* The nonzero nilpotents are the punctured space by the previous proposition; the zero
divisors are the punctured space by *Zero Divisors of the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra*, because the
block is alternating and every nonzero element is its own annihilating partner; and the roots of
$0$ are the punctured space by §*The Roots of Zero* of this article. The three descriptions select
one set.

**Corollary (the implication *nilpotent implies zero divisor*).** For the block the implication
holds in the total form: every nonzero nilpotent is a two-sided zero divisor, with itself as the
annihilating partner; the origin is the only nilpotent that is not a zero divisor, and it is
excluded by the definition of the zero divisor, which requires both the element and its partner to
be nonzero.

*Proof.* A nonzero nilpotent $\tilde\Upsilon$ satisfies $\tilde\Upsilon\wedge\tilde\Upsilon=0$,
and the element $\tilde\Upsilon$ itself is a nonzero partner for both sides; the element is
therefore a two-sided zero divisor. The origin satisfies $0\wedge0=0$ and is not a zero divisor,
the definition requiring nonzero elements.

The implication is one of the rows of the dictionary of the four general products, where it holds
for the plain product and the natural product and fails for the plain sesquilinear product
(*Definitions for the Study of the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space*, §9; *Zero Divisors in the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space*). For the block it holds without exception on
the punctured space, and the exception is the origin alone.

### The Conjugations and the Remarkable Subspaces

**The conjugations preserve the nilpotent class, and every element of every remarkable subspace is a square-zero element of the block, hence a nilpotent; on the four subspaces stable under the bracket the restriction of the block is again alternating.**

**Proposition (the conjugations preserve the class).** The nilpotent class of the block is
preserved by the bar, the natural conjugation and the star; the conjugates of a nilpotent are
nilpotents.

*Proof.* For the block the statement is immediate from the criterion, every element being a
nilpotent, so every conjugate is one; the compatibility of each conjugation with the bracket is
recorded here for the comparison with the other products, where the class is proper and the
preservation is a computation. The bar is an automorphism of the block,
$\overline{\tilde\Xi\wedge\tilde Q}=\bar{\tilde\Xi}\wedge\bar{\tilde Q}$, so it carries a vanishing
square to a vanishing square. The natural conjugation and the star are anti-automorphisms of the
plain product and they reverse the bracket,
$\tilde\Xi^{\natural}\wedge\tilde Q^{\natural}=-(\tilde\Xi\wedge\tilde Q)^{\natural}$ and likewise
for the star, so they carry a vanishing square to the negative of a vanishing square, which
vanishes as well.

**Proposition (the remarkable subspaces and the class).** Every element of every remarkable subspace
is a square-zero element of the block, hence a nilpotent of the block. On each of the four subspaces
stable under the bracket the restriction of the block is again alternating, and its elements are
nilpotents of the restriction; on the anti-quaternion subspace and on the Hermitian subspace the
bracket of two elements leaves the subspace, and the class is read there in the ambient block
alone.

*Proof.* The square of an element of a subspace vanishes as the square of an element of the ambient
algebra vanishes, the block being alternating; this gives the first sentence for all six subspaces,
and it is the case in which the class is inherited without any stability. The four stable subspaces
are the centre, the vector subspace, the quaternion subspace and the anti-Hermitian subspace, and
the two others are the anti-quaternion subspace and the Hermitian subspace; the table of the
stabilities, the four Lie subalgebras and the two failures are *Remarkable Subspaces under the
Antisymmetric Plain Algebra of Biquaternions*.

### The Comparison with the Parent Product

**The parent's nilpotents are the pure elements with isotropic vector part, a proper cone of complex dimension two, and the block's strictly contain them: $e_0$ is a nilpotent of the block ($e_0\wedge e_0=0$) and not of the parent ($e_0^{2}=e_0$).**

**Remark (the parent nilpotents).** The parent general plain bilinear product is associative and
unital, and its nilpotents are the pure elements with isotropic vector part, the vector parts being
paired by the complex bilinear form $(\vec P,\vec Q)=P_1Q_1+P_2Q_2+P_3Q_3$:
$$
\tilde\Xi^{2}=0\iff \Xi_0=0\ \text{and}\ (\vec\Xi,\vec\Xi)=0
$$
(*Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents of the General Plain Algebra*). The parent's nilpotent class is therefore a proper
cone of the space, of complex dimension two, and the implication *nilpotent implies zero divisor*
holds there with the pure isotropic cone as the set. The block is the antisymmetric half of the
parent, and the comparison is the comparison of a proper cone with the whole space: the block's
nilpotents strictly contain the parent's, the element $e_0$ being a nilpotent of the block
($e_0\wedge e_0=0$) and not of the parent ($e_0^{2}=e_0$). The enlargement is the removal of the
scalar part: the parent's criterion is a condition on the square, and the block's square is
identically zero, so the condition is satisfied by every element.

## Summary

For the antisymmetric plain multiplication $\tilde\Xi\wedge\tilde Q=\vec\Xi\times\vec Q$ the square of every element vanishes, so every element is a nilpotent: the origin of index one and every other element of index two. The root set of $0$ is the whole punctured space, at once the nonzero square-zero elements, the nonzero nilpotents and the zero divisors, the three descriptions selecting one set. The implication *nilpotent implies zero divisor* holds in the total form, the origin being the only nilpotent excluded by the definition of the zero divisor; the conjugations preserve the class and every remarkable subspace inherits it. The comparison with the parent product is the comparison of the whole space with the pure isotropic cone, and the enlargement is the removal of the scalar part from the square.
