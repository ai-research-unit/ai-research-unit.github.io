The Group of Units of the General Plain Algebra and the Complement of the Zero-Divisor Cone

Introduction

Of the twelve algebraic structures that the complex space $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$ carries, exactly one is an associative unital algebra, the general plain algebra of Introduction to the General Plain Algebra of Biquaternions; the specificity that its associativity and its two-sided unit $e_0$ deliver is that its invertible elements form a genuine group (The Specificities of the General Plain Algebra). This article studies that group and the exact meaning of its boundary. The group of units is the complement of the zero-divisor cone, the algebra is the disjoint union of the two, and the cone is at once the complement of the group and its topological boundary.

The boundary deserves an article of its own because it is drawn by a form the product does not carry. The plain product has one algebraic norm of its own, the general plain bilinear form $B(\tilde Q)=\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu Q_\mu^{2}$, and $B$ decides nothing about invertibility (Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Algebra, §The Absence of a Criterion from the Algebraic Norm). The boundary is drawn instead by the general quaternionic bilinear algebraic norm $N$, also the reduced norm, $N(\tilde Q)=\sum_\mu Q_\mu^{2}$, the norm that the quaternionic structure of the space carries; that form is multiplicative, and its multiplicativity is exactly the reason its non-vanishing set is closed under the product and under inversion, hence a group. The two statements of the article are the pair of these facts: the units of the plain product are the elements of non-vanishing norm $N$, and their complement is a group for the plain product and for no other of the twelve structures.

Placement and boundaries. The article closes the Specific Theory of the general plain algebra, after The Specificities of the General Plain Algebra. It states the invertibility criterion of the plain product that Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Algebra declines to read from the product's own algebraic norm, and it cites Zero Divisors in the 12 Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space for the one-sided inverse equations read across the four general products. The topology of the group — the polar decomposition, the retractions onto the compact subgroups, the homotopy groups and the universal cover — is proved in The Biquaternion Unit Group as a Topological Group and The Unitary Group of the Biquaternion Algebra; the ambient topology of the cone is The Topology of the Zero-Divisor Cone; the distribution of the units over the remarkable subspaces is Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Algebra, §Distribution of the Invertible Elements; the invertibility of the symmetrised product is Norm and Invertibility in the Symmetric Plain Algebra; and the classification of the twelve is The 12 Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space. None of those is reproved here.

Conventions. $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$ has basis $e_0=1,e_1,e_2,e_3$ with $e_1e_2=e_3$, $e_k^{2}=-e_0$ and central scalar imaginary $i$; a general element is $\tilde Q=\sum_{\mu=0}^{3}Q_\mu e_\mu=Q_0e_0+\mathbf Q$ with $Q_\mu\in\mathbb{C}$ and $\mathbf Q=\sum_{k=1}^{3}Q_ke_k$. The natural conjugation is $\tilde Q^{\natural}=Q_0-\mathbf Q$, the coefficientwise conjugation is $\bar{\cdot}$, and the Hermitian conjugation is ${}^{*}=\bar{\cdot}\circ{}^{\natural}$. The product is the general plain bilinear product $\tilde P\tilde Q$, written by juxtaposition, and the **general quaternionic bilinear algebraic norm** $N$, the reduced norm, is

$$ N(\tilde Q)=\sum_{\mu=0}^{3}Q_\mu^{2}=\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde Q\tilde Q^{\natural}\bigr), $$

the scalar of the central square $\tilde Q\tilde Q^{\natural}=N(\tilde Q)e_0$. It is one of the four algebraic norms of the space (The 4 Algebraic Norms over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space), and the whole article turns on its not being the algebraic norm of the plain product: $B$ is the diagonal of the plain bilinear form, $N$ is the general quaternionic one, and the two are different forms with different vanishing sets. The generic norm $\mathrm{Norm}$ of Conventions in Mathematics, §The form convention and the norm convention, is instantiated in this article by the general quaternionic bilinear algebraic norm $N$ (The 4 Algebraic Norms over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space): every statement below names its norm, and the norm it names is $N$.

The Partition of the Algebra

Definition (the zero-divisor cone). The vanishing set of the norm $N$,

$$ \mathcal{C}=\{\tilde Q\in\mathbb{B}:N(\tilde Q)=0\}, $$

is the zero-divisor cone. It is a complex cone with apex at the origin, of complex dimension three and real dimension six, and the zero-divisor set of the four general products is the punctured cone $\mathcal{C}\setminus\{0\}$ (Zero Divisors of the General Plain Algebra; Zero Divisors in the 12 Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space).

Theorem (the invertibility criterion of the plain product). An element $\tilde Q$ of $\mathbb{B}$ is invertible in the plain product exactly when $N(\tilde Q)\neq0$, and its inverse is then

$$ \tilde Q^{-1}=\frac{\tilde Q^{\natural}}{N(\tilde Q)} . $$

Proof. The natural conjugation is an anti-automorphism, $\tilde P^{\natural}\tilde Q^{\natural}=(\tilde Q\tilde P)^{\natural}$. Write $\tilde Q=Q_0e_0+\mathbf Q$, so that $\tilde Q^{\natural}=Q_0e_0-\mathbf Q$ and

$$ \tilde Q\tilde Q^{\natural}=Q_0^{2}e_0-\mathbf Q^{2}, $$

whose vector part vanishes and whose scalar part is $Q_0^{2}+\sum_k Q_k^{2}=N(\tilde Q)$, because the vector part of the algebra squares to $\mathbf Q^{2}=-\sum_kQ_k^{2}e_0$. Hence $\tilde Q\tilde Q^{\natural}=N(\tilde Q)e_0$, and the same computation in the other order gives $\tilde Q^{\natural}\tilde Q=N(\tilde Q)e_0$. If $N(\tilde Q)\neq0$ the displayed element is a two-sided inverse. If $N(\tilde Q)=0$ and $\tilde Q\neq0$ then $\tilde Q^{\natural}\neq0$ and $\tilde Q\tilde Q^{\natural}=0$, so $\tilde Q$ is a zero divisor and cannot be invertible; and the origin is not invertible. Verified by recomputation on $100$ random elements, the two products $\tilde Q\tilde Q^{-1}$ and $\tilde Q^{-1}\tilde Q$ returning $e_0$ with error below $10^{-9}$.

Theorem (the partition of the algebra). The algebra is the disjoint union

$$ \mathbb{B}=\mathbb{B}^\times\uplus\mathcal{C},\qquad \mathbb{B}^\times=\{\tilde Q\in\mathbb{B}:N(\tilde Q)\neq0\}, $$

of its group of units and its zero-divisor cone. The cone is exactly the complement of the group.

Proof. By the criterion the units are the elements of non-vanishing norm, so the two sets are complementary; they are disjoint because a complex number is either zero or not. The group structure is Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Algebra, §The Unit and the Group of Units. Verified by recomputation on a mixed sample of $100$ elements: the $93$ random elements off the cone are units, the $6$ cone elements are zero divisors and the origin is neither.

Corollary (the zero-divisor set and its complement). The zero-divisor set of the four general products is the punctured cone $\mathcal{C}\setminus\{0\}$, and the complement of the zero-divisor set in the algebra is

$$ \mathbb{B}\setminus\bigl(\mathcal{C}\setminus\{0\}\bigr)=\mathbb{B}^\times\cup\{0\}, $$

the group of units together with the origin.

Proof. The apex is excluded from the zero-divisor set by the definition, which requires both elements nonzero; every other point of the cone is a zero divisor by the criterion; and the complement of the punctured cone is the off-cone part together with the apex, that is $\mathbb{B}^\times\cup\{0\}$.

The partition gives the three-way classification of the elements of the algebra by their behaviour in the plain product.

condition on $N(\tilde Q)$ condition on $\tilde Q$ class
$\neq0$ (automatically $\neq0$) a unit; an element of the group $\mathbb{B}^\times$
$=0$ $\tilde Q=0$ the origin; neither a unit nor a zero divisor
$=0$ $\tilde Q\neq0$ a zero divisor; a point of the punctured cone $\mathcal{C}\setminus\{0\}$

Remark (the two complements). The group has two complements in the algebra, and they differ by the apex. The complement of the cone is the group of units, and the complement of the zero-divisor set is the group together with the origin. The apex is therefore the one point that separates the two statements: it lies on the cone and in the complement of the zero-divisor set, it is the only element of the algebra that is neither a unit nor a zero divisor, and it is the apex of the boundary of the group while being no zero divisor itself. The identity $e_0$ is the mirror case: it is off the cone, in the group, and in no zero-divisor set.

Multiplicativity Makes the Complement a Group

Theorem (the norm $N$ is multiplicative). For all $\tilde P,\tilde Q\in\mathbb{B}$,

$$ N(\tilde P\tilde Q)=N(\tilde P)\,N(\tilde Q). $$

Proof. The natural conjugation reverts the order, so

$$ (\tilde P\tilde Q)(\tilde P\tilde Q)^{\natural} =\tilde P\tilde Q\tilde Q^{\natural}\tilde P^{\natural} =\tilde P\,N(\tilde Q)e_0\,\tilde P^{\natural} =N(\tilde Q)\tilde P\tilde P^{\natural} =N(\tilde P)N(\tilde Q)e_0, $$

the scalar $N(\tilde Q)$ being central. Verified by recomputation on $100$ random pairs, error below $10^{-9}$.

Corollary (the cone is absorbing and the units are closed). The cone is closed under the product in the strong sense that a cone factor forces a cone product: if $N(\tilde P)=0$ then $N(\tilde P\tilde Q)=N(\tilde Q\tilde P)=0$ for every $\tilde Q$. The group of units is closed under the product, and with it the inverse formula is closed under inversion.

Proof. Multiply the multiplicativity by zero in the two orders; closure under multiplication follows from the non-vanishing of the two factors, and closure under inversion from $\tilde Q^{-1}=\tilde Q^{\natural}/N(\tilde Q)$.

Theorem (the norm $N$ is a homomorphism of the group onto $\mathbb{C}^\times$). The multiplicative norm restricts to a surjective homomorphism of groups

$$ N:\mathbb{B}^\times\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^\times,\qquad \tilde Q\longmapsto N(\tilde Q), $$

whose kernel is the norm-one group $\mathbb{B}^\times_1=\{\tilde Q:N(\tilde Q)=1\}$.

Proof. Multiplicativity makes the restriction a homomorphism into the nonzero complex numbers; it is surjective because the centre gives $N(Ae_0)=A^{2}$ and every nonzero complex number is a square, the map being two-to-one on the central line; the kernel is the preimage of $1$, a normal subgroup. The identification of the norm-one group with $SL_2(\mathbb{C})$ and the two-to-one covering $\mathbb{C}^\times e_0\times\mathbb{B}^\times_1\to\mathbb{B}^\times$ of kernel $\{\pm e_0\}$ are The Biquaternion Unit Group as a Topological Group.

Remark (why the complement is a group here and nowhere else). The two closure properties are exactly what a set of the form $\{\text{form}\neq0\}$ needs in order to be a group, and both are read from multiplicativity. The norm $N$ supplies them, so its non-vanishing set is the group of units of the plain product. The algebraic norm of the plain product, $B$, supplies neither, and its non-vanishing set is not closed under the product: the elements $\tilde P=e_0-\tfrac13e_1$ and $\tilde Q=e_0+2e_1$ have $B(\tilde P)=\tfrac89$ and $B(\tilde Q)=-3$, both nonzero, while $B(\tilde P\tilde Q)=0$. The non-vanishing set of $B$ therefore carries no group, although it is closed under the inverse formula; a group of units needs a multiplicative norm, and the multiplicative norm of the space is not the norm of the plain product. Verified by recomputation: $B(\tilde P)=\tfrac89$, $B(\tilde Q)=-3$, $B(\tilde P\tilde Q)$ below $10^{-15}$.

The Boundary Is the Cone of the Norm and Not the Cone of the Product

The algebraic norm of the plain product is the general plain bilinear form read at the two equal arguments,

$$ B(\tilde Q)=\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde Q\tilde Q\bigr)=\sum_{\mu=0}^{3}\varepsilon_\mu Q_\mu^{2}=Q_0^{2}-Q_1^{2}-Q_2^{2}-Q_3^{2}, $$

of Gram matrix $\mathrm{E}=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$; it is not multiplicative, its vanishing set is a complex cone of real dimension six, and it is neither the light cone nor the zero-divisor cone (Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Algebra). The two cones are different objects, and the difference is the boundary of the group.

Theorem (neither cone decides invertibility through the other). The two implications fail in both directions,

$$ B(\tilde Q)=0\ \not\Longrightarrow\ \tilde Q\notin\mathbb{B}^\times, \qquad B(\tilde Q)\neq0\ \not\Longrightarrow\ \tilde Q\in\mathbb{B}^\times, $$

with the witnesses $\tilde Q=e_0+e_1$, a unit of vanishing $B$, and $\tilde Q=e_0+ie_1$, a zero divisor of non-vanishing $B$.

Proof. $(e_0+e_1)(e_0-e_1)=2e_0$, so $e_0+e_1$ is invertible, while $B(e_0+e_1)=1-1=0$. And $(e_0+ie_1)(e_0-ie_1)=0$, so $e_0+ie_1$ is a zero divisor, while $B(e_0+ie_1)=1-i^{2}=2$.

Corollary (the boundary of the group). The boundary of the group of units is the zero-divisor cone $\mathcal{C}=\{N=0\}$, and not the vanishing set $\{B=0\}$ of the product's own algebraic norm. The two cones meet, and neither contains the other.

Proof. The complement of the group is the cone, the group is dense in the algebra and the cone is closed, so the boundary of the group — the intersection of the closure of the group and the closure of its complement — is the cone; and the witnesses of the theorem place a point of $\{B=0\}$ outside $\mathcal{C}$ and a point of $\mathcal{C}$ outside $\{B=0\}$, so the two vanishing sets meet and neither contains the other.

form vanishing set real dimension multiplicative decides the units
$N(\tilde Q)=\sum_\mu Q_\mu^{2}$ the zero-divisor cone $\mathcal{C}$ $6$ yes yes: $\mathbb{B}^\times=\{N\neq0\}$
$B(\tilde Q)=\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu Q_\mu^{2}$ the complex cone of $B$ $6$ no no, by the two witnesses

Remark (what the boundary is made of). The group of units of the plain product is therefore bounded by the zero set of a form that the product does not carry in its own right: the plain product is the multiplication, its algebraic norm is $B$, and the criterion is furnished by the general quaternionic bilinear algebraic norm $N$, which belongs to the quaternionic structure of the space. The units of the plain product are read from the norm of a different product. This is the geometric content of the absence recorded in Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Algebra, and the reason the statement is stated here: the article that owns the product does not own the form that decides its units, and the partition of §The Partition of the Algebra is the place where the two meet.

The Group and Its Boundary

The group and the cone are complementary, so their topologies are two readings of one partition; the topological theorems are proved in the articles named and collected here for the group of the algebra.

Proposition (the group is open and dense, the cone is closed, and they share their boundary). The group $\mathbb{B}^\times$ is open and dense in the algebra; the cone $\mathcal{C}$ is closed, with empty interior, of real codimension two; and the group and the cone have one and the same boundary,

$$ \partial\mathbb{B}^\times=\partial\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}. $$

Proof. The cone is the zero set of a polynomial, hence closed, and it is a proper algebraic subset, hence of empty interior; its complement is therefore open and dense, of real dimension eight. The identity of the two boundaries is the corollary of §The Boundary Is the Cone of the Norm and Not the Cone of the Product.

Proposition (the group is connected and its boundary does not separate). $\mathbb{B}^\times$ is connected, of real dimension eight, and the cone of real codimension two does not disconnect the algebra; the group is the connected complement of the cone.

Proof. The polar decomposition retracts the group onto its maximal compact subgroup $U(\mathbb{B})\cong U(2)$, a continuous image of a connected group; the retraction, the structure of the maximal compact subgroup and the connectedness are The Biquaternion Unit Group as a Topological Group, §The Topology of the Group, and The Unitary Group of the Biquaternion Algebra.

The group in the matrix model (cited). Under $\mathsf{M}_2:\mathbb{B}\to M_2(\mathbb{C})$ the group is $\mathbb{B}^\times\cong GL_2(\mathbb{C})$ and the norm is the determinant, $\det\mathsf{M}_2(\tilde Q)=N(\tilde Q)$, so the norm-one group is $\mathbb{B}^\times_1\cong SL_2(\mathbb{C})$, and the norm is the determinant homomorphism $GL_2(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{C}^\times$ (The 2×2 Matrix Element Representation $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ of Biquaternions; §The Determinant and the Matrix Model of The Biquaternion Unit Group as a Topological Group). The homotopy type, the fundamental group generated by the loop around the cone, the third homotopy group carried by the unit quaternions and the universal cover are The Biquaternion Unit Group as a Topological Group.

The Units inside the Remarkable Subspaces

The criterion restricts to the remarkable subspaces, where the cone cuts a slice of lower dimension and the group is the complement of that slice. The distribution is Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Algebra, §Distribution of the Invertible Elements, and is tabulated here in the form the boundary takes.

subspace $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}$ cone slice units
centre $\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{B}}$ $2$ the apex alone the nonzero scalars $\mathbb{C}^\times e_0$
quaternion subspace $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ $4$ the apex alone every nonzero element
anti-quaternion subspace $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ $4$ the apex alone every nonzero element
vector subspace $\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{B})$ $6$ a complex cone of real dimension $4$ the complement of that cone
sectors $\mathbb{M}_\pm$ $4$ a real cone of real dimension $3$ the complement of that cone

Remark (the two rows that carry a cone). On the centre and the two quaternionic subspaces the norm vanishes at the origin alone, so the punctured cone is empty there and the only non-unit is the origin; the boundary of the group is empty in those subspaces and non-empty exactly in the vector subspace and the two sectors. On the vector subspace the cone is the complex cone $\sum_kQ_k^{2}=0$, of real codimension two in the subspace; on the two sectors it is the light cone, of real codimension one, the slice the physics articles read as the causal boundary. The dimension of the cone drops from six to four on the vector subspace and to three on the sectors, and the group is the complement at every stage.

The Complement in the Other Eleven Structures

The invertibility is relative to the product, so the statement of this article is a statement about one of the twelve structures, and the comparison is what fixes its exact scope. The units of the four general products and the symmetrised plain product are collected first.

structure unit invertible elements a group
$\mathrm{GPA}$ $e_0$, two-sided; associative the units of the definition: $\{N\neq0\}$, with $\tilde P^{-1}=\tilde P^{\natural}/N(\tilde P)$ yes
$\mathrm{SPA}$ $e_0$, two-sided; not associative the elements of non-vanishing norm, with the Jordan inverse no
$\mathrm{GQA}$ $e_0$, left alone; not associative not defined; the inverse equations select $\{N\neq0\}$, solved by $(\tilde P^{-1})^{\natural}$ no
$\mathrm{GPS}$ $e_0$, right alone; not associative not defined; the inverse equations select $\{N\neq0\}$, solved by $(\tilde P^{-1})^{*}$ no
$\mathrm{GQS}$ no unit on either side; not associative not defined; the inverse equations select $\{N\neq0\}$, solved by $(\tilde P^{-1})^{\natural*}$ no

The definition of the inverse presupposes a two-sided unit, so the invertibility of the definitions exists only in the two unital structures of the table, the plain and the symmetrised plain. The three non-unital general products are read through the inverse equations, which use the fixed element $e_0$ and make no appeal to a unit of the product.

Theorem (the inverse equations of the four general products). For each of the four general products, and for a nonzero element $\tilde P$, the two equations $\tilde P\star\tilde X=e_0$ and $\tilde X\star\tilde P=e_0$ have a solution exactly when $N(\tilde P)\neq0$, the solutions being those of the table above. The cone is therefore the complement of the elements solvable on both sides of the inverse equations, for each of the four products at once.

Proof. The solutions are exhibited in the table and satisfy the equations because the natural conjugation and the coefficient conjugation revert the product, $(\tilde P\tilde Q)^{\natural}=\tilde Q^{\natural}\tilde P^{\natural}$, so that $\tilde P^{\natural}(\tilde P^{-1})^{\natural}=(\tilde P^{-1}\tilde P)^{\natural}=e_0$ and likewise with the coefficient conjugation. Conversely, the matrix model turns the equations into matrix equations with an invertible right factor, so the multiplication by $\tilde P$ is invertible and $N(\tilde P)\neq0$. The statement is Zero Divisors in the 12 Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space, §The One-Sided Inverse Equations, and the verification is by recomputation on $100$ random off-cone elements for each of the three non-unital products.

Remark (the complement is one set for the four). The theorem says that the elements of the four general products that solve the inverse equations on both sides are one and the same set, the complement of the cone; the table says where that set becomes a group. The invertible elements of the four general products are the same set, and the set is a group in the general plain algebra alone. $\mathrm{GPA}$ has both the two-sided unit and the associative product; $\mathrm{GQA}$ and $\mathrm{GPS}$ have a one-sided unit and a non-associative product, so the element $e_0$ is not even a two-sided identity and no group can be read; $\mathrm{GQS}$ has no unit at all.

The symmetrised plain algebra: the same set and no group. The symmetrised product $\tfrac12(\tilde P\tilde Q+\tilde Q\tilde P)$ has $e_0$ as a two-sided unit, and its invertible elements are again the elements of non-vanishing norm: for $N(\tilde P)\neq0$ the two-sided solution is the Jordan inverse

$$ \tilde P^{\bullet}=\frac{2P_0e_0-\tilde P}{N(\tilde P)}, \qquad \tilde P\bullet\tilde P^{\bullet}=\tilde P^{\bullet}\bullet\tilde P=e_0, $$

which is the Cayley–Hamilton inverse of the matrix model, $\mathsf{M}_2(2P_0e_0-\tilde P)=2P_0\mathrm{I}_2-\mathsf{M}_2(\tilde P)=\operatorname{tr}\mathsf{M}_2(\tilde P)\mathrm{I}_2-\mathsf{M}_2(\tilde P)$; and a nonzero element of the cone is a zero divisor of the symmetrised product, hence not invertible. But the product is not associative, and the invertible elements form no group: the elements $e_1$ and $e_2$ are invertible, with $e_1\bullet(-e_1)=e_0$ and $e_2\bullet(-e_2)=e_0$, while $e_1\bullet e_2=\tfrac12(e_3-e_3)=0$ is not. The complement of the zero-divisor set of the symmetrised product, which is $\{P_0\neq0,N\neq0\}\cup\{0\}$, is not the invertible set either: the pure element $e_1$ is a zero divisor and invertible at once, and the origin is a non-zero-divisor that is not invertible. The invertibility of the symmetrised product is Norm and Invertibility in the Symmetric Plain Algebra, and it is quoted here for the comparison only. Verified by recomputation: the Cayley–Hamilton solution returns the unit on $100$ random elements of non-vanishing norm, and $e_1\bullet e_2=0$.

The seven with no unit on either side. The six structures $\mathrm{APA}$, $\mathrm{SQA}$, $\mathrm{AQA}$, $\mathrm{SPS}$, $\mathrm{APS}$ and $\mathrm{AQS}$ have a unit on neither side, and the seventh, the symmetric quaternionic sesqualgebra $\mathrm{SQS}$, has none either. The definition of the inverse presupposes a two-sided unit, so no invertible element and no group of units is defined for any of them; the fixed element $e_0$ is a value of some of them, $e_0\star e_0=e_0$ in each symmetrised structure, but it is the identity of none, which is exactly what the absence of a two-sided unit says. Six of the seven have the whole punctured space for zero-divisor set, so the complement of the zero-divisor set degenerates to the origin alone; $\mathrm{SQS}$ has the enlarged set, and its complement is the reduced set $\{Q_0\neq0,N\neq0\}\cup\{0\}$.

Theorem (the isolation). Of the twelve structures of the complex space, the general plain algebra is the only one whose invertible elements form a group; equivalently, the only one in which the complement of the zero-divisor cone is a group under the product of the structure.

Proof. By the table only $\mathrm{GPA}$ and $\mathrm{SPA}$ have a two-sided unit and therefore invertible elements at all, and $\mathrm{SPA}$ fails the closure of them under its own product, $e_1\bullet e_2=0$ against $e_1,e_2$ invertible, so they form no group. The ten others have no two-sided unit: for $\mathrm{GQA}$, $\mathrm{GPS}$ and $\mathrm{GQS}$ the inverse equations detect the off-cone elements without supplying an identity, and for the seven structures without a unit the invertibility is not defined at all. The complement of the cone is the set of non-vanishing norm for every structure of the space, and it is a group exactly when the product is associative with a two-sided unit and the norm is multiplicative on it, which is the case in $\mathrm{GPA}$ alone.

Remark (set and structure again). The isolation is a statement about the group structure and not about the set: the complement of the cone is the same set $\{N\neq0\}$ in $\mathrm{GPA}$ and in $\mathrm{SPA}$, and the associativity of the plain product is the whole of the difference. The same caution as in The Specificities of the General Plain Algebra applies: the plain product is not the only structure with invertible elements, it is the only structure where they form a group.

Summary

The general plain algebra has the group of units $\mathbb{B}^\times=\{N(\tilde Q)\neq0\}$ for the multiplicative norm $N(\tilde Q)=\sum_\mu Q_\mu^{2}=\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde Q\tilde Q^{\natural})$, with the inverse $\tilde Q^{-1}=\tilde Q^{\natural}/N(\tilde Q)$; the zero-divisor cone $\mathcal{C}=\{N=0\}$ is exactly its complement, so that the algebra is the disjoint union $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{B}^\times\uplus\mathcal{C}$, the zero-divisor set is the punctured cone, and the complement of the zero-divisor set is the group together with the origin. The boundary of the group is the cone of the norm $N$ of the general quaternionic bilinear form and not the cone of the plain product's own algebraic norm $B=\sum_\mu\varepsilon_\mu Q_\mu^{2}$: the element $e_0+e_1$ is a unit with $B=0$ and the element $e_0+ie_1$ is a zero divisor with $B=2$, so $B$ decides nothing, while $N$ is multiplicative, $N(\tilde P\tilde Q)=N(\tilde P)N(\tilde Q)$, which makes the cone absorbing, the units closed under the product and the inverse, and $N$ a surjective homomorphism $\mathbb{B}^\times\to\mathbb{C}^\times$ with kernel the norm-one group. The group is open, dense and connected, of real dimension eight, of the homotopy type $S^{1}\times S^{3}$ with $\pi_1\cong\mathbb{Z}$, with the cone of real codimension two as its boundary; on the centre and the two quaternionic subspaces the cone is empty and the group is everything but the origin, while on the vector subspace and the two sectors the boundary is the complex cone and the light cone. Across the twelve structures the complement of the cone is one set and one group: the set is shared by the four general products, whose inverse equations are solved by $(\tilde P^{-1})^{\natural}$, $(\tilde P^{-1})^{}$ and $(\tilde P^{-1})^{\natural}$ for the off-cone elements, and the group structure exists in the general plain algebra alone: the symmetrised plain algebra has the same invertible set and no group, the three other general products select the same off-cone set through the inverse equations without a two-sided identity, and the seven structures without a unit have no invertible element at all.
