Square Roots of a General Element in the Symmetric Quaternionic Algebra

Introduction

A square root of an element $\tilde Q$ in a product is an element with $\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=\tilde Q$, and the roots of a central value are the special case $\tilde Q=\lambda e_0$ (Definitions for the Study of the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space §Powers, the Square and the Roots). In the parent plain algebra the problem has a rich classification — roots with non-vanishing vector part, roots of a central value, the reduction of the general element to its scalar part (Square Roots of a General Element in the General Plain Algebra). In the symmetric quaternionic block the problem collapses: the square of an element is always central,

$$ \tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=N(\tilde\Xi)e_0, $$

so a square root exists only for the elements of the central line. This article states the collapse, computes the root set of a central radicand, classifies the two possible cases, and works the examples. The absence of the general case is the content, and the article is short because the absence is stated and not filled.

Boundaries. The roots of the central values $\pm1$ are Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the Symmetric Quaternionic Algebra; the roots of $0$, that is the case $\tilde Q=0$, and the square-zero elements are Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents of the Symmetric Quaternionic Algebra; the diagonal and the non-isotropic class are Norm and Invertibility in the Symmetric Quaternionic Algebra; the general definitions are the definitions article; the parent's classification, which the block collapses, is Square Roots of a General Element in the General Plain Algebra.

Conventions. $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$ has basis $e_0,e_1,e_2,e_3$ with $e_1e_2=e_3$, and central scalar imaginary $i$; a general element is $\tilde Q=Q_0e_0+\vec Q$. The symmetric quaternionic multiplication is $\tilde\Xi\star\tilde Q=N(\tilde\Xi,\tilde Q)e_0$ with $N(\tilde\Xi,\tilde Q)=\Xi_0Q_0+(\vec\Xi,\vec Q)$, and its diagonal is $N(\tilde\Xi)=\sum_{\mu=0}^{3}\Xi_\mu^2$; the operation is also written $\bullet$. The centre is $\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{B}}=\mathbb{C}e_0$.

The results. In the block the square of every element is central, $\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=N(\tilde\Xi)e_0$, so a square root exists only for the central radicands $\tilde Q=\lambda e_0$, and for those the root set is the level set $\mathcal{R}(\lambda)=\{\tilde\Xi:N(\tilde\Xi)=\lambda\}$: the punctured isotropic cone for $\lambda=0$ and an affine hyperquadric of complex dimension three for $\lambda\neq0$, nonempty in both cases. A non-central radicand has no root at all, and there is no finite nonempty root set, so the classification has exactly two cases.

The Reduction

The square of the block is always central, $\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=N(\tilde\Xi)e_0$, so the square-root equation has a solution only for the central radicands $\tilde Q=\lambda e_0$, and a non-central radicand has no root at all.

Theorem (the square of the block is always central). For every $\tilde\Xi$,

$$ \tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=N(\tilde\Xi)e_0\in\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{B}}, $$

so the image of the square map of the block is the central line.

Proof. The product is $\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=N(\tilde\Xi,\tilde\Xi)e_0$ by the definition of the block, and $N(\tilde\Xi,\tilde\Xi)=N(\tilde\Xi)$ is a scalar.

Corollary (the obstruction to a square root). The equation $\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=\tilde Q$ has a solution only for the central radicands $\tilde Q=\lambda e_0$; for a non-central $\tilde Q$ the root set is empty. In particular the block has no element whose square is a vector, and the case of the general theory that treats the roots with non-vanishing vector part does not occur here: there are neither roots of that shape nor a second case to distinguish.

The Roots of a Central Value

For a central radicand $\tilde Q=\lambda e_0$ the root set is the level set $\mathcal{R}(\lambda)=\{\tilde\Xi:N(\tilde\Xi)=\lambda\}$, nonempty for every $\lambda$: the punctured isotropic cone when $\lambda=0$ and an affine hyperquadric of complex dimension three when $\lambda\neq0$; on a complex line $\mathbb{C}\tilde A$ with $N(\tilde A)\neq0$ a nonzero central value has the two roots $t=\pm\sqrt{\lambda/N(\tilde A)}$ and $0$ has none, while the line lies in the root set of $0$ minus the origin when $N(\tilde A)=0$.

Theorem (the root set of a central value). Let $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$ and let $\tilde Q=\lambda e_0$. The root set

$$ \mathcal{R}(\lambda)=\{\tilde\Xi:\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=\lambda e_0\}=\{\tilde\Xi:N(\tilde\Xi)=\lambda\} $$

is nonempty for every $\lambda$, and it is the level set of the diagonal at $\lambda$: the cone $\{N=0\}$ minus the origin when $\lambda=0$, and an affine hyperquadric of complex dimension three when $\lambda\neq0$.

Proof. The reduction gives $N(\tilde\Xi)=\lambda$. Nonemptiness: for $\lambda\neq0$ choose a square root $\mu$ of $\lambda$ complex and take $\tilde\Xi=\mu e_0$, whose $N$ is $\mu^2=\lambda$; for $\lambda=0$ take an isotropic element such as $e_0+ie_1$, whose $N=1-1=0$. The description of the level sets is the one of Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the Symmetric Quaternionic Algebra and Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents of the Symmetric Quaternionic Algebra.

Proposition (the counting on a line). On a complex line $\mathbb{C}\tilde A$ through the origin, the equation reads $t^2N(\tilde A)=\lambda$, so the number of roots of the central value $\lambda\neq0$ on the line is two when $N(\tilde A)\neq0$ and zero when $N(\tilde A)=0$; the roots of $0$ on the line are the line minus the origin when $N(\tilde A)=0$, and there are none when $N(\tilde A)\neq0$, the origin being excluded as a root and the equation $t^2N(\tilde A)=0$ then reading $t=0$.

Proof. $N(t\tilde A)=t^2N(\tilde A)$ by the homogeneity of the diagonal; for $\lambda\neq0$ and $N(\tilde A)\neq0$ the two values $t=\pm\sqrt{\lambda/N(\tilde A)}$ are nonzero and distinct, and for $N(\tilde A)=0$ the equation $0=\lambda\neq0$ is impossible; for $\lambda=0$ the equation is $t^2N(\tilde A)=0$, solved by every $t\neq0$ when $N(\tilde A)=0$ and by no nonzero $t$ otherwise, the origin being excluded as a root.

Corollary (the root set of a central value, restated). For $\lambda\neq0$ the root set is stable under $\tilde\Xi\mapsto-\tilde\Xi$ and under the natural conjugation, which preserve $N$, while the star and the Hermitian conjugation carry it onto the root set of $\overline{\lambda}$; and it contains the two central roots $\pm\mu e_0$ with $\mu^2=\lambda$. For $\lambda=0$ the root set is the punctured isotropic cone, and its elements are exactly the square-zero elements, the roots of $0$ of the block.

The Classification

The square-root equation has exactly two cases: for a central radicand $\tilde Q=\lambda e_0$ the root set is $\{\tilde\Xi:N(\tilde\Xi)=\lambda\}$, nonempty and of complex dimension three, equal to the cone minus the origin when $\lambda=0$; for a non-central radicand there is no root, and no case has a finite nonempty root set.

Theorem (the two cases). Let $\tilde Q\in\mathbb{B}$. The square-root equation of the block has

  1. for a central radicand $\tilde Q=\lambda e_0$: the root set $\{\tilde\Xi:N(\tilde\Xi)=\lambda\}$, nonempty, of complex dimension three, and equal to the cone minus the origin when $\lambda=0$;
  2. for a non-central radicand: no root at all.

There is no third case, and there is no case in which the root set is a finite nonempty set: the equation is one scalar quadratic equation in four unknowns and its solution set is a three-dimensional quadric, or empty.

Proof. A root satisfies $\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=N(\tilde\Xi)e_0$, which is central; comparing with $\tilde Q$ gives the two cases, and the first is the theorem of the preceding section. The dimension is the one of the zero set in $\mathbb{C}^4$ of one non-degenerate quadratic equation; the set is never a finite nonempty set because a non-degenerate quadratic in four variables has a three-dimensional zero set off its singular cases, and the singular case is the cone $\lambda=0$.

Remark (the collapse and what causes it). The parent's classification separates the radicands by the scalar part and treats the non-central radicands by a reduction to a scalar equation with a vector unknown (Square Roots of a General Element in the General Plain Algebra). The block needs none of that machinery, and the reason is structural: the product is central-valued, so the square map loses the vector part entirely, and there is no associativity and no unit with which to reconstruct an element from its square. The collapse is the shadow of the centrality of the block, the same property that makes it a rank-one pairing.

The Worked Examples

Example (a non-central radicand). Let $\tilde Q=e_1$. The equation $\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=e_1$ has no solution, since a square of the block is central and $e_1$ is not. In the parent plain algebra the same equation has solutions (Square Roots of a General Element in the General Plain Algebra), and the comparison is the sharpest witness of the collapse.

Example (a central radicand, $\lambda=1$). The roots are the level set $N=1$: the four basis elements, the elements $\pm\mu e_0$ with $\mu^2=1$, and the hyperquadric computed in Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the Symmetric Quaternionic Algebra.

Example (an imaginary radicand). Let $\tilde Q=ie_0$, that is $\lambda=i$. A square root of $i$ in $\mathbb{C}$ is $\mu=e^{i\pi/4}$, and $\tilde\Xi=\mu e_0$ is a root: $N(\mu e_0)=\mu^2=i$. The root set is the quadric $\{\tilde\Xi:Q_0^2+(\vec\Xi,\vec\Xi)=i\}$, of complex dimension three, and it contains the non-central elements as well: for instance any $\tilde\Xi=\Xi_0e_0+\vec\Xi$ with $\Xi_0$ a square root of $i-(\vec\Xi,\vec\Xi)$ is a root.

Example (the radicand zero). The roots of $0$ are the punctured isotropic cone, $\{N=0\}\setminus\{0\}$: for instance $e_0+ie_1$, with $\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=0$ and $\tilde\Xi\neq0$. The element $0$ is not a root, by the definition of a root, and it is the only element whose square is $0$ and that is excluded from the root set.

Summary

In the symmetric quaternionic block the square of every element is central, $\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=N(\tilde\Xi)e_0$, so the square-root problem has a solution only for the central radicands $\tilde Q=\lambda e_0$, and for those the root set is the level set $\{\tilde\Xi:N(\tilde\Xi)=\lambda\}$: the punctured isotropic cone for $\lambda=0$, an affine hyperquadric of complex dimension three for $\lambda\neq0$, nonempty in both cases, the sign and the natural conjugation preserving each level set while the star and the Hermitian conjugation exchange the level sets of $\lambda$ and of $\overline{\lambda}$. A non-central radicand has no root, the case of the general theory with a non-vanishing vector part does not occur, and the classification therefore has exactly two cases with three-dimensional solution sets and no finite nonempty case. The three special values $\lambda=-1,0,1$ are treated in the companion articles Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the Symmetric Quaternionic Algebra and Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents of the Symmetric Quaternionic Algebra; the parent's general classification, which the block collapses, is the model article of the same slot in the General Plain Algebra.
