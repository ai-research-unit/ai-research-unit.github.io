Zero Divisors of the General Plain Sesqualgebra

Introduction

The general plain sesqualgebra is the operation $\tilde Q_1\star\tilde Q_2=\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2^{*}$ on the biquaternion space (Introduction to the General Plain Sesqualgebra). This article studies its zero divisors: the nonzero elements that annihilate a nonzero element.

A zero divisor names a product, and the definition is relative; it is stated once for a general product in Definitions for the Study of the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space and is read here on the block. The criterion of the block is one element: because the involution ${}^{*}$ is a bijection of $\mathbb{B}$, the vanishing of $\tilde Q_1\star\tilde Q_2=\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2^{*}$ is the vanishing of the plain product $\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2^{*}$, and $\tilde Q_2^{*}$ ranges over all of $\mathbb{B}$ as $\tilde Q_2$ does, so the block's zero-divisor set is exactly the plain one, the nonzero elements of the zero-divisor cone $\{C(\tilde Q)=\sum_\mu Q_\mu^{2}=0\}$, which is also the common set of the four general products (Zero Divisors in the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space). The set is therefore inherited unchanged; what is not inherited is the internal structure the parent reads on it. The parent reads a square, and its pure family is the nonzero nilpotents of index two; the block reads its own square, the Hermitian element $\tilde Q\star\tilde Q=\tilde Q\tilde Q^{*}$, on which the pure family is not square-zero, and it is the pure family that is the block's nilpotent family, with the index three instead of two (Nilpotents of the General Plain Sesqualgebra). The article states the coincidence, computes the annihilators, and separates the part of the classification that survives from the part that does not.

Placement. The article is the third entry of the Common Theory section of the block, after Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Sesqualgebra and before the square roots of the central values. It uses the product and the class of the introduction, the algebraic norm and the invertibility criterion of Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Sesqualgebra, and the common zero-divisor set of the four general products.

Boundaries. The product, its class and its placement are Introduction to the General Plain Sesqualgebra of Biquaternions; the relative definitions of the zero divisor, the annihilator and the regular element are Definitions for the Study of the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space; the common set of the four general products and the theorem that the four coincide are Zero Divisors in the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space, and the same set read on the plain product with its two families and their geometry is Zero Divisors of the General Plain Algebra, Idempotents of the General Plain Algebra and Nilpotents of the General Plain Algebra. The algebraic norm and the invertibility criterion are Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Sesqualgebra; the square-zero set of the block is Nilpotents in the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternioid $\mathbb{C}$ Space. Nothing topological and nothing metric appears.

Conventions. $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$ has basis $e_t,\vec e_x,\vec e_y,\vec e_z$ with $\vec e_x\vec e_y=\vec e_z$ and central scalar imaginary $i$. A general element is $\tilde Q=\sum_{\mu\in\{t,x,y,z\}}Q_\mu e_\mu=Q_te_t+\vec Q$ with $\vec Q=Q_x\vec e_x+Q_y\vec e_y+Q_z\vec e_z$ and $Q_\mu\in\mathbb{C}$; the natural conjugation is $\tilde Q^{\natural}=Q_t-\vec Q$, the coefficientwise conjugation is $\overline{\tilde Q}=\overline{Q_t}e_t+\overline{\vec Q}$, and the Hermitian conjugation is $\tilde Q^{*}=\overline{\tilde Q^{\natural}}=\overline{Q_t}-\overline{\vec Q}$. Juxtaposition is the plain product and $\star$ the block, $\tilde Q_1\star\tilde Q_2=\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2^{*}$. The central square is $C(\tilde Q)=\tilde Q\tilde Q^{\natural}=Q_t^{2}+(\vec Q,\vec Q)$ with $(\vec Q,\vec Q)=Q_x^{2}+Q_y^{2}+Q_z^{2}$, and its vanishing set, the origin removed, is the common zero-divisor cone.

The results. A nonzero biquaternion is a zero divisor of the block $\tilde Q_1\star\tilde Q_2=\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2^{*}$ exactly when its central square vanishes, $C(\tilde Q_1)=Q_{1t}^{2}+(\vec Q_1,\vec Q_1)=0$; the block's zero-divisor set is therefore the parent's, the nonzero complex cone of complex dimension three, and it is the common set of the four general products. Every zero divisor of the block is two-sided, and the two annihilators coincide, each a complex plane of complex dimension two on the cone and $\{0\}$ off it. The parent's partition by the scalar coordinate survives: the pure family ($Q_{1t}=0$, isotropic) is the block's nilpotent family, of index three, and the non-pure family ($Q_{1t}\neq0$) consists of the complex multiples of the nontrivial idempotents, among which exactly the Hermitian idempotents $\tfrac12(e_t+\tilde\Xi)$ are idempotents of the block.

The Criterion

Theorem (the criterion of the block). For nonzero $\tilde Q_1$, the following are equivalent:

  1. $\tilde Q_1$ is a left zero divisor of $\star$: there is a nonzero $\tilde Q_2$ with $\tilde Q_1\star\tilde Q_2=0$; 2. $\tilde Q_1$ is a right zero divisor of $\star$: there is a nonzero $\tilde Q_2$ with $\tilde Q_2\star\tilde Q_1=0$; 3. $\tilde Q_1$ is a zero divisor of the plain product: there is a nonzero $\tilde Q_2$ with $\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2=0$; 4. $C(\tilde Q_1)=0$.

Proof. The equation $\tilde Q_1\star\tilde Q_2=\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2^{*}=0$ is, because ${}^{*}$ is a bijection, the existence of a nonzero $\tilde Q_2^{*}$ with $\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2^{*}=0$, which is (3). The right equation is the same: $\tilde Q_2\star\tilde Q_1=\tilde Q_2\tilde Q_1^{*}=0$, and applying ${}^{*}$ gives $\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2^{*}=0$ again, which is (1). The equivalence of (3) and (4) is the invertibility criterion of the parent algebra, reproduced in Zero Divisors of the General Plain Algebra: $\tilde Q$ is a zero divisor of the plain product exactly when $C(\tilde Q)=0$.

Corollary (the set). The zero-divisor set of the block is

$$ \mathcal{Z}_\star=\{\tilde Q\neq0: C(\tilde Q)=0\}=\{\tilde Q\neq0: Q_t^{2}+(\vec Q,\vec Q)=0\}, $$

the nonzero elements of the complex cone $\mathcal{C}$, of complex dimension three; every zero divisor of the block is two-sided, and the set is the common zero-divisor set of the four general products.

Proof. The criterion gives the set, and the equivalence of the one-sided statements makes every member two-sided. The cone is the zero set of a non-degenerate quadratic form on $\mathbb{C}^{4}$, hence of complex dimension three, and it is the common set by Zero Divisors in the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space, §The Four General Products Coincide.

Remark (the criterion of the block is one element, like the parent's). Unlike the symmetric plain sesqualgebra, whose criterion is a bilinear equation in the element and its partner, the block's criterion is a single quadratic equation $C(\tilde Q_1)=0$ in the element alone, and the reason is that its annihilation equation $\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2^{*}=0$ still has the element alone on the left. The block changes the square and not the shape of the criterion, and its zero divisors are a cone.

The Two Annihilators

The annihilator of an element is the kernel of the left or the right multiplication, the two coinciding because the star is an anti-automorphism of order two, with the common annihilator the conjugate of the plain annihilator. The annihilators, the plain reading, the rank table and the reading on the remarkable subspaces are the companion article Annihilators of the General Plain Sesqualgebra, below this article in this category.

The Two Families, and What the Block Does to Them

The parent's partition by the scalar coordinate survives: the pure family ($Q_t=0$, isotropic) is the block's nilpotent family, of index three rather than two, and the non-pure family ($Q_t\neq0$) is the nonzero complex multiples of the nontrivial idempotents, among which exactly the Hermitian idempotents $\tfrac12(e_t+\tilde\Xi)$ are idempotents of the block.

The plain product partitions its zero divisors by the scalar coordinate (Zero Divisors of the General Plain Algebra): the pure family has $Q_t=0$ and $(\vec Q,\vec Q)=0$, and its nonzero members are the nilpotents, $\tilde Q^{2}=0$; the non-pure family has $Q_t\neq0$, and its members are the nonzero complex multiples of the nontrivial idempotents $\tfrac12(e_t+\tilde\Xi i)$ over the roots $\tilde\Xi$ of $-1$. The partition is by the scalar coordinate, which is a datum of the element and not of the product, so it survives verbatim for the block; what does not survive is the reading of the two families through the square.

Proposition (the pure family is not square-zero for the block). Let $\tilde Q$ be a nonzero pure zero divisor, $\tilde Q=\vec Q$ with $(\vec Q,\vec Q)=0$. Then its plain square vanishes, but its block-square does not:

$$ \tilde Q^{2}=0,\qquad \tilde Q\star\tilde Q=\tilde Q\tilde Q^{*}=\mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde Q\tilde Q^{*}\bigr)e_t+\mathrm{Vect}\bigl(\tilde Q\tilde Q^{*}\bigr)\neq0 . $$

Proof. The plain square vanishes because $\tilde Q^{2}=(\vec Q,\vec Q)e_t$ by the product rule and $(\vec Q,\vec Q)=0$. For the block-square, $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde Q\tilde Q^{*})=\sum_\mu\lvert Q_\mu\rvert^{2}=\langle\tilde Q\rangle_{*}$, the algebraic norm of the block, which is positive for $\tilde Q\neq0$ by the definiteness of Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Sesqualgebra; so the block-square is nonzero. The witness $\tilde Q=\vec e_x+i\vec e_y$ has $\tilde Q\star\tilde Q=2e_t+2i\vec e_z$.

Remark (the index of the pure family is raised). The parent calls the pure family the nonzero nilpotents of index two; the block calls the same family its nonzero nilpotents of index three, because the block-square of a pure zero divisor does not vanish and the third power does (Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents of the General Plain Sesqualgebra). The pure zero divisors are therefore a family of zero divisors that are nilpotents of the block, with the index raised from two to three, and the non-pure zero divisors are the family that is not nilpotent. The partition of the parent is kept, and the index attached to each family is read from the square of the product in force.

Proposition (the non-pure family and the idempotents of the block). The non-pure zero divisors are the nonzero complex multiples of the nontrivial idempotents of the plain product, and among them exactly the Hermitian idempotents $\tfrac12(e_t+\tilde\Xi)$ over the real unit vectors $\vec u$ are idempotents of the block; the others are zero divisors of the block and not idempotents of it.

Proof. The plain description is Zero Divisors of the Biquaternion Algebra. An idempotent of the block is a Hermitian idempotent of the plain product (Idempotents of the General Plain Sesqualgebra), and a Hermitian idempotent $\tilde\Pi$ has $\tilde\Pi\star\tilde\Pi=\tilde\Pi\tilde\Pi^{*}=\tilde\Pi^{2}=\tilde\Pi$; the Hermitian ones are those with a real root $\tilde\Xi=\pm\vec u$, that is the family $\tfrac12(e_t+\tilde\Xi)$. A multiple $\lambda\tilde\Pi$ with $\lambda\neq1$ is a zero divisor but is idempotent for neither product unless $\lambda=1$.

Remark (the scalar part of a block idempotent is fixed). The block idempotents $\tfrac12(e_t+\tilde\Xi)$ have scalar part $\tfrac12$, which is the positive value the algebraic norm $\langle\tilde\Pi\rangle_{*}=\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde\Pi\star\tilde\Pi)=\sum_\mu\lvert\Pi_\mu\rvert^{2}$ forces on any idempotent of the block; in particular $C\bigl(\tfrac12(e_t+\tilde\Xi)\bigr)=\tfrac14(1-(\vec u,\vec u))=0$, so every block idempotent is a zero divisor, of the non-pure family. The unit $e_t$ is not a zero divisor, $c(e_t)=1$.

The Comparison with the Parent and with the Four General Products

The block shares the criterion $C(\tilde Q)=0$, the nonzero cone, the two-sidedness and the pure family with the parent and with the four general products, and it changes only the square-theoretic index of the pure family, from two to three.

The block's set is the common set of the four general products, and the coincidence is a theorem of the space and not a property of the block (Zero Divisors in the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space). What the block adds is the reading of the set through its own square: the scalar-coordinate partition is inherited, because it is a partition of the elements, and the index attached to the pure family is read from the square in force, so it becomes three in place of two. The four general products share the set, the pure family and the criterion $Q_t=0$; they do not share the square, and the index of nilpotency is the visible consequence.

Summary

The zero divisors of the general plain sesqualgebra are decided by one equation in the element alone, and the equation is the parent's: because $\tilde Q_1\star\tilde Q_2=\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2^{*}$ and ${}^{*}$ is a bijection, an element is a zero divisor of the block exactly when it is a zero divisor of the plain product, that is exactly when its central square vanishes, $C(\tilde Q)=Q_t^{2}+(\vec Q,\vec Q)=0$ with $\tilde Q\neq0$. The set is the nonzero zero-divisor cone, of complex dimension three, which is the common set of the four general products, and every member is two-sided. The annihilators are equal on the two sides and are the image under the involution of the plain annihilator $\langle\tilde Q_1\rangle_{\natural*}=\{\tilde Q_2:\tilde Q_1\tilde Q_2=0\}$, a complex plane of dimension two for every nonzero zero divisor and the origin otherwise; the two-sided annihilator is $\{0\}$. The two families of the parent are inherited as a partition of the elements, since the partition is read on the scalar coordinate: the pure family has $Q_t=0$ and isotropic vector part, and the non-pure family consists of the nonzero complex multiples of the nontrivial idempotents. What changes is the square-theoretic reading: a pure zero divisor is a nilpotent of index two for the parent and a nilpotent of index three for the block, its block-square being the nonzero Hermitian element $\tilde Q\tilde Q^{*}$ with positive scalar part; the non-pure zero divisors are nilpotent for neither; and of the non-pure multiples, exactly the Hermitian idempotents $\tfrac12(e_t+\tilde\Xi)$ are idempotents of the block. The block keeps the set, keeps the criterion, the partition and the annihilators, and changes the index.
