Biquaternion Anti-Quaternion Subspace

Introduction

The anti-quaternion subspace $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ is the anti-fixed space of complex conjugation: the elements of $\mathbb{B}$ all of whose four complex coefficients are purely imaginary. It is the multiple of the quaternion subspace by the central imaginary unit, and together the two subspaces give the quaternion decomposition $\mathbb{B} = \mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}} \oplus i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ of the algebra into its real and imaginary coefficient parts. Like the quaternion subspace it is four-dimensional and free of zero divisors, but its biquaternion norm is negative definite rather than positive definite, and it is not a subalgebra: the product of two of its elements lands in the quaternion subspace.

The article follows the plan of its companions, including a final section on the place of the subspace among the particular cases of the algebra. All statements are algebraic; the only coordinates used are the coefficients $Q_\mu = q_\mu + iq'_\mu$.

Definition and Basis

The Defining Involution

Definition. The anti-quaternion subspace is the anti-fixed space of complex conjugation,

$$ i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}} = \left\{ \tilde{Q} \in \mathbb{B} : \tilde{Q}^{*} = -\tilde{Q} \right\} , $$

with $\tilde{Q}^{*} = Q_0^{*}e_0 + Q_1^{*}e_1 + Q_2^{*}e_2 + Q_3^{*}e_3$.

The Condition in Coordinates

Comparing $\tilde{Q}^{*} = -\tilde{Q}$ coefficient by coefficient gives $Q_\mu^{*} = -Q_\mu$ for each $\mu$, so every coefficient is purely imaginary:

$$ \tilde{Q} = i q'_0 e_0 + i q'_1 e_1 + i q'_2 e_2 + i q'_3 e_3 = i\left(q'_0e_0 + q'_1e_1 + q'_2e_2 + q'_3e_3\right) , \qquad q'_\mu \in \mathbb{R} . $$

The bracket is a real quaternion, so the subspace is exactly the set of products $i\tilde{P}$ with $\tilde{P} \in \mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$, which is the reason for the notation. In terms of the eight real coordinates it is

$$ i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}} = \left\{ \tilde{Q} : q_0 = q_1 = q_2 = q_3 = 0 \right\} . $$

Basis and Dimension

Proposition. $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ is a real vector space of dimension $4$, with basis $ie_0, ie_1, ie_2, ie_3$, and it decomposes along the two coordinate blocks it meets,

$$ i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}} = i\mathbb{R}e_0 \oplus \operatorname{span}\{ie_1, ie_2, ie_3\} . $$

Proof. Four imaginary coefficients are four real parameters; the four basis elements are independent over $\mathbb{R}$.

The subspace is the image of the quaternion subspace under multiplication by $i$, and that multiplication exchanges the two coordinate blocks: the scalar line $\mathbb{R}e_0$ of $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ is sent to $i\mathbb{R}e_0$, and the pure imaginary triple $\operatorname{span}\{e_1,e_2,e_3\}$ to $\operatorname{span}\{ie_1,ie_2,ie_3\}$.

Algebra and Module Structure

It Is Not a Subalgebra

Proposition. The product of two elements of $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ lies in $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$, and in particular not in $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ unless it vanishes:

$$ (i\tilde{P})(i\tilde{Q}) = -\tilde{P}\tilde{Q} , \qquad \tilde{P}, \tilde{Q} \in \mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}} . $$

Proof. The central unit $i$ is multiplied out and $\tilde{P}, \tilde{Q}$ are real quaternions, so their product lies in the quaternion subspace.

In particular $(ie_k)^2 = i^2e_k^2 = +e_0$ for $k = 1,2,3$: the three imaginary vector units are roots of plus one, not of minus one. The single element $ie_0$ satisfies $(ie_0)^2 = -e_0$ and is the only root of minus one in the subspace.

It Is a Two-Sided Module over the Quaternion Subspace

Proposition. For real quaternions $\tilde{P}, \tilde{Q}$,

$$ (i\tilde{P})\tilde{Q} = i(\tilde{P}\tilde{Q}) \in i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}} , \qquad \tilde{Q}(i\tilde{P}) = i(\tilde{Q}\tilde{P}) \in i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}} , $$

so $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ is a two-sided module over $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$, and as such it is free of rank one, generated by $i$. It is also a module over the centre subspace, which lies in the quaternion subspace and acts by scalar extension.

Proof. The central element $i$ commutes with every element, so it can be moved outside the products; both products are therefore of the form $i(\cdot)$ with a real quaternion in the bracket.

The statement is the module-theoretic face of the non-closure: the subspace is not stable under being multiplied into itself, but it is stable under multiplication into it by the quaternion subspace from either side.

Powers and Squares

Proposition. For $\tilde{Q} = i\tilde{P}$ with $\tilde{P} = p_0e_0 + \mathbf{p} \in \mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ one has

$$ \tilde{Q}^2 = -\tilde{P}^2 = -\left(p_0^2 - |\mathbf{p}|^2\right)e_0 - 2p_0\mathbf{p} , $$

so the square of a subspace element is minus the square of a real quaternion: it has the same vector direction as $\mathbf{p}$ and the opposite scalar part. For the imaginary vectors, $\tilde{Q} = i\mathbf{p}$ with $p_0 = 0$, the square is central,

$$ (i\mathbf{p})^2 = |\mathbf{p}|^2 e_0 = -N(i\mathbf{p})\, e_0 , $$

of the same form as the square of a real vector.

Proof. Centrality of $i$ gives $(i\tilde{P})^2 = i^2\tilde{P}^2 = -\tilde{P}^2$; the developed form is the square of a quaternion, and the pure case has $p_0 = 0$ together with $N(i\tilde{P}) = -N(\tilde{P}) = -|\mathbf{p}|^2$.

The Biquaternion Norm

Theorem. On the anti-quaternion subspace the biquaternion norm is the negative definite quadratic form

$$ N(\tilde{Q}) = -\left((q'_0)^2 + (q'_1)^2 + (q'_2)^2 + (q'_3)^2\right) \leq 0 , \qquad N(\tilde{Q}) = 0 \iff \tilde{Q} = 0 , $$

and it is related to the norm of the corresponding real quaternion by $N(i\tilde{P}) = -N(\tilde{P})$.

Proof. Substituting $Q_\mu = iq'_\mu$ in $N(\tilde{Q}) = \sum_\mu Q_\mu^2$ gives $\sum_\mu (iq'_\mu)^2 = -\sum_\mu (q'_\mu)^2$; the multiplicativity of $N$ and the centrality of $i$ give the second statement.

The subspace is thus the exact negative counterpart of the quaternion subspace: it is one of the four subspaces on which the biquaternion norm is real, and the only one on which it is negative definite. Its real restriction has signature $(0,4)$ in the basis $ie_0, ie_1, ie_2, ie_3$.

Units and Zero Divisors

Corollary. Every non-zero element of $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ is a unit, with

$$ (i\tilde{P})^{-1} = -i\,\frac{\bar{\tilde{P}}}{N(\tilde{P})} , $$

which is again an element of $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$. The group of units of the subspace is $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}\setminus\{0\}$, which retracts onto the sphere $S^3$ of the elements of norm $-1$.

Proof. The inverse formula is $\tilde{Q}^{-1} = \bar{\tilde{Q}}/N(\tilde{Q})$; with $\tilde{Q} = i\tilde{P}$ one has $\bar{\tilde{Q}} = i\bar{\tilde{P}}$ and $N(\tilde{Q}) = -N(\tilde{P})$, giving the displayed expression, in which $i$ is held by a real quaternion and the result is therefore in the subspace.

Corollary. The anti-quaternion subspace has no zero divisors, and its only idempotent is $0$.

Proof. The biquaternion norm vanishes only at the origin, which gives the absence of zero divisors by the general criterion. For the idempotents, suppose $(i\tilde{P})^2 = i\tilde{P}$; the square is $-\tilde{P}^2$, which lies in the quaternion subspace, while $i\tilde{P}$ lies in the anti-quaternion subspace; the two subspaces intersect only at the origin, so $\tilde{P} = 0$.

The first corollary is what distinguishes the subspace from the sectors and from the vector subspace: like the quaternion subspace and unlike them, it contains no null elements, so every non-zero element is invertible and the subspace contributes no zero divisors to the algebra.

The Roots of Plus One and of Minus One

Proposition. Inside $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$,

$$ \left\{ \tilde{Q} : \tilde{Q}^2 = e_0 \right\} = \left\{ i\mathbf{p} : \mathbf{p} \in \operatorname{span}\{e_1,e_2,e_3\}, \ |\mathbf{p}| = 1 \right\} , \qquad \left\{ \tilde{Q} : \tilde{Q}^2 = -e_0 \right\} = \left\{ ie_0, -ie_0 \right\} . $$

The roots of plus one fill a two-sphere; the roots of minus one are exactly two points.

Proof. Write $\tilde{Q} = i\tilde{P}$. The equation $\tilde{Q}^2 = e_0$ is $-\tilde{P}^2 = e_0$, that is $\tilde{P}^2 = -e_0$, which holds exactly for the pure real unit vectors; the equation $\tilde{Q}^2 = -e_0$ is $\tilde{P}^2 = e_0$, which holds exactly for $\tilde{P} = \pm e_0$, since the scalar and vector parts of $\tilde{P}^2$ must match those of $e_0$.

The asymmetry is worth noting because it is the opposite of the situation in the quaternion subspace, where the roots of minus one fill a two-sphere and $e_0$ is the only square root of plus one: multiplication by $i$ interchanges the two problems, $i\tilde{P}$ squaring to $-\tilde{P}^2$.

The Four Involutions on It

In the basis $ie_0, ie_1, ie_2, ie_3$:

involution matrix effect
$\bar{\cdot}$ $\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$ quaternion conjugation, leaving the subspace
${}^{*}$ $-\mathrm{id}$ minus the identity, by definition of the subspace
${}^{\dagger}$ $\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,1,1)$ minus quaternion conjugation
$\flat$ $\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$ as quaternion conjugation

The subspace is invariant under all four. Complex conjugation acts as the negative of the identity, which is its defining property; quaternion conjugation acts on it as it acts on the quaternion subspace, negating the three imaginary vector directions and fixing the central imaginary direction; Hermitian conjugation is the negative of that; and reversal coincides with quaternion conjugation. The last two rows are consistent with $\dagger = {}^{*}\circ\bar{\cdot}$: on the subspace, minus the identity composed with quaternion conjugation is minus quaternion conjugation.

$i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}} \cap \mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ $\{0\}$ $0$
$i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}} \cap \mathbb{M}_+$ $\operatorname{span}\{ie_1,ie_2,ie_3\}$ $3$
$i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}} \cap \mathbb{M}_-$ $i\mathbb{R}e_0$ $1$

The subspace is complementary to the quaternion subspace, and its decomposition along the coordinate blocks is $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}} = i\mathbb{R}e_0 \oplus \operatorname{span}\{ie_1,ie_2,ie_3\}$, in which the first line is shared with the anti-Hermitian subspace and the triple with the Hermitian one. Its sums with the centre and with $\mathbb{M}_+$ have dimension $5$, and its sums with the vector subspace and with $\mathbb{M}_-$ have dimension $7$; only the pair with $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ spans the algebra.

The Subspace in the Algebra

The section places the subspace among the particular cases that the Algebra articles treat one by one — the idempotents, the ideals and the Peirce decomposition, the roots of minus one, the zero divisors and the commutator bracket — each stated for the subspace and referred to its article.

Idempotents

Proposition. The only idempotent of $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ is $0$.

Proof. If $(i\tilde{P})^2 = i\tilde{P}$ then $-\tilde{P}^2 = i\tilde{P}$; the left side lies in $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ and the right in $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$, whose intersection is the origin, so $\tilde{P} = 0$.

The subspace shares with the vector subspace the distinction of carrying no idempotent other than the origin — not even the unit $e_0$, which is not in it — while the centre and the quaternion subspace carry $0$ and $e_0$. The idempotent theory reaches it only through the central unit: multiplication by $i$ carries a Hermitian idempotent $\tilde\Pi$ to $i\tilde\Pi \in i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$, which satisfies $(i\tilde\Pi)^2 = -\tilde\Pi$ and is not an idempotent but a zero divisor, below.

Ideals and the Peirce Decomposition

$i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ is not a subalgebra: the product of two of its elements is $i^2\tilde{P}\tilde{R} = -\tilde{P}\tilde{R}$, which lies in $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$. Since an ideal is in particular closed under products of its own elements, the subspace is no one-sided ideal, and, being a proper non-zero subspace of the simple algebra $\mathbb{B}$, it is no two-sided ideal either. It is the natural module of the quaternion decomposition: it is a free two-sided module of rank one over the division algebra $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$, generated by $i$, and

$$ \mathbb{B} = \mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}} \oplus i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}} $$

exhibits $\mathbb{B}$ as a free module of rank two over $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ with basis $e_0, i$.

The Peirce decomposition is not adapted to the subspace: its primitive idempotents and its nilpotent corners lie in $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ and $\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{B})$. Multiplication by $i$ exchanges the two remaining sectors, $i\mathbb{M}_+ = \mathbb{M}_-$ and $i\mathbb{M}_- = \mathbb{M}_+$, and exchanges $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ with $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$; and complex conjugation acts on $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ as minus the identity, interchanging the two standard minimal left ideals. So the subspace marks the companion half of the real structure rather than a piece of the ideal lattice.

The Roots of Minus One

Proposition. The roots of $-1$ in $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ are exactly the two elements $\pm ie_0$.

Proof. For $\tilde{Q} = i\tilde{P}$ one has $\tilde{Q}^2 = -\tilde{P}^2$, so $\tilde{Q}^2 = -e_0$ if and only if $\tilde{P}^2 = e_0$; in the division algebra $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ the roots of $+1$ are $\pm e_0$.

These are the two trivial roots $\pm i$ of Biquaternion Roots of Minus One, which the subspace shares with the centre; the two-sphere of real roots and the four-real-dimensional family of non-trivial roots lie outside it. The subspace carries instead the mirror family: the elements

$$ \eta = i\mu, \qquad \mu \in \operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{e_1,e_2,e_3\}, \quad |\mu| = 1 , $$

satisfy $\eta^2 = +e_0$ and are the roots of $+1$ of the subspace, filling a two-sphere in exact exchange with the two-sphere of roots of $-1$ in $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$. This is the two spheres asymmetry of the pair of subspaces: the roots of one equation in each subspace form a sphere, and the roots of the other equation form two points.

Zero Divisors

Proposition. $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ contains no zero divisor; it is the third of the three subspaces free of them.

Proof. For $\tilde{Q} = i\tilde{P}$ the norm is $N(\tilde{Q}) = -N(\tilde{P})$, which is the negative definite sum of four squares and vanishes only at the origin; the criterion of Biquaternion Zero Divisors makes every non-zero element a unit.

So the subspace contains no null element, and in the distribution of that article it completes, with the centre and the quaternion subspace, the three subspaces free of zero divisors; the vector subspace and the two sectors carry the null sets.

The Lie Algebra Structure

The subspace is not closed under the bracket. For $i\tilde{P}, i\tilde{R} \in i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$, since $i$ is central,

$$ [i\tilde{P}, i\tilde{R}] = i^2[\tilde{P},\tilde{R}] = -[\tilde{P},\tilde{R}] \in \operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{e_1,e_2,e_3\} = \mathrm{K}, $$

because $[\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}},\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}] = \mathrm{K}$; so the bracket carries $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ into the rotation subalgebra of the quaternion subspace, not into the subspace itself. As a real vector space the subspace splits as

$$ i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}} = i\mathbb{R}e_0 \oplus \operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{ie_1,ie_2,ie_3\}, $$

the first summand the central imaginary line $i\mathbb{R}e_0 = \mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{B}} \cap i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ and the second the hyperbolic directions $\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{ie_1,ie_2,ie_3\} = i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}} \cap \mathbb{M}_+$. The central line is an abelian Lie subalgebra, the hyperbolic directions are not, and neither is the whole subspace. The details are in Biquaternion Lie Algebra.

Examples

The Imaginary Units and Their Squares

The four basis elements behave differently under squaring:

$$ (ie_0)^2 = -e_0 , \qquad (ie_1)^2 = (ie_2)^2 = (ie_3)^2 = +e_0 . $$

So $ie_0$ is a root of minus one and the other three are roots of plus one; all four have norm $-1$ and are units. Inverses follow from the squares: an element with $\tilde{Q}^2 = -e_0$ has $\tilde{Q}^{-1} = -\tilde{Q}$, and an element with $\tilde{Q}^2 = e_0$ has $\tilde{Q}^{-1} = \tilde{Q}$, so

$$ (ie_0)^{-1} = -ie_0 , \qquad (ie_k)^{-1} = ie_k \quad (k = 1,2,3) , $$

and in each case the inverse lies in the subspace, as the corollary requires.

A General Element and Its Matrix

Take $\tilde{Q} = ie_1 + 2ie_2 + 3ie_3$, with $q'_0 = 0$, $q'_1 = 1$, $q'_2 = 2$, $q'_3 = 3$. Its biquaternion norm is

$$ N(\tilde{Q}) = -(1 + 4 + 9) = -14 , \qquad \tilde{Q}^2 = -\tilde{P}^2 = |\mathbf{p}|^2 e_0 = 14\,e_0 , $$

with $\mathbf{p} = e_1 + 2e_2 + 3e_3$. The element is a unit whose biquaternion norm is negative, and its square is the central element $14e_0$: the square of an element of the anti-quaternion subspace has left the subspace, as the product table requires.

The Two Spheres of Roots

The root of minus one $ie_0$ and the sphere of roots of plus one, of which $i(e_1 + e_2)/\sqrt{2}$ is an instance,

$$ \left(\frac{ie_1 + ie_2}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{2}\left((ie_1)^2 + (ie_1)(ie_2) + (ie_2)(ie_1) + (ie_2)^2\right) = \frac{1}{2}\left(e_0 + i^2(e_1e_2 + e_2e_1) + e_0\right) = \frac{1}{2}\left(e_0 - (e_3 - e_3) + e_0\right) = e_0 , $$

exhibit the interchange produced by multiplication by $i$: the two-sphere of roots of minus one in $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ becomes the two-sphere of roots of plus one in $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$, and the pair $\pm e_0$ of roots of plus one in $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ becomes the pair $\pm ie_0$ of roots of minus one in $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$.

A Module Multiplication

For $\tilde{P} = i(e_1 + e_2)$ and the real quaternion $\tilde{Q} = e_1$,

$$ \tilde{P}\tilde{Q} = i(e_1+e_2)e_1 = i(e_1^2 + e_2e_1) = i(-e_0 - e_3) = -ie_0 - ie_3 \in i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}} , $$

which is the module statement in a concrete instance: multiplication of a subspace element by a real quaternion on either side keeps it in the subspace, while the product of two subspace elements leaves it.

Summary

The anti-quaternion subspace $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ is the anti-fixed space of complex conjugation, the set of elements with purely imaginary coefficients, a real vector space of dimension $4$ with basis $ie_0, ie_1, ie_2, ie_3$, equal to the product of the quaternion subspace with the central imaginary unit. It is not a subalgebra: the product of two of its elements lies in $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$, and in particular $(ie_k)^2 = +e_0$ for the three imaginary vector units, so the subspace contains a two-sphere of square roots of plus one and exactly the two square roots $\pm ie_0$ of minus one. It is a free two-sided module of rank one over the quaternion subspace, generated by $i$. Its biquaternion norm is the negative definite form $-\sum_\mu (q'_\mu)^2$, so every non-zero element is a unit and there are no zero divisors; its only idempotent is $0$. Complex conjugation acts as minus the identity, quaternion conjugation and reversal coincide on the subspace, and Hermitian conjugation is their negative. It is complementary to the quaternion subspace, its intersection with the vector subspace is the triple of imaginary vectors, and its decomposition along the coordinate blocks is $i\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}} = i\mathbb{R}e_0 \oplus \operatorname{span}\{ie_1,ie_2,ie_3\}$. In the algebra of particular cases the subspace carries only the trivial idempotent $0$, it is a free rank-one module over the quaternion subspace and no ideal of $\mathbb{B}$, its only roots of minus one are the two trivial points $\pm ie_0$ while a two-sphere of roots of plus one fills it, it contains no zero divisor, and the bracket carries it into the rotation subalgebra $\mathrm{K}$ of the quaternion subspace.
