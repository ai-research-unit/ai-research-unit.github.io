Matrix Representations : The Centraliser and the Double Centraliser

Introduction

A matrix has a commutant: the set of matrices that commute with it. The commutant is small for a non-scalar matrix and large for a scalar one. The double commutant returns the algebra generated by the matrix in every case. In the regular model there are two further objects: the commutant of the single regular matrix, which contains the mixing of the two spinor planes, and the commutant of the whole left image, which is the right image. The three objects have different dimensions, and the differences are the subject of this article.

This article computes the commutant and the double commutant in the $2\times2$ model and in the $4\times4$ regular model, the relative commutant inside the image, and the double centraliser of the image. It is the article in which the multiplicity of the regular model is separated from the algebra of the element.

Conventions. The algebra and the displays are those of Matrix Representations : The Models and the Change of Frame. The element is $\tilde Q=Q_te_t+\vec Q$, its $2\times2$ matrix is $A=\mathsf M_2(\tilde Q)$, and its regular matrix is $M=\mathsf M_4^{L}(\tilde Q)=I_2\otimes A$ in the Peirce frame. The commutant of $A$ is $$ C(A)=\{X\in M_2(\mathbb{C}):XA=AX\}, $$ and the algebra generated by $A$ is $\mathbb{C}[A]$. The left and right images are $M_4(\mathbb{C})_{\mathbb{B}_L}$ and $M_4(\mathbb{C})_{\mathbb{B}_R}$.

Boundaries. The module-theoretic commutant is The Centraliser; the eigenbasis is Matrix Representations : The Eigenbasis and the Diagonalising Matrix; the Jordan case is Matrix Representations : The Jordan Basis and the Jordanising Matrix; the right regular operators are Matrix Representations : The Right Regular Operators; the Sylvester operator is The Sylvester Operator and the Kronecker Sum of $M_4(\mathbb{C})$.

The Commutant of an Element

The definition. For $X\in M_2(\mathbb{C})$ the commutant, or centraliser, is $$ C(X)=\{Y\in M_2(\mathbb{C}):XY=YX\}. $$ It is a unital subalgebra containing the polynomial algebra $\mathbb{C}[X]$. The double commutant is $C(C(X))$.

The theorem. $$ C(X)= \begin{cases} M_2(\mathbb{C}), & X=\lambda\mathrm I_2,\\[2pt] \mathbb{C}[X]=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{\mathrm I_2,X\}, & X\neq\lambda\mathrm I_2, \end{cases} \qquad C\bigl(C(X)\bigr)=\mathbb{C}[X]. $$ The dimension is $4$ for a scalar and $2$ for a non-scalar.

The three types. The minimal polynomial $m_X$ classifies the non-scalar case.

Type of $X$ $m_X(t)$ $\mathbb{C}[X]$ Dimension
scalar $t-\lambda$ $\mathbb{C}$ $1$
distinct eigenvalues $(t-\lambda_+)(t-\lambda_-)$ $\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}$ $2$
repeated eigenvalue $(t-\lambda)^2$ $\mathbb{C}[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$ $2$

In the distinct case the two factors are the evaluations at the two eigenvalues, and the two spectral projectors are a basis of idempotents. In the repeated case the algebra is the dual numbers, with $\varepsilon=N$ the nilpotent part and $N^2=0$. The scalar case has a one-dimensional polynomial algebra inside a four-dimensional commutant, because every matrix commutes with a scalar.

The proof in the two non-scalar cases. If the eigenvalues are distinct, $X$ is conjugate to $\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_+,\lambda_-)$; a commuting matrix is diagonal, and every diagonal matrix is the interpolation of its two entries at the two eigenvalues. If the eigenvalue is repeated, $X$ is conjugate to $\lambda\mathrm I_2+N$ with $N\neq0$ and $N^2=0$; a matrix commuting with $N=E_{12}$ is upper triangular Toeplitz, $a\mathrm I_2+bN$. In both cases the commutant is commutative and two-dimensional, hence equal to $\mathbb{C}[X]$.

The double commutant in the $2\times2$ model. For a non-scalar matrix, $C(X)=\mathbb{C}[X]$ is maximal commutative, so an element commuting with all of it lies in it. For a scalar, $C(X)=M_2(\mathbb{C})$ and $C(M_2(\mathbb{C}))=\mathbb{C}\mathrm I_2=\mathbb{C}[X]$. The double commutant therefore never enlarges the algebra.

The Commutant in the Regular Model

The theorem. In the Peirce frame, $$ C(M)=\{B\in M_4(\mathbb{C}):B(I_2\otimes A)=(I_2\otimes A)B\} =M_2(\mathbb{C})\otimes C(A). $$

Proof. Write $B$ as a $2\times2$ block matrix with blocks $B_{ij}\in M_2(\mathbb{C})$. The condition $BM=MB$ is $B_{ij}A=AB_{ij}$ for every pair $(i,j)$, so each block lies in $C(A)$.

The dimensions. $$ \dim_{\mathbb{C}}C(M)=4\dim_{\mathbb{C}}C(A)= \begin{cases} 16, & \tilde Q\text{ scalar},\\ 8, & \tilde Q\text{ non-scalar}. \end{cases} $$ The factor $4$ is the dimension of the multiplicity space of the two spinor planes. It is present in $M_4(\mathbb{C})$ and absent from the $2\times2$ model.

The meaning. The tensor factor $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ mixes the two planes arbitrarily, and the factor $C(A)$ acts on the values. When $A$ has distinct eigenvalues, $C(A)$ is the diagonal algebra in the eigenbasis, and $$ C(M)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix} a_1\mathrm I_2&b_1\mathrm I_2\\ c_1\mathrm I_2&d_1\mathrm I_2 \end{pmatrix}:\ a_i,b_i,c_i,d_i\in\mathbb{C}\right\} \cong M_2(\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathbb{C}^2. $$ When $A$ is a Jordan block, $C(A)=\mathbb{C}[N]$ and $$ C(M)=M_2(\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathbb{C}[N] =\{a\mathrm I_4+b(I_2\otimes N)\}, $$ again of dimension eight. The mixing survives; what changes is the value algebra.

The relative commutant. The part of the commutant that lies in the left image is $$ C_L(M)=\{X\in M_4(\mathbb{C})_{\mathbb{B}_L}:XM=MX\} =L\bigl(C(\tilde Q)\bigr). $$ Its dimension is $2$ for a non-scalar element and $4$ for a scalar. The gap between the full commutant and the relative one is six for a non-scalar element and twelve for a scalar; it measures the mixing of the two planes, which is invisible inside the image.

The scalar case. For $A=\lambda\mathrm I_2$, the regular matrix is scalar and every matrix commutes with it: $C(M)=M_4(\mathbb{C})$, of dimension sixteen, while the relative commutant is the four-dimensional image.

The Double Centraliser of the Element

The theorem. $$ C\bigl(C(M)\bigr)=\mathbb{C}[M]=\operatorname{span}\{\mathrm I_4,M\}. $$ The dimension is $1$ for a scalar and $2$ for a non-scalar.

Proof. For non-scalar $A$, $C(M)=M_2(\mathbb{C})\otimes C(A)$ with $C(A)=\mathbb{C}[A]$ commutative. The centraliser of $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ in $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ is the scalar line, and the centraliser of $C(A)$ is $C(A)$ itself, because $C(A)$ is maximal commutative. The centraliser of the tensor product is therefore $$ \mathrm I_2\otimes C(A)=\mathbb{C}[I_2\otimes A]=\mathbb{C}[M]. $$ For scalar $A$, $C(M)=M_4(\mathbb{C})$ and its centraliser is the scalar line, again $\mathbb{C}[M]$.

The comparison with the $2\times2$ model. In both models the double commutant of the element is the algebra generated by it: dimension $2$ for a non-scalar and $1$ for a scalar. The passage to the regular model does not change this algebra, because the two copies are equal and the minimal polynomial does not grow. What grows is the commutant, not its double commutant.

The Double Centraliser of the Image

The right regular matrix. Right multiplication is the other side of the double centraliser. Its matrix in the Peirce frame is $$ \mathsf M_4^{R}(\tilde Q)=\mathsf M_2(\tilde Q)^{\mathsf T}\otimes\mathrm I_2 = \begin{pmatrix} (Q_t-iQ_z)\mathrm I_2&(-iQ_x+Q_y)\mathrm I_2\\ (-iQ_x-Q_y)\mathrm I_2&(Q_t+iQ_z)\mathrm I_2 \end{pmatrix}. $$ It acts on the copy label and is the identity on the value factor. It is anti-multiplicative, $$ \mathsf M_4^{R}(\tilde P)\mathsf M_4^{R}(\tilde Q)=\mathsf M_4^{R}(\tilde Q\tilde P), $$ and injective.

The commutant of the left image. If $T$ commutes with every left multiplication, then $$ T(\tilde X)=T(\tilde Xe_t)=\tilde X\,T(e_t), $$ so $T$ is right multiplication by $T(e_t)$. Hence $$ \{T:T\mathsf M_4^{L}(\tilde Q)=\mathsf M_4^{L}(\tilde Q)T\ \text{for all }\tilde Q\} =M_4(\mathbb{C})_{\mathbb{B}_R}. $$ The converse is associativity, and the same argument with the sides exchanged gives $$ \{T:TM_R=M_RT\ \text{for all }M_R\}=M_4(\mathbb{C})_{\mathbb{B}_L}. $$

The dimensions. Both images have complex dimension four, and their intersection is the scalar line $\mathbb{C}\mathrm I_4$. The double centraliser of the image is therefore the image, not the algebra generated by one element: $$ C\bigl(M_4(\mathbb{C})_{\mathbb{B}_L}\bigr)=M_4(\mathbb{C})_{\mathbb{B}_R}, \qquad C\bigl(M_4(\mathbb{C})_{\mathbb{B}_R}\bigr)=M_4(\mathbb{C})_{\mathbb{B}_L}. $$

The three statements. The element and the image behave differently because the element generates a commutative subalgebra, while the image is the whole four-dimensional simple algebra.

Object Commutant Double commutant
the element $M$ dimension $8$ or $16$ $\mathbb{C}[M]$, dimension $2$ or $1$
the left image the right image, dimension $4$ the left image
the right image the left image, dimension $4$ the right image

The coefficient display. In the coefficient frame the right matrix is $$ \mathsf M_{4,\mathrm{coef}}^{R}(\tilde Q) =C_{\Pi}\bigl(\mathsf M_2(\tilde Q)^{\mathsf T}\otimes\mathrm I_2\bigr)C_{\Pi}^{-1} =D\,\mathsf M_{4,\mathrm{coef}}^{L}(\tilde Q)^{\mathsf T}D =D\,\mathsf M_{4,\mathrm{coef}}^{L}(\tilde Q^{\natural})D, $$ with $D=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$. In the coefficient frame transposition is quaternion conjugation, $\mathsf M_{4,\mathrm{coef}}^{L}(\tilde Q)^{\mathsf T}=\mathsf M_{4,\mathrm{coef}}^{L}(\tilde Q^{\natural})$. The two factors $D$ are the coefficient form of the exchange.

The Eigenbasis Ambiguity

The diagonalising matrices. Two matrices diagonalise $M$ exactly when they differ by an element of the commutant of the diagonal form: $$ C'=CG,\qquad G\in C\bigl(\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_+,\lambda_+,\lambda_-,\lambda_-)\bigr). $$ For distinct eigenvalues this commutant is $GL_2(\mathbb{C})\times GL_2(\mathbb{C})$, of dimension eight. The two factors change the basis inside the two eigenspaces.

The comparison with $2\times2$. In the $2\times2$ model the same eigenvalues have multiplicity one and the ambiguity is the invertible diagonal group, of dimension two. The extra six parameters are exactly the mixing of the two planes, the same factor that appears in the commutant of $M$. The eigenbasis ambiguity of the regular model and the commutant of the regular matrix are the same multiplicity made visible in two ways.

The degenerate case. When the eigenvalues coincide, a diagonalisable $M$ is scalar and the ambiguity is all of $GL_4(\mathbb{C})$; a non-scalar $M$ with a repeated eigenvalue has no eigenbasis, and its commutant is the eight-dimensional algebra of §The Commutant in the Regular Model.

The Adjugate and the Dagger

The adjugate. By Cayley–Hamilton in the $2\times2$ model, $$ \operatorname{adj}A=(\operatorname{Tr}A)\mathrm I_2-A, $$ so the adjugate lies in $\mathbb{C}[A]\subseteq C(A)$ for every $A$. A matrix commutes with its adjugate, and the inverse of an invertible matrix lies in the commutant as well.

The dagger. The Hermitian conjugate lies in the commutant exactly when the matrix is normal: $$ A^{\dagger}\in C(A)\iff AA^{\dagger}=A^{\dagger}A. $$ For a normal matrix the spectral theorem makes $A^{\dagger}$ a polynomial in $A$; for a non-normal matrix the two do not commute.

The regular model. The same statements hold with $M$ in place of $A$. The adjugate is $N M^{\natural}$, where $M^{\natural}=\mathsf M_4^{L}(\tilde Q^{\natural})$ is the regular matrix of the conjugate element. Since $M^{\natural}=2Q_t\mathrm I_4-M$, the adjugate is a polynomial in $M$ and lies in the commutant. The dagger lies in $C(M)$ exactly on the normal locus; then the spectral projectors lie in it as well.

Summary

The commutant of a non-scalar $2\times2$ matrix is the plane $\mathbb{C}[X]=\operatorname{span}\{\mathrm I_2,X\}$, of dimension two; for a scalar it is the whole algebra, of dimension four. The double commutant is $\mathbb{C}[X]$ in every case. In the regular model the commutant of the single matrix is $M_2(\mathbb{C})\otimes C(A)$, of dimension eight for a non-scalar and sixteen for a scalar, while the relative commutant inside the image has dimension two or four. The commutant of the whole left image is the right image, both of dimension four, and the double centraliser of the image returns the image. The eigenbasis ambiguity is $GL_1\times GL_1$ in $2\times2$ and $GL_2\times GL_2$ in $4\times4$; the extra six parameters are the mixing of the two spinor planes, the same multiplicity that separates the commutant of the regular matrix from the commutant of the element.
