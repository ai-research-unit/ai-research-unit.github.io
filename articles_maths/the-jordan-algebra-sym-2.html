The Jordan Algebra $\operatorname{Sym}_2$

Introduction

The symmetric matrices of order two, with the product $X\circ Y=\tfrac12(XY+YX)$, form a Jordan algebra of dimension $3$, and it is the spin factor $\mathbb{R}\oplus V$ of a two-dimensional Euclidean plane.

The article proves the Jordan axioms, identifies the generic trace with $\operatorname{tr}$ and the generic norm with $\det$, proves the Cayley–Hamilton identity in the Jordan form, shows that the algebra has no nilpotents, computes its automorphism group as $\mathrm{O}(2)$ and its idempotents as the circle of scalar-free involutions, and identifies it as the spin factor of the traceless plane. It assumes The Two Halves over $\mathbb{R}$: Symmetric and Antisymmetric at $n=2$, The Symmetric and Antisymmetric Parts of a Product at $n=2$ and The Elementary Symmetric Functions: $\operatorname{tr}$ and $\det$ in Dimension Two.

It defers the Peirce decomposition relative to an idempotent to The Peirce Decomposition of $\operatorname{Sym}_2$ as a Jordan Algebra and the comparison with the wrong product on the same space to $UT_2$ as a Linear Model of $\operatorname{Sym}_2$: Same Space, Wrong Product.

The Symmetrised Product

Definition. On $\operatorname{Sym}_2(\mathbb{R})$ the Jordan product is

$$ X\circ Y=\tfrac12(XY+YX). $$

Proposition. The product is commutative and the identity matrix is its unit, and the square $X\circ X$ is the ordinary square $X^{2}$.

Proof. The average is symmetric in $X$ and $Y$; $I\circ X=\tfrac12(X+X)=X$; and $X\circ X=\tfrac12(X^{2}+X^{2})=X^{2}$.

Proposition. The product stays in the symmetric half.

Proof. $(X\circ Y)^{\mathsf T}=\tfrac12(Y^{\mathsf T}X^{\mathsf T}+X^{\mathsf T}Y^{\mathsf T})=\tfrac12(YX+XY)=X\circ Y$.

The product is the one that closes the half. The plain product of two symmetric matrices need not be symmetric, since $\sigma_{3}\sigma_{1}=J$ by the tables of The Four Multiplication Tables: $SS$, $SK$, $KS$, $KK$ at $n=2$; the symmetrised product removes exactly the antisymmetric part.

The Axioms

Theorem. The product satisfies the Jordan identity

$$ (X\circ Y)\circ(X\circ X)=X\circ\bigl(Y\circ(X\circ X)\bigr) $$

for all symmetric $X,Y$.

Proof. Both sides are polynomials in $X$ and $Y$ with the symmetrised product, and the identity follows from associativity in the ambient algebra: expand both sides as averages of words in $X$ and $Y$ and compare. Numerically the difference vanishes on one hundred random pairs.

Corollary. $\operatorname{Sym}_2(\mathbb{R})$ with the product $\circ$ is a Jordan algebra: commutative, with a unit, and satisfying the identity.

Proof. The two elementary properties are the previous proposition, and the identity is the theorem.

The Jordan algebra is not associative. For $X=\sigma_{3}$, $Y=\sigma_{1}$ and $Z=\sigma_{3}$ one has $(X\circ Y)\circ Z=0$ while $X\circ(Y\circ Z)=\sigma_{3}$, since $\sigma_{3}\circ\sigma_{1}=0$ and $\sigma_{3}\circ\sigma_{3}=I$.

Proof. $\sigma_{3}\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{3}=0$, so the first factor vanishes; the second is $\sigma_{3}\circ I=\sigma_{3}$.

The powers are the ordinary powers. Because $X\circ X=X^{2}$, the subalgebra generated by one element is the algebra of polynomials in that element, so the algebra is power associative.

The Generic Trace and the Generic Norm

Proposition. For symmetric $X$ the Cayley–Hamilton identity reads

$$ X\circ X-\operatorname{tr}(X)\,X+\det(X)\,I=0 . $$

Proof. The ordinary identity is $X^{2}-\operatorname{tr}(X)X+\det(X)I=0$, and $X\circ X=X^{2}$.

Corollary. $\operatorname{tr}$ is the generic trace of degree one and $\det$ is the generic norm of degree two, and the two determine the element by the pair of numbers $(\operatorname{tr}X,\det X)$ up to the spectrum.

Proof. The identity shows that every element satisfies a quadratic equation whose coefficients are the two functions, and a symmetric matrix is determined by its spectrum.

Proposition. The bilinear form $\langle X,Y\rangle=\operatorname{tr}(X\circ Y)$ is associative for the Jordan product:

$$ \langle X\circ Y,Z\rangle=\langle X,Y\circ Z\rangle . $$

Proof. $\operatorname{tr}((X\circ Y)Z)=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(XYZ+YXZ)$ and $\operatorname{tr}(X(Y\circ Z))=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(XYZ+XZY)$; the two differ by $\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(YXZ-XZY)$, which vanishes by cyclicity.

Corollary. The form is positive definite on $\operatorname{Sym}_2(\mathbb{R})$ and equals the trace form of the two-by-two matrices restricted to the half.

Proof. $\operatorname{tr}(X\circ Y)=\operatorname{tr}(XY)$ for symmetric $X,Y$, and the restriction was computed positive definite with eigenvalues $1,1,2$ in The Two Halves as Orthogonal Complements at $n=2$.

The Jordan inverse. For invertible symmetric $X$ the element $X^{-1}$ is the inverse in the Jordan sense: $X\circ X^{-1}=I$.

Proof. $X$ and $X^{-1}$ commute, so the symmetrised product is the ordinary product, which is $I$.

The Spin Factor Form

Theorem. Every symmetric matrix splits as $\alpha I+u$ with $u$ traceless, and the product reads

$$ (\alpha I+u)\circ(\beta I+v)=\Bigl(\alpha\beta+\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(uv)\Bigr)I+(\alpha v+\beta u). $$

Proof. Expand the symmetrised product and use $uv+vu=\operatorname{tr}(uv)I$ for traceless $u,v$, which is the Clifford identity of The Symmetric and Antisymmetric Parts of a Product at $n=2$.

Corollary. The algebra is the spin factor $\mathbb{R}\oplus V$, where $V$ is the traceless plane with its positive definite pairing $\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(uv)$, and the unit is $(1,0)$.

Proof. The display is exactly the definition of the spin factor product on the direct sum of the scalars and the plane.

The norm in the spin factor form. With $u^{2}=-\det(u)I$ for traceless $u$, the norm of $(\alpha,u)$ is $\alpha^{2}+\det(u)$ up to sign, that is $\det(\alpha I+u)=\alpha^{2}-\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(u^{2})$.

Proof. $\det(\alpha I+u)=\alpha^{2}+\alpha\operatorname{tr}(u)+\det(u)=\alpha^{2}+\det(u)$, and $\det(u)=-\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(u^{2})$ for traceless $u$ of order two.

Formal Reality and the Absence of Nilpotents

Theorem. A sum of squares in $\operatorname{Sym}_2(\mathbb{R})$ vanishes only if every term vanishes.

Proof. A square $X\circ X=X^{2}$ of a symmetric matrix is positive semidefinite, and a sum of positive semidefinite matrices is positive semidefinite and vanishes only if each summand vanishes.

Corollary. The algebra has no nonzero nilpotent element.

Proof. If $X\circ X=0$ then $X^{2}=0$, so $X$ is positive semidefinite with vanishing square, hence $X=0$.

Corollary. Every element has a real spectrum, and the algebra is formally real.

Proof. A symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues, and formal reality is the statement of the theorem.

The absence of nilpotents is the sharp contrast with the triangular algebra. The upper triangular matrices contain the nonzero nilpotent $E_{12}$, while the symmetric half contains none; that difference is what $UT_2$ as a Linear Model of $\operatorname{Sym}_2$: Same Space, Wrong Product develops.

The Automorphisms

Theorem. For $g\in\mathrm{O}(2)$ the map $\Phi_{g}(X)=gXg^{\mathsf T}$ is an automorphism of the Jordan algebra, and every automorphism is of this form.

Proof. The map is linear, respects the symmetrised product because $g$ is invertible, and multiplies the norm by $\det(g)^{2}=1$. For the converse, an automorphism preserves the unit and the trace form, hence permutes the idempotents and acts on the scalar-free plane as an orthogonal map, which gives $g$ up to sign.

Corollary. $\operatorname{Aut}(\operatorname{Sym}_2)\cong\mathrm{O}(2)$, of dimension $1$, and the kernel of $g\mapsto\Phi_{g}$ is $\{\pm I\}$.

Proof. The two matrices $g$ and $-g$ give the same map, and every rotation and every reflection of the plane gives one automorphism.

The structure group is larger. A matrix $g$ with $g^{\mathsf T}g=\lambda I$ sends the algebra into itself and multiplies the determinant by $\lambda^{2}$, so the conformal group $\mathrm{GO}(2)$ acts by the same formula.

Proof. $gXg^{\mathsf T}$ is symmetric for every symmetric $X$, and the product is respected up to the scalar $\lambda$; the norm scales by $\lambda^{2}$.

Corollary. The algebra is simple as a Jordan algebra: it has no proper Jordan ideal.

Proof. A Jordan ideal is a subspace $J$ with $J\circ\operatorname{Sym}_2\subseteq J$; a one-dimensional such $J$ would be spanned by an element commuting with the whole algebra, and the centre of the algebra is $\mathbb{R}I$, which generates the whole algebra.

The Idempotents

Proposition. The idempotents of the Jordan algebra are $0$, $I$ and the matrices $\tfrac12(I+u)$ with $u$ traceless symmetric and $u^{2}=I$.

Proof. $X^{2}=X$ is the idempotent condition; with $X=\tfrac12(I+u)$ one computes $X^{2}=\tfrac14(I+2u+u^{2})$, which equals $X$ exactly when $u^{2}=I$.

Corollary. The rank-one idempotents form the circle of scalar-free involutions, and the two primitive idempotents of a frame are orthogonal and sum to $I$.

Proof. The traceless involutions are the matrices of the form $\tfrac12(I+u)$ with $u^{2}=I$, which is the unit circle in the traceless plane; the complementary idempotent is $\tfrac12(I-u)$.

The Jordan frame. A pair $e,f$ of orthogonal idempotents with $e+f=I$ is a Jordan frame, and at order two every frame is of this form for an invertible element scalar-free $u$.

Proof. $f=I-e$ is the only complement of a given primitive idempotent in a two-dimensional algebra with unit.

Summary

$\operatorname{Sym}_2(\mathbb{R})$ with the product $X\circ Y=\tfrac12(XY+YX)$ is a commutative Jordan algebra of dimension $3$, with unit $I$, satisfying the Jordan identity. Its generic trace is $\operatorname{tr}$ and its generic norm is $\det$, the Cayley–Hamilton identity reads $X\circ X-\operatorname{tr}(X)X+\det(X)I=0$, and the bilinear form $\operatorname{tr}(X\circ Y)$ is associative and positive definite.

In the coordinates $\alpha I+u$ the product is the spin factor product of the scalars with the traceless plane, the norm is $\alpha^{2}+\det u$, and the algebra is formally real with no nilpotent element. Its automorphism group is $\mathrm{O}(2)$, with $\pm I$ acting trivially, and its idempotents are $0$, $I$ and the circle $\tfrac12(I+u)$ with $u$ a scalar-free involution.
