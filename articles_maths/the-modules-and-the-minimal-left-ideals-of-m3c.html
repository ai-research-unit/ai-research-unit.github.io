The Modules and the Minimal Left Ideals of $M_3(\mathbb{C})$

Introduction

The algebra $M_{3}(\mathbb{C})$ acts on $\mathbb{C}^{3}$ by matrix multiplication. The action is the defining representation, and the space $\mathbb{C}^{3}$ is the carrier of the principal $\mathfrak{sl}_2$. The same representation can be read as a minimal left ideal of the algebra: the column space $$ M_{3}P_{1}=\{AP_{1}:A\in M_{3}\},\qquad P_{1}=E_{11}, $$ is a left $M_{3}$-module isomorphic to $\mathbb{C}^{3}$. Its dual is the minimal right ideal $P_{1}M_{3}$, the row space, and the algebra itself is the tensor product of the two: $$ M_{3}(\mathbb{C})\cong\mathbb{C}^{3}\otimes(\mathbb{C}^{3})^{*}. $$ The article proves these statements, describes the Peirce carrier $$ C(P)=PMP\oplus PMQ $$ of a rank-one idempotent $P$, and identifies the module that carries the principal $\mathfrak{sl}_2$.

The article is the module-theoretic companion of The Peirce Decomposition of $M_{3}(\mathbb{C})$. The Peirce decomposition is a decomposition of the algebra into lines; the present article is about the modules over the algebra, which are different objects. In particular, a minimal left ideal is a three-dimensional module, not a one-dimensional Peirce line, and it is not a spinor. For $n=3$ the carrier is the highest-weight-two module $\mathbb{C}^{3}=\operatorname{Sym}^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{2})$; only the case $n=2$ has a two-dimensional spinorial module. The distinction is stated once here and used throughout the category.

The construction is root-first. The idempotents $P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}$ are those of Roots of Unity and the Lagrange Idempotents of $M_3(\mathbb{C})$; the frame $E_{ij}$ and the gauge are those of The Frame, the Simple Nilpotents and the Gauge of $M_3(\mathbb{C})$ and The Two-Parameter Gauge of $M_3(\mathbb{C})$.

The Minimal Left Ideal

Definition. For the idempotent $P_{1}=E_{11}$, the minimal left ideal is $$ M_{3}P_{1}=\{AP_{1}:A\in M_{3}(\mathbb{C})\}. $$

Proposition. $M_{3}P_{1}$ is a left ideal of dimension three. As a left $M_{3}$-module it is simple, and it is isomorphic to $\mathbb{C}^{3}$ with the defining action.

Proof. For $A,B\in M_{3}$, $(AB)P_{1}=A(BP_{1})\in M_{3}P_{1}$, so it is a left ideal. The product $AP_{1}$ is the matrix whose first column is the first column of $A$ and whose other columns are zero; the map $A\mapsto AP_{1}$ therefore has image the space of matrices with only the first column nonzero, of dimension three, with basis $$ E_{11},E_{21},E_{31}. $$ The left action on this space is the action on the first column: if $v=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})^{\mathsf T}$ is the first column, then $$ A\left(\sum_{i}v_{i}E_{i1}\right)=\sum_{i}(Av)_{i}E_{i1}. $$ So $M_{3}P_{1}\cong\mathbb{C}^{3}$ as a left module.

For simplicity, let $x=\sum_{i}v_{i}E_{i1}\neq0$. Some $v_{i}\neq0$. Then $$ E_{1i}x=v_{i}E_{11}, $$ so $E_{11}=(1/v_{i})E_{1i}x\in M_{3}x$. Hence $M_{3}P_{1}=M_{3}E_{11}\subseteq M_{3}x$, and the reverse inclusion is clear; every nonzero element generates the whole module. So the module is simple.

The annihilator. The annihilator of $P_{1}$ is $$ \operatorname{Ann}(P_{1})=\{A:AP_{1}=0\}=\{A:\text{the first column of }A\text{ is zero}\}, $$ of dimension six. The quotient $M_{3}/\operatorname{Ann}(P_{1})$ is the column space, of dimension three, and it is the simple module.

The endomorphism algebra. Every $M_{3}$-linear endomorphism of $M_{3}P_{1}$ is right multiplication by an element of $P_{1}M_{3}P_{1}=\mathbb{C}P_{1}$, so $$ \operatorname{End}_{M_{3}}(M_{3}P_{1})\cong\mathbb{C}. $$ This is Schur's lemma for the simple module: the endomorphism algebra is a division algebra over $\mathbb{C}$, hence $\mathbb{C}$ itself.

The explicit column. A vector $v=(1,2,3)^{\mathsf T}$ corresponds to the matrix $$ V=\sum_{i=1}^{3}v_{i}E_{i1} =\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\2&0&0\\3&0&0\end{pmatrix}. $$ The left action of $A$ is the matrix product $AV$, whose first column is $Av$ and whose other columns are zero. The module is the column, and the matrix $V$ is the column written as a matrix with the other columns zero.

The Right Ideal and the Dual

Definition. The minimal right ideal is $$ P_{1}M_{3}=\{P_{1}A:A\in M_{3}(\mathbb{C})\}. $$

Proposition. $P_{1}M_{3}$ is a right ideal of dimension three, with basis $$ E_{11},E_{12},E_{13}. $$ It is the row space: $P_{1}A$ is the matrix whose first row is the first row of $A$ and whose other rows are zero. As a right $M_{3}$-module it is simple, and it is the dual of the left module $M_{3}P_{1}$.

Proof. The right ideal property is $(P_{1}A)B=P_{1}(AB)\in P_{1}M_{3}$. The product $P_{1}A$ has only the first row nonzero, and the basis is $E_{11},E_{12},E_{13}$. The pairing $$ M_{3}P_{1}\times P_{1}M_{3}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C},\qquad (x,y)\longmapsto\operatorname{tr}(xy), $$ is nondegenerate: for $x=\sum_{i}v_{i}E_{i1}$ and $y=\sum_{j}w_{j}E_{1j}$, $$ xy=\sum_{i,j}v_{i}w_{j}E_{ij},\qquad \operatorname{tr}(xy)=\sum_{i}v_{i}w_{i}, $$ which is the standard dot product of the column $v$ and the row $w$. The pairing is left-linear in $x$ and right-linear in $y$, and it identifies $P_{1}M_{3}$ with the dual of $M_{3}P_{1}$.

The row as a functional. A row $w=(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3})$ is the linear functional $v\mapsto\sum_{i}w_{i}v_{i}$ on the column. The right action of $A$ on the row is the dual action: $(wA)(v)=w(Av)$. The row module is the contragredient representation of the column module. For $\mathfrak{sl}_{2}$ the highest-weight-two module is self-dual, so the row and the column are isomorphic as modules; the isomorphism is not canonical.

The transpose. The transpose identifies the column with the row: $$ (M_{3}P_{1})^{\mathsf T}=P_{1}M_{3},\qquad \left(\sum_{i}v_{i}E_{i1}\right)^{\mathsf T}=\sum_{i}v_{i}E_{1i}. $$ The transpose is an isomorphism of vector spaces, but it reverses the multiplication: $(AB)^{\mathsf T}=B^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}$. It is not an isomorphism of left modules unless the algebra is commutative. The duality is the correct module statement; the transpose is a convenient coordinate identification.

The Tensor Product

Theorem. $$ M_{3}(\mathbb{C})\cong\mathbb{C}^{3}\otimes(\mathbb{C}^{3})^{*} $$ as a bimodule, with $$ E_{ij}\longleftrightarrow e_{i}\otimes e_{j}^{*}, $$ where $e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}$ is the standard basis of the column and $e_{1}^{*},e_{2}^{*},e_{3}^{*}$ is the dual basis of the row.

Proof. The nine elementary tensors $e_{i}\otimes e_{j}^{*}$ are a basis of the tensor product, which has dimension $3\cdot3=9$; the map sends them to the nine matrix units, which are a basis of $M_{3}$. The product is $$ (e_{i}\otimes e_{j}^{*})(e_{k}\otimes e_{l}^{*}) =\delta_{jk}\,e_{i}\otimes e_{l}^{*}, $$ which is the matrix-unit product $E_{ij}E_{kl}=\delta_{jk}E_{il}$. The left action of $M_{3}$ is on the first factor, and the right action is on the second; the two actions commute, and the tensor product is the bimodule of endomorphisms of the column.

The trace and the identity. Under the isomorphism, $$ I=\sum_{i}e_{i}\otimes e_{i}^{*},\qquad \operatorname{tr}(e_{i}\otimes e_{j}^{*})=\delta_{ij}. $$ The trace is the contraction of the two factors; the identity is the invariant tensor of the pair of dual spaces. The determinant is the top exterior power; the algebra is the full endomorphism algebra of a three-dimensional space.

The Peirce decomposition in the tensor picture. The idempotent $P_{k}=E_{kk}$ is $e_{k}\otimes e_{k}^{*}$, the projection onto the $k$-th line. The Peirce line $A_{kl}=\mathbb{C}E_{kl}$ is the line $\mathbb{C}e_{k}\otimes e_{l}^{*}$, of dimension one. The left ideal $M_{3}P_{k}$ is $\mathbb{C}^{3}\otimes e_{k}^{*}$, a copy of the column; the right ideal $P_{k}M_{3}$ is $e_{k}\otimes(\mathbb{C}^{3})^{*}$, a copy of the row. The nine Peirce lines are the nine lines of the tensor product, not nine modules.

The Minimal Left Ideals and the Non-Canonical Exchange

The three minimal left ideals. For $k=1,2,3$ the space $$ M_{3}P_{k}=\{AP_{k}:A\in M_{3}\} $$ is a minimal left ideal, isomorphic to $\mathbb{C}^{3}$. The isomorphism is the projection onto the $k$-th column: $$ M_{3}P_{k}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{3},\qquad \sum_{i}v_{i}E_{ik}\longmapsto (v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})^{\mathsf T}. $$ The three modules are isomorphic, but the isomorphisms depend on the choice of the column; there is no canonical identification of the $k$-th column with the $l$-th column.

The exchange. For $k\neq l$, right multiplication by $E_{kl}$ maps $M_{3}P_{k}$ to $M_{3}P_{l}$: $$ xE_{kl}\in M_{3}P_{k}E_{kl}=M_{3}P_{l}, $$ because $P_{k}E_{kl}=E_{kl}=E_{kl}P_{l}$. The inverse is right multiplication by $E_{lk}$: $$ xE_{kl}E_{lk}=xP_{k}=x. $$ So $E_{kl}$ gives an isomorphism of left modules $$ M_{3}P_{k}\longrightarrow M_{3}P_{l},\qquad x\longmapsto xE_{kl}. $$

The exchange is not canonical. The matrix unit $E_{kl}$ is determined only up to the gauge $$ E_{kl}\longmapsto \frac{t_{k}}{t_{l}}E_{kl}, $$ as in The Two-Parameter Gauge of $M_3(\mathbb{C})$. The isomorphism is multiplied by the scalar $t_{k}/t_{l}$, so the set of isomorphisms $M_{3}P_{k}\to M_{3}P_{l}$ is a torsor under $\mathbb{C}^{\times}$. The choice of an isomorphism is a choice of normalized frame, not a canonical structure of the algebra. The three minimal left ideals are isomorphic, but not canonically isomorphic.

The general minimal left ideal. Every minimal left ideal of $M_{3}$ is of the form $M_{3}Q$ for a rank-one idempotent $Q$. Every rank-one idempotent is conjugate to $P_{1}$: if $Q$ has image the line $\mathbb{C}v$ and kernel a plane, choose $g\in GL_{3}$ with $g e_{1}=v$ and $g$ mapping the standard plane to the kernel; then $Q=gP_{1}g^{-1}$. The map $$ M_{3}P_{1}\longrightarrow M_{3}Q,\qquad x\longmapsto xg $$ is an isomorphism of left modules, with inverse $y\mapsto yg^{-1}$. The choice of $g$ is a choice of basis adapted to $Q$; different choices differ by the stabilizer of $P_{1}$, the diagonal torus, and the isomorphism is again non-canonical.

The Peirce Carrier

Definition. Let $P$ be a rank-one idempotent and $Q=I-P$. The Peirce carrier is $$ C(P)=PMP\oplus PMQ. $$

Proposition. $\dim_{\mathbb{C}}C(P)=1+2=3$, and $C(P)=PM_{3}$ is the minimal right ideal generated by $P$. Its dual is the minimal left ideal $M_{3}P=PMP\oplus QMP$, also of dimension $1+2=3$. The two are isomorphic as vector spaces and dual as modules.

Proof. The corner $PMP$ is one-dimensional, because $P$ has rank one; the corner $PMQ$ is two-dimensional, because $Q$ has rank two and $P$ has rank one. Their sum is $PM(P+Q)=PM$, the right ideal generated by $P$. The left ideal $M_{3}P=(P+Q)MP=PMP\oplus QMP$ has the same dimensions, $1+2$. The pairing $$ M_{3}P\times PM_{3}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C},\qquad (x,y)\longmapsto\operatorname{tr}(xy), $$ is nondegenerate, so the right carrier is the dual of the left carrier.

The Peirce coordinates of the carrier. For $P=P_{1}$, the carrier $P_{1}M_{3}$ is the row space, with basis $E_{11},E_{12},E_{13}$: one diagonal coordinate and two mixed coordinates. The left carrier $M_{3}P_{1}$ is the column space, with basis $E_{11},E_{21},E_{31}$: one diagonal coordinate and two mixed coordinates. The scalar coordinate is the $PMP$ line; the two mixed coordinates are the $PMQ$ or $QMP$ plane. The carrier is the module, not the Peirce line: the line is one-dimensional, the carrier is three-dimensional.

The carrier of a rank-one idempotent is the simple module. For every rank-one $P$, $C(P)=PM_{3}\cong\mathbb{C}^{3}$ as a right module, and $M_{3}P\cong\mathbb{C}^{3}$ as a left module. The isomorphism depends on $P$ and on the frame; the dimension and the module structure do not. The Peirce carrier of the menu is the right module $PM_{3}$; the left module $M_{3}P$ is its dual. The principal $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ of the next section is carried by the left module $M_{3}P_{1}$; the right module gives the same representation up to the duality, which is an isomorphism for the highest-weight-two module.

The rank-two case. For a rank-two idempotent $P$, the corner $PMP$ has dimension four and $PMQ$ has dimension two, so $PM_{3}$ has dimension six; it is not a minimal ideal. The minimal left ideals are the modules $M_{3}P_{k}$ of the rank-one idempotents, of dimension three. The rank-two carrier is the direct sum of two minimal modules, not a simple module.

The Module as the Carrier of the Principal $\mathfrak{sl}_2$

The module. The left module $M_{3}P_{1}\cong\mathbb{C}^{3}$ is the carrier of the defining representation. The principal $\mathfrak{sl}_{2}$ of the frame acts on it by left multiplication: $$ A\cdot v=Av,\qquad A\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2},\quad v\in\mathbb{C}^{3}. $$ With the standard basis $e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}$ and the operators $$ J_{3}=\operatorname{diag}(1,0,-1),\qquad J_{+}=\sqrt2(E_{12}+E_{23}),\qquad J_{-}=\sqrt2(E_{21}+E_{32}), $$ the module has weights $1,0,-1$ and ladder coefficients $\sqrt2$: $$ J_{+}e_{2}=\sqrt2 e_{1},\quad J_{+}e_{3}=\sqrt2 e_{2},\quad J_{-}e_{1}=\sqrt2 e_{2},\quad J_{-}e_{2}=\sqrt2 e_{3}. $$ It is the highest-weight-two module $\operatorname{Sym}^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{2})$, of dimension $3=2\cdot1+1$.

The identification with the symmetric square. Let $x,y$ be a basis of $\mathbb{C}^{2}$ and put $$ e_{1}=x^{2},\qquad e_{2}=\sqrt2\,xy,\qquad e_{3}=y^{2}. $$ Then $J_{3}=\tfrac12(x\partial_{x}-y\partial_{y})$, $J_{+}=x\partial_{y}$, $J_{-}=y\partial_{x}$, and the actions above are the actions on the symmetric square. The carrier is the highest-weight-two module; it is not a spinor.

The Peirce carrier and the principal $\mathfrak{sl}_2$. The left carrier $M_{3}P_{1}$ is the column; the right carrier $P_{1}M_{3}$ is the row. The principal $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ can be read on either, because the highest-weight-two module is self-dual. The right module carries the contragredient action, whose weights are the negatives; the two readings are isomorphic because the spin-one representation is self-dual, but the isomorphism is not the transpose: the transpose reverses the multiplication, $(AB)^{\mathsf T}=B^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}$, and is not an intertwiner of the two actions. The isomorphism is the duality pairing, and it is not canonical. The article uses the left module for the principal $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ and the right module for the Peirce carrier, and states the duality rather than identifying them canonically.

No spinors for $n=3$. The carrier is three-dimensional and has highest weight two. A spinor would be a two-dimensional module, and $M_{3}(\mathbb{C})$ has no two-dimensional simple module: its simple module is $\mathbb{C}^{3}$, of dimension three. The two-dimensional spinorial module exists only for $M_{2}(\mathbb{C})$, where the minimal left ideal is $\mathbb{C}^{2}$ and the Peirce decomposition has four lines. For $n=3$ the Peirce lines are one-dimensional subspaces of the algebra, the minimal left ideal is a three-dimensional module, and neither is a spinor. The word spinor is reserved for the $n=2$ case; the carrier of the $n=3$ principal $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ is the highest-weight-two module.

The Peirce line is not a module. The Peirce line $A_{kl}=\mathbb{C}E_{kl}$ is a one-dimensional subspace of the algebra. It is not closed under the left action of $M_{3}$: $E_{21}E_{11}=E_{21}$ and $E_{31}E_{11}=E_{31}$, so the left action sends the line $A_{11}$ into $A_{21}$ and $A_{31}$; the left ideal it generates is the whole first column $M_{3}E_{11}$, of dimension three, not the line. The line is a Peirce summand of the algebra, not a submodule of the defining representation. The confusion between a Peirce line and a spinor is the confusion between a one-dimensional subspace of the algebra and a minimal module; the two have different dimensions and different actions.

Summary

The minimal left ideal is $$ \boxed{\;M_{3}P_{1}=\{AP_{1}:A\in M_{3}\}\cong\mathbb{C}^{3},\qquad \dim_{\mathbb{C}}=3,\;} $$ the column space, with basis $E_{11},E_{21},E_{31}$. It is simple as a left module. Its dual is the minimal right ideal $$ P_{1}M_{3}=\{P_{1}A:A\in M_{3}\}\cong(\mathbb{C}^{3})^{*}, $$ the row space, with basis $E_{11},E_{12},E_{13}$; the pairing is $(x,y)\mapsto\operatorname{tr}(xy)$.

The algebra is the tensor product $$ \boxed{\;M_{3}(\mathbb{C})\cong\mathbb{C}^{3}\otimes(\mathbb{C}^{3})^{*},\qquad E_{ij}\leftrightarrow e_{i}\otimes e_{j}^{*}.\;} $$ The Peirce line $A_{kl}$ is the line $\mathbb{C}e_{k}\otimes e_{l}^{*}$; the left ideal $M_{3}P_{k}$ is $\mathbb{C}^{3}\otimes e_{k}^{*}$; the right ideal $P_{k}M_{3}$ is $e_{k}\otimes(\mathbb{C}^{3})^{*}$.

The three minimal left ideals $M_{3}P_{k}$ are isomorphic, but not canonically: the exchange $x\mapsto xE_{kl}$ is defined up to the gauge $E_{kl}\mapsto(t_{k}/t_{l})E_{kl}$, a $\mathbb{C}^{\times}$-torsor.

The Peirce carrier of a rank-one idempotent $P$ is $$ C(P)=PMP\oplus PMQ,\qquad \dim_{\mathbb{C}}=1+2=3, $$ the minimal right ideal $PM_{3}$; its dual is the left ideal $M_{3}P=PMP\oplus QMP$, also of dimension $1+2=3$.

The carrier of the principal $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ is $M_{3}P_{1}\cong\mathbb{C}^{3}$, with weights $1,0,-1$ and ladder coefficients $\sqrt2$; it is the highest-weight-two module $\operatorname{Sym}^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{2})$. There are no spinors for $n=3$: the simple module is three-dimensional, and only $n=2$ has a two-dimensional spinorial module.
