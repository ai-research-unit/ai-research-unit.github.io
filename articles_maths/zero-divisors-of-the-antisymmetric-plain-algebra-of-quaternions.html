Zero Divisors of the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra

Introduction

The antisymmetric plain algebra of $\mathbb{H}$ carries the cross product $\tilde p\times\tilde q=\tfrac12(\tilde p\tilde q-\tilde q\tilde p)=\mathbf p\times\mathbf q$ of Introduction to the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra of Quaternions. The plain algebra is a division algebra, with no zero divisor; the antisymmetric block is at the opposite extreme. This article determines its zero divisors: the criterion, the annihilator of an element, the pairs that multiply

This is not the algebra $\mathbb{H}$. The two share the space, the addition and the real scalars, but $\times$ is a different multiplication, alternating and without a unit. Throughout, the natural sign ${}^{\natural}$ is the quaternion conjugation, the bar is the identity because $\mathbb{R}$ has no nontrivial involution, and the star coincides with the natural sign and gives nothing new.

The product, its alternation and its table are Introduction to the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra of Quaternions; the multiplication operators of the block are read in The Adjoint Operators and the Derivations of the Antisymmetric Plain Algebra and its two matrix models, all below this article in this category.

Conventions. The algebra is $\mathbb{H}$ with basis $e_0=1,e_1,e_2,e_3$ and $e_1^2=e_2^2=e_3^2=-e_0$. A generic element is $\tilde q=q_0e_0+\mathbf q$ with $\mathbf q=\sum_{k=1}^{3}q_ke_k$ and $q_\mu\in\mathbb{R}$; $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf q$ is the Euclidean dot product and $\mathbf p\times\mathbf q$ the vector product of the vector parts; the centre is $\mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{H}}=\mathbb{R}e_0$ and the vector subspace is $\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})=\{\mathbf q\}$.

The Notion and the Product

Definition. A nonzero element $\tilde p$ is a zero divisor of the block when there is a nonzero element $\tilde q$ with

$$ \tilde p\times\tilde q=0 . $$

Because the operation is alternating, $\tilde p\times\tilde p=0$ for every $\tilde p$; hence every nonzero element is a zero divisor, with itself as partner on both sides. The left and the right zero divisors coincide, and the block's zero-divisor set is total.

Theorem (the zero-divisor set is total). The set of zero-divisor elements is the punctured space

$$ \mathbb{H}\setminus\{0\}, $$

the whole four-dimensional space with the origin removed. The block is the extreme opposite of the plain one, whose only zero divisor is $0$.

Proof. For every $\tilde p$ the relation $\tilde p\times\tilde p=0$ exhibits a nonzero partner $\tilde q=\tilde p$ when $\tilde p\neq0$. No nonzero element is excluded, and $0$ is excluded by the definition.

The Criterion

Theorem (the criterion). For nonzero $\tilde p,\tilde q$,

$$ \tilde p\times\tilde q=0\iff \mathbf p\times\mathbf q=0\iff \mathbf p\ \text{and}\ \mathbf q\ \text{are linearly dependent in}\ \mathbb{R}^3 . $$

The dependence is the parallelism of the vector parts: the criterion reads $\mathbf q=\lambda\mathbf p$, or $\mathbf p=\lambda\mathbf q$, for some real $\lambda$, the zero vector being proportional to every vector.

Proof. The operation is the vector product of the vector parts, which vanishes exactly when the two vectors are linearly dependent. Two nonzero vectors of $\mathbb{R}^3$ are linearly dependent exactly when they are proportional.

Remark (the criterion is blind to the scalars). The scalar coordinates $p_0,q_0$ do not enter the criterion at all: a pure and a non-pure element with the same vector part have the same partners. This is the reason the collapse that the definiteness of the norm forces on the other blocks does not occur here — the operation never reads a norm.

The Annihilator of an Element

Theorem. The left annihilator of a nonzero element is

$$ \operatorname{Ann}(\tilde p)=\{\tilde q:\tilde p\times\tilde q=0\} =\begin{cases} \mathbb{R}e_0\oplus\mathbb{R}\mathbf p, & \mathbf p\neq0,\\[2pt] \mathbb{H}, & \mathbf p=0 . \end{cases} $$

For a noncentral element the annihilator is the two-dimensional real plane spanned by the centre and the vector part of the element; for a central element every element is annihilated.

Proof. If $\mathbf p\neq0$, the equation $\mathbf p\times\mathbf q=0$ is $\mathbf q\in\mathbb{R}\mathbf p$, a line of the vector subspace, and $q_0$ is free; with the centre that is the plane $\mathbb{R}e_0\oplus\mathbb{R}\mathbf p$, of real dimension two. If $\tilde p=\lambda e_0$ with $\lambda\neq0$, then $\tilde p\times\tilde q=\lambda e_0\times\tilde q=0$ for every $\tilde q$.

The Operator and Its Invariants

Proposition. The left multiplication operator $L^{\times}_{\tilde p}\tilde q=\tilde p\times\tilde q$ has the block matrix $\operatorname{diag}(0,C_{\mathbf p})$ in the basis $(e_0,e_1,e_2,e_3)$, with $C_{\mathbf p}$ the cross-product matrix of $\mathbf p$ on the vector subspace. Its invariants are

$$ \operatorname{Tr}L^{\times}_{\tilde p}=0,\qquad \det L^{\times}_{\tilde p}=0,\qquad \operatorname{rank}L^{\times}_{\tilde p}=\begin{cases}2,&\mathbf p\neq0,\\0,&\mathbf p=0,\end{cases} $$

and its characteristic polynomial is

$$ \chi(t)=t^2\bigl(t^2+|\mathbf p|^2\bigr). $$

Proof. The scalar coordinate of the value is zero and the vector part is $\mathbf p\times\mathbf q$, so the operator is $\operatorname{diag}(0,C_{\mathbf p})$; the invariants follow from $C_{\mathbf p}^2=\mathbf p\mathbf p^{\mathsf T}-|\mathbf p|^2\mathrm{I}_3$, of characteristic polynomial $t(t^2+|\mathbf p|^2)$, and from the vanishing of the scalar coordinate. The rank is two when $\mathbf p\neq0$, because $C_{\mathbf p}$ has the kernel line $\mathbb{R}\mathbf p$ inside the three-dimensional vector subspace, and the centre adds one kernel dimension.

The Pairs That Multiply to Zero

The Families

Theorem. The zero-divisor pairs $(\tilde p,\tilde q)$ with $\tilde p,\tilde q\neq0$ and $\tilde p\times\tilde q=0$ form two disjoint families:

$$ \text{(i) the pure pairs: } \tilde p=\mathbf u,\ \tilde q=\mathbf v \ \text{with}\ \mathbf u,\mathbf v\neq0\ \text{and parallel}; \qquad \text{(ii) the pairs with a scalar part: } \tilde p\ \text{or}\ \tilde q\ \text{non-pure}, $$

the vector parts being parallel in the second family too, or one of them zero.

Proof. By the criterion the vector parts are parallel. Two pure elements with nonzero vector parts give the first family; a non-pure element gives the second, its partner having a parallel vector part by the criterion. If one vector part is zero the corresponding element is the pure scalar $\lambda e_0$ with $\lambda\neq0$, a case of the second family with the partner free; if both vector parts are nonzero and parallel, the pair is in the first family exactly when both elements are pure. The two families are disjoint and cover every pair.

Remark (the two families and the annihilator). For a noncentral element the annihilator is the plane $\mathbb{R}e_0\oplus\mathbb{R}\mathbf p$: its vector line $\mathbb{R}\mathbf p$ carries the pure partners and the rest of the plane the non-pure ones, so the pure pairs are the vector-line stratum and the pairs with a scalar part the complementary stratum. For a central element the annihilator is the whole space and the partner is arbitrary.

The Dimension Count

Proposition. The zero-divisor set has real dimension four. The zero-divisor pairs form a set of real dimension six, and the annihilator of a noncentral element is a plane of real dimension two.

Proof. The zero-divisor set is $\mathbb{H}\setminus\{0\}$, of dimension four. For a pair, choose the first element: if it is noncentral, four parameters, then the partner ranges over the annihilator plane $\mathbb{R}e_0\oplus\mathbb{R}\mathbf p$, of dimension two, giving a family of dimension six, which dominates the central stratum $\{\lambda e_0\}\times(\mathbb{H}\setminus\{0\})$, of dimension five. So the pairs have dimension six.

Summary

The antisymmetric plain block has no nontrivial zero divisor theory to discover: the operation is alternating, so $\tilde p\times\tilde p=0$ and every nonzero element is a zero divisor with itself as partner, the zero-divisor set being the punctured four-dimensional space. The criterion for a pair is the parallelism of the vector parts, $\mathbf p\times\mathbf q=0\iff\mathbf q=\lambda\mathbf p$ or $\mathbf p=\lambda\mathbf q$, and the annihilator of a noncentral element is the two-dimensional plane $\mathbb{R}e_0\oplus\mathbb{R}\mathbf p$, of the left multiplication operator $\operatorname{diag}(0,C_{\mathbf p})$, of vanishing trace and determinant and rank two off the centre. The zero-divisor pairs form two families, the pure parallel pairs and the pairs with a scalar part, of total real dimension six. Over $\mathbb{C}$ the criterion is identical and the only difference is the possible degeneracy of the operator's quadratic factor on the isotropic cone.
