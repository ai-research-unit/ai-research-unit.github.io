The Hamilton Root Pairing of the Polar Element Representation of Biquaternions

Introduction

Every biquaternion with non-vanishing norm has the polar representation $\tilde{Q} = r\,e^{i\alpha}\tilde U_{\mathrm b}\tilde u_{\mathrm r}$ of The Polar Element Representation of Biquaternions, four factors that can be grouped in pairs in exactly three ways. This article treats the grouping in which the scale multiplies the phase and the boost multiplies the rotor,

$$ \tilde{Q} = R\,\exp(\hat\Xi\Theta), \qquad R = r\,e^{i\alpha}, \qquad \exp(\hat\Xi\Theta) = \tilde U_{\mathrm b}\,\tilde u_{\mathrm r} , $$

the Hamilton representation of the biquaternion literature. It is called the Hamilton root pairing here because its second factor is the exponential of a root of $-1$: the axis $\hat\Xi$ of the representation is a root of minus one, and the same root, read on the set of the roots of one, is what ties the pairing to the classification of those roots.

The pairing is partial, in the precise sense of The Quaternion Pairing of the Polar Element Representation of Biquaternions: its modulus $R$ is a composite object, the product of the scale and the phase, and the representation is available only where that object can be constructed from the parts of the element, which requires the vector part to be non-null. The second partial grouping, in which the scale is grouped with the rotor, is the quaternion pairing of that article; the third grouping, in which the scale is grouped with the boost and the phase with the rotor, is not partial and is The Cartan Hermitian Pairing of the Polar Element Representation of Biquaternions.

The result, in one sentence. On the complement of the null cone and of the quadric cone $(\vec Q,\vec Q) = 0$, the element factors as $R\exp(\hat\Xi\Theta)$, with $R$ the complex modulus, $\hat\Xi = \vec Q/B$ the root of $-1$ parallel to the vector part and $\Theta$ the complex angle whose real part is the rotation angle and whose imaginary part is the rapidity; the roots of one, $\tilde\Xi^2 = e_0$, are the elements at which the same four-factor word closes, and they carry a second exponential form, a single exponential of the halving idempotent, whose exponent is recovered from the four-factor word by the product of its last two factors. The two exponents of the non-central factors, $i\frac{\psi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm b}$ and $\frac{\theta}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm r}$, are one Hermitian element and one real pure element, and they do not commute; the two factors are a single exponential with a complex number inside on the whole domain, the complex number being the sum of the two exponents exactly when the two axes are parallel.

The article has two halves, and they are one subject: the axis of the first half is a root of $-1$, and the second half is the same four-factor word read at the elements where it closes, the roots of one. The first half develops the Hamilton representation: the pairing, the statement, the domain, the exponential of the element, the correlated non-uniqueness of its three parameters, and the behaviour of its data under the three conjugations. The second half develops the roots of one in the two exponential forms, the four-factor word at a root and the single exponential of the halving idempotent, and the bridge between them. Read on the exponents of the two non-central factors rather than on the factors, the word gives the roots as a hyperbolic family generated by a Hermitian element and by a fixed quarter turn, and the same reading answers the question of the commutation of the two factors, on which the two exponents either do or do not add to the complex angle of a single exponential.

The article assumes the four factors and their definition from The Polar Element Representation of Biquaternions and does not re-derive them. No physics is invoked.

Conventions. $\mathbb{B} = \mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$ has the basis $e_0,e_1,e_2,e_3$ with $e_1e_2 = e_3$ and the central scalar imaginary $i$, $i^2 = -1$. An element is $\tilde{Q} = \sum_{\mu=0}^{3}Q_\mu e_\mu = Q_0e_0+\vec Q$ with $\vec Q = \sum_{k=1}^{3}Q_ke_k$ and $Q_\mu\in\mathbb{C}$, and its real and imaginary quaternion parts are $Q_r,Q_i\in\mathbb{H}$ under $\tilde{Q} = Q_r+iQ_i$. The quaternion conjugation is $\tilde{Q}^{\natural} = Q_0e_0-\vec Q$, the coefficientwise conjugation is $\overline{\tilde{Q}} = \overline{Q_0}e_0+\overline{\vec Q}$, and the Hermitian conjugation is $\tilde{Q}^{*} = \overline{\tilde{Q}^{\natural}}$, the two operations commuting. The norm is $\langle\tilde{Q},\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural} = \tilde{Q}\tilde{Q}^{\natural} = \sum_{\mu=0}^{3}Q_\mu^{2}$, a complex number in general, and the domain of the polar representation is $\langle\tilde{Q},\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural}\neq0$. The symbols $r$, $e^{i\alpha}$, $\tilde U_{\mathrm b}$, $\tilde u_{\mathrm r}$, $\rho = re^{i\alpha} = \sqrt{\langle\tilde{Q},\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural}}$, $\mathcal{A}$ and the subspaces $\mathbb{M}_\pm$, $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$, $\mathrm{Sp}(1)$ are those of The Polar Element Representation of Biquaternions. Two different glyphs are in play and must not be confused: the Hamilton axis $\hat\Xi$, a root of $-1$, and a root of one $\tilde\Xi$, with $\tilde\Xi^2 = e_0$. The Hamilton modulus is $R$, its argument $\Theta$; the halving idempotent of a root is $\tilde\Pi_-$.

The Hamilton Representation

The Pairing

The Hamilton representation is the pairing of the scale with the phase against the boost with the rotor:

$$ \tilde{Q} = R\,\exp(\hat\Xi\Theta), \qquad R = r\,e^{i\alpha} = \sqrt{\langle\tilde{Q},\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural}}, \qquad \exp(\hat\Xi\Theta) = \tilde U_{\mathrm b}\,\tilde u_{\mathrm r} . $$

The modulus is the complex scalar $\rho$ of the polar representation, and the exponential is the whole rotor part, boost and rotation together. The identification of $\tilde U_{\mathrm b}\tilde u_{\mathrm r}$ with a single exponential is the content of the construction of The Polar Element Representation of Biquaternions, and it is the reason the Hamilton representation is the closest of the three to the quaternion polar representation: when all four coefficients are real, $\tilde U_{\mathrm b} = e_0$, $\tilde u_{\mathrm r}$ is the unit quaternion, and the representation reduces to $q = r\exp(\mu\tfrac{\theta}{2})$ term by term.

Both factors of the exponential are themselves exponentials of a single element, so the Hamilton factor is the single exponential of a product of two exponentials. The boost is

$$ \tilde U_{\mathrm b} = \exp\Bigl(i\frac{\psi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm b}\Bigr) = \cosh\frac{\psi}{2}\,e_0 + i\sinh\frac{\psi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm b} , $$

with $\hat u_{\mathrm b}$ a unit vector and $\psi$ the rapidity, and the rotor is

$$ \tilde u_{\mathrm r} = \exp\Bigl(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm r}\Bigr) = \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,e_0 + \sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,\hat u_{\mathrm r} , $$

with $\hat u_{\mathrm r}$ a unit vector and $\theta$ the rotation angle. Each exponent is a parameter times a root of $\pm e_0$: $i\hat u_{\mathrm b}$ is a Hermitian root of $+e_0$ and $\hat u_{\mathrm r}$ a real pure root of $-e_0$. Their product is again a single exponential because the vector part is non-null on the domain of the representation, and its angle is the complex $\Theta$ whose real part is the rotation angle and whose imaginary part is the rapidity. The exponent of that single exponential is a complex multiple $\Theta\hat\Xi$ of a root of $-e_0$, and it is the sum $i\frac{\psi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm b}+\frac{\theta}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm r}$ of the two exponents of the factors exactly when the two axes are parallel, as the second half shows.

The Statement

The representation is stated as follows. Write $\tilde{Q} = Q_0e_0+\vec Q$ with vector part $\vec Q = Q_1e_1+Q_2e_2+Q_3e_3$, suppose $(\vec Q,\vec Q) = Q_1^2+Q_2^2+Q_3^2 \neq 0$ and $\langle\tilde{Q},\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural}\neq0$, and put

$$ R = \sqrt{\langle\tilde{Q},\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural}}, \qquad \hat\Xi = \frac{\vec Q}{B}, \qquad B = \sqrt{(\vec Q,\vec Q)}, \qquad \cos\Theta = \frac{Q_0}{R}, \qquad \sin\Theta = \frac{B}{R} . $$

Then $\tilde{Q} = R(\cos\Theta+\hat\Xi\sin\Theta)$, and $\hat\Xi$ is a root of $-1$: $\hat\Xi^2 = \vec Q^2/B^2 = -\left(\vec Q,\vec Q\right)e_0/B^2 = -e_0$, since the square of a pure biquaternion is $-\left(\vec Q,\vec Q\right)e_0$. The angle $\Theta$ is complex and the modulus $R$ is complex, and the constraint $\cos^2\Theta+\sin^2\Theta = 1$ is the identity $Q_0^2+(\vec Q,\vec Q) = \langle\tilde{Q},\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural}$.

The axis. The axis $\hat\Xi$ is a root of $-1$, and the normalisation $\hat\Xi^2 = -e_0$ is what the definition $B = \sqrt{(\vec Q,\vec Q)}$ provides. It is not an arbitrary root but the one parallel to the vector part of the element, and that parallelism is what makes the representation canonical. When the vector part is null the normalisation fails, $B = 0$, and with it the axis; that is the domain restriction below.

The Domain

The Hamilton representation is therefore available on

$$ \left\{\tilde{Q} : \langle\tilde{Q},\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural}\neq0 \ \text{ and } \ (\vec Q,\vec Q)\neq0\right\}, $$

which is the complement of the union of the null cone and the quadric cone $(\vec Q,\vec Q) = 0$. The second condition is not implied by the first and is not weaker: the element $1+e_1+ie_2$ has $N = 1$ and $(\vec Q,\vec Q) = 1+i^2 = 0$, so it is invertible and has no Hamilton representation. Within its domain the representation is unique once the branch of $R = \sqrt{\langle\tilde{Q},\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural}}$ is fixed; the literature states the uniqueness as up to the correlated replacement $(R,\Theta)\mapsto(-R,\Theta+\pi)$, which the branch choice of the polar representation removes.

The Exponential of the Element

The representation $\tilde{Q} = R\exp(\hat\Xi\Theta)$ is a statement about $\tilde{Q}$ itself, and it must not be read as a statement about its exponential. Write $\vec Q = B\hat\Xi$ and $\tilde{Q} = Q_0e_0 + B\hat\Xi$. Since $Q_0e_0$ is central,

$$ \exp(\tilde{Q}) = e^{Q_0}\exp(B\hat\Xi) = e^{Q_0}\bigl(\cos B\,e_0 + \sin B\,\hat\Xi\bigr), $$

using $\hat\Xi^2 = -e_0$ as above. The right-hand side is the Hamilton representation of the element $\exp(\tilde{Q})$, with modulus $e^{Q_0}$ and angle $B$; it is not the Hamilton representation of $\tilde{Q}$, whose modulus and angle are $R$ and $\Theta$. The two Hamilton data therefore agree only in the special case

$$ R = e^{Q_0} \ \text{ and }\ \Theta = B, \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad \langle\tilde{Q},\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural} = \exp(2Q_0) \ \text{ and the angle } \Theta \text{ equals } B , $$

by the identity $\langle\tilde{Q},\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural} = Q_0^2 + B^2$. In general the inverse relation is the one that holds: the logarithm of $\tilde{Q}$ reads off its own modulus and angle, $\log\tilde{Q} = \log R\,e_0 + \Theta\,\hat\Xi$ (Biquaternion Elementary Functions, §The Logarithm), so it is the logarithm, not the exponential, that recovers $(R,\Theta)$ from the element. The distinction matters whenever an element and its exponential are both in play, as for the exponential map of $\mathbb{B}^\times$ in Biquaternion Lie Group and Exponential Structure.

Non-Uniqueness of the Parameters

The three parameters of the Hamilton representation are not determined independently; the representation is unique only up to three correlated replacements.

  1. The axis is defined up to sign. The relation $\vec Q = B\hat\Xi$ determines $\hat\Xi$ only together with the sign of $B = \sqrt{(\vec Q,\vec Q)}$, since $B$ is a square root; replacing $B$ by $-B$ replaces $\hat\Xi$ by $-\hat\Xi$ and leaves both $\hat\Xi^2 = -e_0$ and $\vec Q$ unchanged.
  2. The angle is defined up to sign, correlated with the axis. Replacing $(\hat\Xi,\Theta)$ by $(-\hat\Xi,-\Theta)$ leaves $\tilde{Q} = R(\cos\Theta + \hat\Xi\sin\Theta)$ fixed, because the two changes cancel in the term $\hat\Xi\sin\Theta$.
  3. The modulus is defined up to sign, correlated with a shift of the angle. Replacing $(R,\Theta)$ by $(-R,\Theta+\pi)$ gives the same element, since $\cos(\Theta+\pi) = -\cos\Theta$ and $\sin(\Theta+\pi) = -\sin\Theta$.

Fixing the branches removes the first and the third: the four-factor representation fixes the modulus as $R = \rho = \sqrt{\langle\tilde{Q},\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural}}$ with $r>0$, and a choice of the square root $B$ fixes the axis; then $\hat\Xi$ and $\Theta$ are determined up to the period of the angle. What remains is the ambiguity of $\Theta$ modulo $2\pi$, which is precisely the multivaluedness of the logarithm: the branches of $\log\tilde{Q}$ differ by the kernel of the exponential, and the power functions inherit the ambiguity. This is why the logarithm and the powers are many-valued on $\mathbb{B}^\times$ while the exponential is single-valued.

Behaviour Under the Three Conjugations

The algebra has three nontrivial conjugations: quaternion conjugation ${}^{\natural}$, the anti-automorphism fixing the centre $\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{B}}$; complex conjugation $\bar{\cdot}$, the automorphism conjugating every coefficient; and Hermitian conjugation ${}^{*} = {}^{\natural}\circ\bar{\cdot}$, the two operations commuting because one acts on the coefficients and the other on the units. The three act on the Hamilton data as follows.

The Hamilton Data

In $\tilde{Q} = R\exp(\hat\Xi\Theta)$ the modulus $R$ and the angle $\Theta$ are complex scalars and therefore central, while the axis $\hat\Xi$ is pure, so that $\hat\Xi^{\natural} = -\hat\Xi$. Quaternion conjugation fixes the centre and reverses a pure element, complex conjugation is an automorphism and conjugates the coefficients of the axis as it conjugates the rest, and Hermitian conjugation does both. The axis of a conjugated element is again a root of $-1$, and it is read off the conjugate: complex conjugation carries $\hat\Xi$ to $\bar{\hat\Xi}$, quaternion conjugation to $-\hat\Xi$, and Hermitian conjugation to $-\bar{\hat\Xi}$:

$$ (\tilde{Q})^{\natural} = R\exp(-\hat\Xi\Theta), \qquad \bar{\tilde{Q}} = \bar{R}\exp(\bar{\hat\Xi}\,\bar{\Theta}), \qquad \tilde{Q}^{*} = \bar{R}\exp(-\bar{\hat\Xi}\,\bar{\Theta}) . $$

conjugation $R$ $\hat\Xi$ $\Theta$ result
quaternion ${}^{\natural}$ $R$ $-\hat\Xi$ $\Theta$ $R\exp(-\hat\Xi\Theta)$
complex $\bar{\cdot}$ $\bar{R}$ $\bar{\hat\Xi}$ $\bar{\Theta}$ $\bar{R}\exp(\bar{\hat\Xi}\bar{\Theta})$
Hermitian ${}^{*}$ $\bar{R}$ $-\bar{\hat\Xi}$ $\bar{\Theta}$ $\bar{R}\exp(-\bar{\hat\Xi}\bar{\Theta})$

The consistency of the second row is a consequence of $\langle\bar{\tilde{Q}},\bar{\tilde{Q}}\rangle_{\natural} = \overline{\langle\tilde{Q},\tilde{Q}\rangle_{\natural}}$, hence $R(\bar{\tilde{Q}}) = \overline{R(\tilde{Q})}$: the modulus conjugates, the angle conjugates, and the axis conjugates coefficientwise. Quaternion conjugation is the operation that reverses the axis, complex conjugation the one that reverses nothing, and Hermitian conjugation the one that does both.

The Roots of One in the Two Exponential Forms

The Polar Word at a Root of One

The elements with $\tilde\Xi^2 = e_0$ are the square roots of the algebra unit. The norm is multiplicative, so taking norms in $\tilde\Xi^2 = e_0$ gives $\langle\tilde\Xi,\tilde\Xi\rangle_{\natural}^{2} = e_0$, and the norm of a root of one is one of the two square roots of the unit,

$$ \langle\tilde\Xi,\tilde\Xi\rangle_{\natural} = \pm e_0 . $$

Since $\langle\tilde\Xi,\tilde\Xi\rangle_{\natural} = r^2e^{2i\alpha}$ with $r>0$ and $\alpha\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2]$, the two signs are the two cases of the word.

The unit-norm roots. For $\langle\tilde\Xi,\tilde\Xi\rangle_{\natural} = e_0$ the modulus and the phase are trivial, $r = 1$ and $\alpha = 0$, and the word reduces to the Cartan case $\tilde\Xi = \tilde U_{\mathrm b}\tilde u_{\mathrm r}$ of §Degenerate Cases of The Polar Element Representation of Biquaternions. The roots of one of unit norm are exactly the central pair. The halving element $\tilde\Pi_- = \tfrac12(e_0-\tilde\Xi)$ is an idempotent,

$$ \tilde\Pi_-^2 = \tfrac14\bigl(e_0-2\tilde\Xi+\tilde\Xi^2\bigr) = \tfrac12(e_0-\tilde\Xi) = \tilde\Pi_- , $$

and the norm of an idempotent is an idempotent complex number, hence $0$ or $1$; here the norm is

$$ \langle\tilde\Pi_-,\tilde\Pi_-\rangle_{\natural} = \tfrac14\bigl(1+\langle\tilde\Xi,\tilde\Xi\rangle_{\natural}-2\Xi_0\bigr) = \tfrac12(1-\Xi_0), $$

so $\Xi_0 = \pm1$. Writing $\tilde\Xi = \Xi_0e_0+\vec\Xi$ with $\vec\Xi = \Xi_1e_1+\Xi_2e_2+\Xi_3e_3$, the square is $\tilde\Xi^2 = \bigl(\Xi_0^2-(\vec\Xi,\vec\Xi)\bigr)e_0+2\Xi_0\vec\Xi$, and its vector part $2\Xi_0\vec\Xi$ vanishes: $\tilde\Xi = \pm e_0$. The two elements $\pm e_0$ are the only involutions of unit norm in the algebra.

The roots of norm $-e_0$. For $\langle\tilde\Xi,\tilde\Xi\rangle_{\natural} = -e_0$ the modulus is $r = 1$ and the phase solves $e^{2i\alpha} = -1$, so $\alpha = \pi/2$, the phase factor is $e^{i\alpha} = i$, and the word is

$$ \tilde\Xi = i\,\tilde U_{\mathrm b}\,\tilde u_{\mathrm r} , \qquad (\tilde U_{\mathrm b}\tilde u_{\mathrm r})^2 = -e_0 . $$

The condition is not automatic: it says that the unit product $\tilde U_{\mathrm b}\tilde u_{\mathrm r}$ is itself a unit-norm root of $-e_0$. Read on the two axes, it says that the two axes are orthogonal and that the rotor is pure,

$$ \hat u_{\mathrm b}\perp\hat u_{\mathrm r} , \qquad \tilde u_{\mathrm r} = \hat u_{\mathrm r}\in\mathcal{A} \quad (\theta = \pi) , $$

so that the rotor is a point of the axis sphere, its stored half-angle is $\pi/2$ and its rotation angle is $\pi$. The equivalence is the classification of the roots of one of the plain algebra (Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the General Plain Algebra) read in the word. The family that remains has the four real parameters of that classification: the rapidity $\psi$, the rotor axis $\hat u_{\mathrm r}$ (two parameters), and the boost axis $\hat u_{\mathrm b}$ in the plane orthogonal to the rotor axis (one). The closure of the two factors, and the orthogonality of the axes with it, follows from $\tilde\Xi^2=e_0$ alone; the next subsection reads the two factors off the root directly, without the word.

A Direct Derivation Without the Polar Form

The two factors of a root can be read off the root directly, from $\tilde\Xi^2 = e_0$ and the norm alone, without passing through the polar word. Write the root as $\tilde\Xi = \Xi_0e_0+\vec\Xi$ with $\vec\Xi = \Xi_1e_1+\Xi_2e_2+\Xi_3e_3$ and $\Xi_\mu\in\mathbb{C}$. A pure vector squares to a scalar, $\vec\Xi^2 = -(\Xi_1^2+\Xi_2^2+\Xi_3^2)e_0$, so the square of the root is

$$ \tilde\Xi^2 = \Bigl(\Xi_0^2-\Xi_1^2-\Xi_2^2-\Xi_3^2\Bigr)e_0+2\,\Xi_0\,\vec\Xi . $$

The equation $\tilde\Xi^2 = e_0$ is therefore the pair of equations $2\Xi_0\vec\Xi = 0$ and $\Xi_0^2-\Xi_1^2-\Xi_2^2-\Xi_3^2 = 1$. Either $\vec\Xi = 0$, and then $\Xi_0 = \pm1$, which is the central pair $\pm e_0$; or $\Xi_0 = 0$, and then

$$ \Xi_1^2+\Xi_2^2+\Xi_3^2 = -1 . $$

In the second case the norm follows from the same pure-vector square, $\langle\tilde\Xi,\tilde\Xi\rangle_{\natural} = -\vec\Xi^2 = \bigl(\Xi_1^2+\Xi_2^2+\Xi_3^2\bigr)e_0 = -e_0$, so the non-central roots are exactly the roots of norm $-e_0$, and this too is a consequence of $\tilde\Xi^2 = e_0$ rather than a separate hypothesis.

Split each coefficient in the coordinates of the root. In the convention of the corpus a complex coefficient is written $Q_\mu = q_\mu+iq'_\mu$ with $q_\mu,q'_\mu\in\mathbb{R}$, and the two triples $(q_1,q_2,q_3)$ and $(q'_1,q'_2,q'_3)$ are the real and the imaginary coordinates of the element on the real basis $e_0,e_1,e_2,e_3,ie_0,ie_1,ie_2,ie_3$ (Biquaternions as a Vector Space over $\mathbb{C}$, §The Coordinate Systems). Writing the root's coordinates as $\Xi_\mu = \xi_\mu+i\xi'_\mu$ and collecting the two real triples $\vec\xi = (\xi_1,\xi_2,\xi_3)$ and $\vec\xi' = (\xi'_1,\xi'_2,\xi'_3)$ of $\mathbb{R}^3$, the single complex equation $\Xi_1^2+\Xi_2^2+\Xi_3^2 = -1$ becomes the two real equations

$$ \vec\xi\cdot\vec\xi' = 0 , \qquad \lvert\vec\xi'\rvert^2-\lvert\vec\xi\rvert^2 = 1 , $$

so the real and the imaginary coordinate triples of the root are orthogonal, and the imaginary triple is the longer one. Set

$$ \cosh\tfrac{\psi}{2} = \lvert\vec\xi'\rvert , \qquad \sinh\tfrac{\psi}{2} = \lvert\vec\xi\rvert , \qquad \hat u_{\mathrm r} = \frac{\vec\xi'}{\lvert\vec\xi'\rvert} , \qquad \hat u_{\mathrm b} = \Bigl(\frac{\vec\xi}{\lvert\vec\xi\rvert}\Bigr)\hat u_{\mathrm r} \quad (\vec\xi\neq0) . $$

The imaginary coordinate triple gives the rotor axis and the real coordinate triple the boost axis. The vector $\hat u_{\mathrm b}$ is a product of two orthogonal unit pure quaternions, hence itself a unit pure quaternion, orthogonal to $\hat u_{\mathrm r}$; its definition is exactly the closure condition of the polar word. Reassembling the coordinates gives the root in its two factors,

$$ \tilde\Xi = i\cosh\tfrac{\psi}{2}\,\hat u_{\mathrm r}-\sinh\tfrac{\psi}{2}\,\hat u_{\mathrm b}\hat u_{\mathrm r} = i\Bigl(\cosh\tfrac{\psi}{2}\,e_0+i\sinh\tfrac{\psi}{2}\,\hat u_{\mathrm b}\Bigr)\hat u_{\mathrm r} = i\,\tilde U_{\mathrm b}\,\tilde u_{\mathrm r} , $$

with $\tilde u_{\mathrm r} = \hat u_{\mathrm r}$ pure and $\hat u_{\mathrm b}\perp\hat u_{\mathrm r}$. The Hermitian case is $\vec\xi = 0$, where the real coordinates of the vector part vanish: then $\psi = 0$, $\tilde U_{\mathrm b} = e_0$, and $\tilde\Xi = i\hat u_{\mathrm r} = i\hat u_{\mathrm n}$ is the central pair's companion, the purely imaginary family.

Two consequences come for free. The factorisation is unique as soon as $\vec\xi\neq0$, because the pair of lengths $(\lvert\vec\xi\rvert,\lvert\vec\xi'\rvert)$ and the directions $\vec\xi/\lvert\vec\xi\rvert$, $\vec\xi'/\lvert\vec\xi'\rvert$ are read from the coordinates of the root; and the Hermitian roots are precisely the case $\vec\xi = 0$, in agreement with the Hermitian roots of the closing subsection. The polar word is therefore not needed for the factorisation of the roots: it names the same two objects, $\tilde U_{\mathrm b}$ and $\tilde u_{\mathrm r}$, and the direct reading above shows that on the root set they are forced by $\tilde\Xi^2 = e_0$ alone.

The Two Exponents of the Word

The two non-central factors are exponentials, and the elements whose exponentials they are, rather than the factors themselves, are the primitive data of the word. In the vocabulary of the polar article the exponent of a factor is the parameter times the root that generates it, and the two exponents here are

$$ \mathcal H = i\frac{\psi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm b} , \qquad \mathcal R = \frac{\theta}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm r} , \qquad \tilde U_{\mathrm b} = \exp(\mathcal H) , \qquad \tilde u_{\mathrm r} = \exp(\mathcal R) . $$

The two exponents are of opposite kinds, and this is the four-factor picture read on them. The exponent $\mathcal H$ is Hermitian, $\mathcal H^{*} = \mathcal H$, and lies in the Hermitian subspace $\mathbb{M}_+$; it is the real number $\frac{\psi}{2}$ times the Hermitian root $i\hat u_{\mathrm b}$ of $+e_0$, so that its square is the positive scalar $\mathcal H^2 = \frac{\psi^2}{4}e_0$ and its exponential is the hyperbolic factor. The exponent $\mathcal R$ is real and pure, $\mathcal R^{\natural} = -\mathcal R$ and $\mathcal R^{*} = -\mathcal R$, and lies in $\mathbb{M}_-\cap\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{B})$; it is $\frac{\theta}{2}$ times the real pure root $\hat u_{\mathrm r}$ of $-e_0$, so that its square is the negative scalar $\mathcal R^2 = -\frac{\theta^2}{4}e_0$ and its exponential is the trigonometric factor. One Hermitian exponent and one anti-Hermitian exponent: read on the exponents, the two non-central factors are the two halves of the Hermitian split of the exponent, which is the split that The Cartan Hermitian Pairing of the Polar Element Representation of Biquaternions reads at the level of the element.

The root of one on the exponents. On the root set the rotor is pure, so $\theta = \pi$, the rotor exponent is the fixed quarter turn $\mathcal R = \frac{\pi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm r}$, and the root and its halving idempotents read

$$ \tilde\Xi = i\exp(\mathcal H)\exp(\mathcal R) = i\exp\Bigl(i\frac{\psi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm b}\Bigr)\exp\Bigl(\frac{\pi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm r}\Bigr) , \qquad \tilde\Pi_\pm = \frac{1}{2}\Bigl(e_0\pm i\exp(\mathcal H)\exp(\mathcal R)\Bigr) . $$

The reading makes plain what the factor form leaves implicit. The rotor exponent is the same quarter turn for every root of the family, the roots being separated by the rapidity inside $\mathcal H$ and by the two directions, and the closing condition $\tilde\Xi^2 = e_0$ is exactly the orthogonality of those directions. On the exponents the roots of one are a one-parameter hyperbolic family generated by $i\frac{\psi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm b}$ together with the fixed $\frac{\pi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm r}$, over a pair of orthogonal unit directions.

The two exponents do not commute. Their bracket is

$$ [\mathcal H,\mathcal R] = i\frac{\psi\theta}{2}\bigl(\hat u_{\mathrm b}\times\hat u_{\mathrm r}\bigr) , $$

non-zero as soon as the two axes are not parallel. On the root set, where the axes are orthogonal and $\theta = \pi$, it is the Hermitian root $i\hat u_{\mathrm b}\hat u_{\mathrm r}$ of $+e_0$ multiplied by the real $\frac{\pi\psi}{2}$,

$$ [\mathcal H,\mathcal R] = i\frac{\pi\psi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm b}\hat u_{\mathrm r} , \qquad \bigl(i\hat u_{\mathrm b}\hat u_{\mathrm r}\bigr)^2 = e_0 , $$

of length $\frac{\pi\psi}{2}$. A non-zero bracket is exactly what forbids adding the exponents: $\exp(\mathcal H)\exp(\mathcal R)$ is the exponential of $\mathcal H+\mathcal R$ only when the bracket vanishes, and the roots of one are the elements on which it never does. The size of the failure is the subject of the next subsection.

The Two Factors and the Single Exponential

Whether the word adds is decided by the bracket of the two factors, and the roots of one are its extreme case. The bracket of the two factors is

$$ [\tilde U_{\mathrm b},\tilde u_{\mathrm r}] = 2i\sinh\frac{\psi}{2}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\bigl(\hat u_{\mathrm b}\times\hat u_{\mathrm r}\bigr) , $$

so the two factors commute exactly when the two axes are parallel or one of the two factors is central,

$$ [\tilde U_{\mathrm b},\tilde u_{\mathrm r}] = 0 \iff \hat u_{\mathrm b}\parallel\hat u_{\mathrm r} \ \text{ or } \ \psi = 0 \ \text{ or } \ \theta\in\{0,2\pi\} . $$

Call such a word aligned. Alignment is a condition of codimension two on the element, the two axes being two independent directions of the axis sphere, so a generic element is not aligned; the worked example below is aligned because its two axes were chosen collinear, which is a special choice.

The roots are never aligned. The axes of a root of one are orthogonal, so the bracket of a root is $2i\sinh\frac{\psi}{2}(\hat u_{\mathrm b}\times\hat u_{\mathrm r})$, which vanishes only at $\psi = 0$, the Hermitian root $i\hat u_{\mathrm r}$ whose boost is trivial. The roots of one sit at one extreme of the bracket and the aligned elements at the other.

An aligned word is a single exponential with a complex number inside. With $\hat u_{\mathrm b} = \epsilon\hat u_{\mathrm r} = \epsilon\hat u$ and $\epsilon = \pm1$, the two exponents add, their sum is one complex number times the common direction, and the product of the two factors is the single exponential

$$ \tilde U_{\mathrm b}\tilde u_{\mathrm r} = \exp(\mathcal H+\mathcal R) = \exp\Bigl(\Bigl(\frac{\theta}{2}+\epsilon i\frac{\psi}{2}\Bigr)\hat u\Bigr) = \cos\Bigl(\frac{\theta}{2}+\epsilon i\frac{\psi}{2}\Bigr)e_0+\sin\Bigl(\frac{\theta}{2}+\epsilon i\frac{\psi}{2}\Bigr)\hat u . $$

The number inside is the rotation half-angle plus $i$ times the rapidity half-angle, and it is exactly the complex angle of the Hamilton representation of that element: on the aligned words $\Theta = \frac{\theta}{2}+\epsilon i\frac{\psi}{2}$ and $\hat\Xi = \hat u$ up to sign. The aligned line is where the two halves of the article meet term by term, the complex angle of the element degenerating into the sum of its two exponents.

Away from the aligned line the exponents do not add. The product is then not the exponential of the sum, and the gap is the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff series of the two exponents,

$$ \Theta\hat\Xi = \mathcal H+\mathcal R+\frac{1}{2}[\mathcal H,\mathcal R]+\frac{1}{12}\bigl[\mathcal H,[\mathcal H,\mathcal R]\bigr]-\frac{1}{12}\bigl[\mathcal R,[\mathcal H,\mathcal R]\bigr]+\cdots , $$

whose leading term is half the bracket. On 100 random pairs with the rapidity and the rotation angle both between $0.02$ and $0.12$ the relative deviation $\lvert\Theta\hat\Xi-(\mathcal H+\mathcal R)\rvert/\lvert\Theta\hat\Xi\rvert$, in the Euclidean norm of the coefficients, is at most $3.9\times10^{-2}$, falls to $1.0\times10^{-3}$ when the term $\frac{1}{2}[\mathcal H,\mathcal R]$ is added, and to $2.1\times10^{-5}$ when the two cubic terms are added.

The single exponential does not need the alignment. The product $\tilde U_{\mathrm b}\tilde u_{\mathrm r}$ has norm one and is the element $\tilde Q$ stripped of its central modulus $R$, and dividing by the central $R$ leaves the Hamilton axis and the complex angle unchanged, both being read from the ratio of the parts of the element. The product is therefore a single exponential with a complex number inside on the whole domain,

$$ \tilde U_{\mathrm b}\tilde u_{\mathrm r} = \exp(\hat\Xi\Theta) , $$

with the same root $\hat\Xi$ of $-e_0$ and the same complex $\Theta$ as the element, its exponent $\Theta\hat\Xi$ being a complex multiple of a root. The single exponential is thus the reward of the norm being one, which is general, and not of the commutativity, which is exceptional; what commutativity buys is only the identity of the exponent $\Theta\hat\Xi$ with the sum $\mathcal H+\mathcal R$ of the two given exponents.

On the root set the complex number collapses to a real one. The extracted element $-i\tilde\Xi = \tilde U_{\mathrm b}\tilde u_{\mathrm r}$ is pure, so $\cos\Theta = 0$, the complex angle is the real quarter turn $\Theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$, and the extracted element, a unit-norm root of $-e_0$, is its own quarter-turn exponential,

$$ -i\tilde\Xi = \tilde U_{\mathrm b}\tilde u_{\mathrm r} = \exp\Bigl(\hat\Xi'\frac{\pi}{2}\Bigr) = \hat\Xi' , \qquad \hat\Xi' = -i\tilde\Xi . $$

The roots of one are therefore the elements furthest from the aligned case and, at the same time, the elements whose single exponential carries a real number in place of the complex one. The complex angle is not lost with them: it is the aligned words that carry the genuinely complex number $\frac{\theta}{2}+\epsilon i\frac{\psi}{2}$ of the subsection above.

The Idempotent Exponential Form of a Root of One

Every root of one has a one-exponential form, built on the same halving element. Since $\tilde\Pi_-$ is idempotent its exponential series truncates,

$$ \exp\bigl(i\pi\tilde\Pi_-\bigr) = e_0 + \bigl(e^{i\pi}-1\bigr)\tilde\Pi_- = e_0 - 2\tilde\Pi_- , $$

and the last element is the root itself:

$$ \tilde\Xi = \exp\bigl(i\pi\tilde\Pi_-\bigr), \qquad \tilde\Pi_- = \tfrac12(e_0-\tilde\Xi), \qquad \tilde\Pi_-^2 = \tilde\Pi_- . $$

The form is a single exponential of one element, where the polar word is an ordered product of four. The two ends of the halving are the two trivial roots: $\tilde\Xi = e_0$ gives $\tilde\Pi_- = 0$ and $\exp(0) = e_0$, while $\tilde\Xi = -e_0$ gives $\tilde\Pi_- = e_0$ and $\exp(i\pi) = -e_0$. The exponent $i\pi\tilde\Pi_-$ is not the sum of the four exponents of the word, and the two are two different factorisations of the same root set. Written on the two exponents of the word, the same idempotent is the halving $\tilde\Pi_- = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(e_0-i\exp(\mathcal H)\exp(\mathcal R)\bigr)$ of §The Two Exponents of the Word.

The Bridge Between the Two Forms

The two forms are tied by the product of the last two factors of the word, read at a root of one. On the roots of norm $-e_0$ the word is $\tilde\Xi = i\,\tilde U_{\mathrm b}\,\tilde u_{\mathrm r}$, so that product is recovered from the root by the left multiplication by $-i$,

$$ -i\,\tilde\Xi = \tilde U_{\mathrm b}\,\tilde u_{\mathrm r} , \qquad \bigl(-i\,\tilde\Xi\bigr)^2 = -e_0 , \qquad \langle -i\,\tilde\Xi , -i\,\tilde\Xi \rangle_{\natural} = e_0 , $$

the first equality the extraction, the second the statement that the extracted element is a root of $-e_0$, and the third that it has unit norm; its argument is $0$, so its phase is trivial and the extraction is already the polar factorisation of the read element. The element $-i\tilde\Xi$ is therefore a unit-norm root of $-e_0$ whenever the root has norm $-e_0$, and it is the product of the last two factors of the word. On the two central roots it is instead $-i$ and $+i$, both of norm $-e_0$, and the extraction carries no polar information.

Substituting the extraction into the halving idempotent and into the idempotent form gives both directions in closed shape,

$$ \tilde\Pi_- = \tfrac12\bigl(e_0-\tilde\Xi\bigr) = \tfrac12\bigl(e_0-i\,\tilde U_{\mathrm b}\,\tilde u_{\mathrm r}\bigr) , \qquad \tilde U_{\mathrm b}\,\tilde u_{\mathrm r} = i\,\bigl(2\tilde\Pi_--e_0\bigr) , $$

so the halving idempotent determines the product of the last two factors completely, and conversely. The exponent of the single exponential is $i\pi\tilde\Pi_-$, which the extraction expands into a central part and a two-factor part,

$$ \tilde\Xi = \exp\bigl(i\pi\tilde\Pi_-\bigr) = \exp\Bigl(\tfrac{i\pi}{2}e_0+\tfrac{\pi}{2}\,\tilde U_{\mathrm b}\,\tilde u_{\mathrm r}\Bigr) = \exp\Bigl(i\pi\,\frac{e_0-i\,\tilde U_{\mathrm b}\,\tilde u_{\mathrm r}}{2}\Bigr) , $$

the central part fixed at $\tfrac{i\pi}{2}e_0$ and the two-factor part the last two factors of the word multiplied by the real half-turn $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$. The exponent is not the sum of the four exponents of the word, and the polar word is the polar decomposition of the root of $-e_0$ read from the root by $-i\tilde\Xi$.

The Hermitian Roots

The roots of one that are Hermitian, $\tilde\Xi^{*} = \tilde\Xi$, are the elements whose word carries a trivial boost. On a root of norm $-e_0$ the word is $\tilde\Xi = i\tilde U_{\mathrm b}\tilde u_{\mathrm r}$ with $\tilde u_{\mathrm r} = \hat u_{\mathrm r}$ pure and the axes orthogonal, and its Hermitian conjugate is $\tilde\Xi^{*} = i\hat u_{\mathrm r}\tilde U_{\mathrm b}$; the two differ by $-2\sinh\tfrac{\psi}{2}\,\hat u_{\mathrm b}\hat u_{\mathrm r}$, which vanishes exactly when the boost is trivial. The Hermitian roots of one are therefore

$$ \tilde\Xi = \pm e_0 , \qquad \tilde\Xi = i\hat u_{\mathrm n} \quad \text{over the unit pure real quaternions } \hat u_{\mathrm n} , $$

the central pair and the purely imaginary family, and in the idempotent form the halving element of a Hermitian root is Hermitian.

The Two Pure Elements as the Source of the Word and of the Idempotents

The whole section is generated by two pure elements.

The two exponents. The source is the two exponents of §The Two Exponents of the Word, the Hermitian $i\frac{\psi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm b}$ of the boost and the quarter turn $\frac{\pi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm r}$ of the rotor, with their data the rapidity $\psi$ and two orthogonal unit pure quaternions of $\mathbb{R}^3$, the boost axis $\hat u_{\mathrm b}$ and the rotor axis $\hat u_{\mathrm r}$. Their product is again a unit pure quaternion, perpendicular to both,

$$ \hat u_{\mathrm b}\perp\hat u_{\mathrm r} , \qquad \bigl(\hat u_{\mathrm b}\hat u_{\mathrm r}\bigr)^2 = -e_0 , \qquad \hat u_{\mathrm b}\perp\hat u_{\mathrm b}\hat u_{\mathrm r} , \qquad \hat u_{\mathrm r}\perp\hat u_{\mathrm b}\hat u_{\mathrm r} , $$

and the root is written along the rotor axis and that product.

The root of one. The rotor takes the half-turn about its axis, $\tilde u_{\mathrm r} = \exp\bigl(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm r}\bigr) = \hat u_{\mathrm r}$, and the boost takes the rapidity $\psi$ about its axis, $\tilde U_{\mathrm b} = \exp\bigl(i\tfrac{\psi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm b}\bigr)$; with the central $i$, the two factors give the root of one,

$$ \tilde\Xi = i\,\tilde U_{\mathrm b}\,\tilde u_{\mathrm r} = i\cosh\tfrac{\psi}{2}\,\hat u_{\mathrm r}-\sinh\tfrac{\psi}{2}\,\hat u_{\mathrm b}\hat u_{\mathrm r} , \qquad \tilde\Xi^2 = e_0 , \qquad \langle\tilde\Xi,\tilde\Xi\rangle_{\natural} = -e_0 . $$

It is a pure element, with no scalar part, lying in the plane of the two orthogonal unit pure directions $\hat u_{\mathrm r}$ and $\hat u_{\mathrm b}\hat u_{\mathrm r}$. At zero rapidity it is the Hermitian root $\tilde\Xi = i\hat u_{\mathrm r}$, a single pure direction, whose axis $\hat u_{\mathrm n} = \hat u_{\mathrm r}$ is that of the subsection above; the boost switches the second direction on, and that is the whole of the non-Hermitian part. The two exponents are then the Hermitian $i\frac{\psi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm b}$ and the real pure $\frac{\pi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm r}$, and they do not commute: their bracket, computed in §The Two Exponents of the Word, is the Hermitian root $i\frac{\pi\psi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm b}\hat u_{\mathrm r}$, so the root is generated by the two directions through a product that is not the exponential of their sum.

The element. The same two non-central factors, dialled by the central scale and the central phase, are the general element,

$$ \tilde Q = r\,e^{i\alpha}\,\tilde U_{\mathrm b}\,\tilde u_{\mathrm r} = \bigl(r\,e^{i\alpha}\bigr)\cdot\bigl(\text{the two non-central factors}\bigr) , $$

so the root is the central dial $i$ and the element is the central dial $r e^{i\alpha}$ on the same two factors. When the two factors close, the element and the root are central multiples of one another, $\tilde Q = -i\,r\,e^{i\alpha}\,\tilde\Xi$; in general the element carries its own two factors, of the same kind, and the root is the closing case of them.

The idempotents and the Peirce decomposition. A root of one is carried by the halving map into the complementary idempotent pair $\tilde\Pi_\pm = \tfrac12(e_0\pm\tilde\Xi)$, and the pair splits the algebra into four Peirce pieces,

$$ \mathbb{B} = \tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+ \;\oplus\; \tilde\Pi_+\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_- \;\oplus\; \tilde\Pi_-\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_+ \;\oplus\; \tilde\Pi_-\mathbb{B}\tilde\Pi_- , $$

each of real dimension two for a root other than $\pm e_0$, the four summing to the real dimension eight of the algebra; the pieces are the ideals of Ideals and Peirce Decomposition of the General Plain Algebra and the Hermitian idempotents of Hermitian Idempotents and the Peirce Decomposition. The chain is thus

$$ \text{two pure elements} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{the root of one} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{the idempotents} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{the Peirce decomposition} , $$

and the same two pure elements, with their angles and the central dial, give the general element.

Worked Examples

The Generic Element

Take $\tilde{Q} = (1+i)e_0+e_1+2e_2$, the generic element of the partial article's tables. Its polar representation is

$$ r = 2.320595787, \quad \alpha = 0.190253189, \quad \tilde U_{\mathrm b} = 1.072349609\,e_0 - 0.173166789\,i\,e_1 - 0.346333577\,i\,e_2, \quad \tilde u_{\mathrm r} = 0.470592172\,e_0+0.394599292\,e_1+0.789198585\,e_2 . $$

The Hamilton regrouping. The vector part is $\vec Q = e_1+2e_2$, so $(\vec Q,\vec Q) = 5$ and $B = \sqrt{5} = 2.236067978$; the axis is $\hat\Xi = (e_1+2e_2)/\sqrt{5} = 0.447213595\,e_1+0.894427191\,e_2$, which satisfies $\hat\Xi^2 = -e_0$. The modulus is $R = \rho = 2.278723854+0.438842117i$, and the complex angle is fixed by

$$ \cos\Theta = \frac{Q_0}{R} = 0.504639332+0.341657461i, \qquad \sin\Theta = \frac{\sqrt{5}}{R} = 0.946188580-0.182219271i , $$

so that $\tilde{Q} = R(\cos\Theta+\hat\Xi\sin\Theta)$; the identity $\exp(\hat\Xi\Theta) = \tilde U_{\mathrm b}\tilde u_{\mathrm r}$ then holds term by term, both sides being $\tilde{Q}/R$, and with it the equality of the Hamilton representation with the first pairing of the four factors.

The two exponents. The polar data also give the two exponents of the word,

$$ \mathcal H = i\frac{\psi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm b} = -0.169107833\,i\,e_1-0.338215666\,i\,e_2 , \qquad \mathcal R = \frac{\theta}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm r} = 0.483363900\,e_1+0.966727800\,e_2 , $$

and here $\hat u_{\mathrm b} = -\hat u_{\mathrm r}$, so the word is aligned and the exponents add. Their sum is

$$ \mathcal H+\mathcal R = \bigl(0.483363900-0.169107833i\bigr)e_1+\bigl(0.966727800-0.338215666i\bigr)e_2 , $$

the complex number $\frac{\theta}{2}-i\frac{\psi}{2} = 1.080834538-0.378136610i$ times the rotor axis, and it is exactly the Hamilton exponent: $\Theta\hat\Xi = \mathcal H+\mathcal R$ to machine precision, with $\Theta = 1.080834538-0.378136610i$ and $\hat\Xi = \hat u_{\mathrm r}$. The alignment here is an accident of the example, whose two axes were chosen collinear; the third example below has the skew case.

An Element at a Root of One

Take the rotor axis $\hat u_{\mathrm r} = (e_1+e_2)/\sqrt2$ and a boost axis orthogonal to it, $\hat u_{\mathrm b} = (e_1-e_2)/\sqrt2$, with the rapidity fixed by $\cosh\tfrac{\psi}{2} = \tfrac{5}{4}$ and $\sinh\tfrac{\psi}{2} = \tfrac{3}{4}$. The two axes multiply to the third pure direction, $\hat u_{\mathrm b}\hat u_{\mathrm r} = e_3$, and the root is

$$ \tilde\Xi = i\cosh\tfrac{\psi}{2}\,\hat u_{\mathrm r}-\sinh\tfrac{\psi}{2}\,\hat u_{\mathrm b}\hat u_{\mathrm r} = 0.883883476\,i\,e_1+0.883883476\,i\,e_2-0.75\,e_3 , \qquad \tilde\Xi^2 = e_0 , \qquad \langle\tilde\Xi,\tilde\Xi\rangle_{\natural} = -e_0 . $$

Its halving idempotent and the product of the last two factors of the word are

$$ \tilde\Pi_- = \tfrac12(e_0-\tilde\Xi) = 0.5\,e_0-0.441941738\,i\,e_1-0.441941738\,i\,e_2+0.375\,e_3 , \qquad -i\tilde\Xi = \tilde U_{\mathrm b}\tilde u_{\mathrm r} = \tfrac{5}{4}\hat u_{\mathrm r}+i\tfrac{3}{4}\,e_3 , $$

and the root is the single exponential of its halving idempotent, $\tilde\Xi = \exp(i\pi\tilde\Pi_-)$. The element is Hermitian only at zero rapidity, where it reduces to $i\hat u_{\mathrm r}$.

The two exponents and the bracket. The exponents of the word are

$$ \mathcal H = i\frac{\psi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm b} = 0.490129072\,i\,e_1-0.490129072\,i\,e_2 , \qquad \mathcal R = \frac{\pi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm r} = 1.110720735\,e_1+1.110720735\,e_2 , $$

with $\psi = 1.386294361$, and their bracket is $[\mathcal H,\mathcal R] = i\frac{\pi\psi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm b}\hat u_{\mathrm r} = 2.177586090\,i\,e_3$, of length $2.177586090$. The two axes are orthogonal, so the root is not aligned and the exponents do not add. The Hamilton data of the root show the gap: its complex angle is the real quarter turn $\Theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$, and $\Theta\hat\Xi$ differs from $\mathcal H+\mathcal R$ by $1.178097$ in the largest coefficient, against $1.088793$ for half the bracket, the leading term of the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff series.

An Element with Skew Axes

Take the rotor axis $\hat u_{\mathrm r} = (e_1+e_2)/\sqrt2$, the boost axis $\hat u_{\mathrm b} = e_2$, the rotation angle $\theta = 1.2$ and the rapidity $\psi = 0.8$. The two directions meet at $45$ degrees, so the word is neither aligned nor orthogonal, and the product of the two factors is

$$ \tilde U_{\mathrm b}\tilde u_{\mathrm r} = \bigl(0.892247531-0.163998009i\bigr)e_0+0.431631682\,e_1+\bigl(0.431631682+0.339008523i\bigr)e_2-0.163998009\,i\,e_3 . $$

The exponents and their bracket are

$$ \mathcal H = 0.4\,i\,e_2 , \qquad \mathcal R = 0.424264069\,e_1+0.424264069\,e_2 , \qquad [\mathcal H,\mathcal R] = i\frac{\psi\theta}{2}\bigl(\hat u_{\mathrm b}\times\hat u_{\mathrm r}\bigr) = -0.339411255\,i\,e_3 , $$

and the exponents do not add: $\exp(\mathcal H+\mathcal R)$ differs from the product by $0.163998009$ in the largest coefficient. The single exponential is nevertheless there, with the genuinely complex angle and the root of $-e_0$

$$ \Theta = 0.551850513+0.307926415i , \qquad \hat\Xi = \bigl(0.636163468-0.308495063i\bigr)e_1+\bigl(0.878459065+0.191155095i\bigr)e_2-\bigl(0.117212379+0.241709650i\bigr)e_3 , $$

so that $\tilde U_{\mathrm b}\tilde u_{\mathrm r} = \cos\Theta\,e_0+\sin\Theta\,\hat\Xi$, and the exponent $\Theta\hat\Xi$ is a complex multiple of a root of $-e_0$ whose difference from $\mathcal H+\mathcal R$ is $0.169760320$, against $0.169705627$ for half the bracket: away from the aligned line the leading Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff term accounts for the gap.

Summary

The Hamilton representation writes a biquaternion of its domain as $R\exp(\hat\Xi\Theta)$, the complex modulus $R = re^{i\alpha}$ times the exponential of the root $\hat\Xi = \vec Q/B$ of $-1$ that is parallel to the vector part. Its domain is the complement of the union of the null cone and the quadric cone $(\vec Q,\vec Q) = 0$, on which the axis has no normalisation; the four-factor grouping $\tilde{Q} = (re^{i\alpha})(\tilde U_{\mathrm b}\tilde u_{\mathrm r})$ exists on the larger domain of the polar representation, and it is the construction of the modulus and the angle from the parts of the element, not the grouping, that fails. The three parameters $R$, $\hat\Xi$ and $\Theta$ are fixed only up to the correlated replacements of the axis, of the angle and of the modulus; the branches of the polar representation remove the first and the third, and the residue is the multivaluedness of the logarithm.

Under the three conjugations the Hamilton data transform by the table above: quaternion conjugation fixes the modulus and the angle and reverses the axis, complex conjugation conjugates the modulus, the axis and the angle and reverses nothing, and Hermitian conjugation conjugates the modulus and the angle and both conjugates and reverses the axis.

The root half of the article reads the same word at a root of one, $\tilde\Xi^2 = e_0$. The norm of a root is $\pm e_0$; the unit-norm roots are the central pair $\pm e_0$, and the roots of norm $-e_0$ are $i\tilde U_{\mathrm b}\tilde u_{\mathrm r}$ with the boost axis orthogonal to a pure rotor. The same root set has the idempotent form $\tilde\Xi = \exp(i\pi\tilde\Pi_-)$, a single exponential of the halving idempotent $\tilde\Pi_- = \tfrac12(e_0-\tilde\Xi)$, and the two forms are tied by the product of the last two factors of the word, $-i\tilde\Xi = \tilde U_{\mathrm b}\tilde u_{\mathrm r}$. The whole construction is generated by two orthogonal unit pure quaternions, the two axes, and the chain runs from those two directions to the root, to the idempotents and to the Peirce decomposition of the algebra.

The word has two exponents, the Hermitian $\mathcal H = i\frac{\psi}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm b}$ of the hyperbolic factor and the real pure $\mathcal R = \frac{\theta}{2}\hat u_{\mathrm r}$ of the trigonometric one, and the two factors commute exactly when the two axes are parallel or one of the two factors is central. On the aligned words the exponents add and the product is a single exponential with the explicit complex number $\frac{\theta}{2}+\epsilon i\frac{\psi}{2}$ on the common axis, which is the complex angle $\Theta$ degenerated into the sum of the exponents; elsewhere the sum is corrected by the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff series, whose leading term is half the bracket $i\frac{\psi\theta}{2}(\hat u_{\mathrm b}\times\hat u_{\mathrm r})$. The single exponential with a complex number inside does not depend on the alignment: the product of the two factors has norm one, and its exponent is the complex multiple $\Theta\hat\Xi$ of a root of $-e_0$, the same root and the same angle as the element whose modulus is stripped away. The roots of one are the extreme of the bracket, their two axes being orthogonal, and the extreme of the complex angle in the other direction, the extracted root of $-e_0$ carrying the real quarter turn $\frac{\pi}{2}$.
