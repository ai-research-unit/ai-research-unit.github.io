Idempotents of the General Quaternionic Algebra

Introduction

This article classifies the idempotents of the quaternion algebra $\mathbb{H}$ read through the general quaternionic bilinear product, and it is the real member of the general quaternionic family whose complex sibling is Idempotents of the General Quaternionic Algebra.

An idempotent is relative to a product, and the definition is stated once in Definitions for the Study of the 6 Algebraic Structures, §The Element Vocabulary; throughout this article the product is the general quaternionic product $\tilde p\star\tilde q=\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde q$, and the unqualified "idempotent" means an idempotent of it. The article rests on one computation: the quaternionic square of every element is the central element $N(\tilde p)e_0$, so an idempotent is central and the idempotent equation collapses to $A^2=A$ on the scalar line. The answer is as small as it could be: the idempotents of the product are $0$ and $e_0$ alone, and the product has no nontrivial idempotent.

The companion article of the complex chapter, Idempotents of the General Quaternionic Algebra, gives the same answer, $0$ and $e_0$; the contrast is with the plain product, whose complex idempotents are the Hermitian idempotents $\tfrac12(e_0+i\hat\mu)$ over the real unit vectors $\hat\mu$, a family of complex dimension two lying on the zero-divisor cone, and the general quaternionic product displaces each of them into a square-zero element (Idempotents of the General Plain Algebra). Over $\mathbb{R}$ neither the family nor the cone exists: there is no central imaginary unit to build the Hermitian idempotents, and the definite norm leaves no isotropic direction, so the plain idempotents collapse to the same two trivial points as the idempotents of the general quaternionic product. The article gives the classification, names the absent correspondence with the roots of $+e_0$, reads the idempotents as projectors on the operator side, and records the collapse on the remarkable subspaces.

Boundaries. The idempotents of the plain product and the Hermitian idempotents of the complex chapter are Idempotents of the General Plain Algebra; the square, the norm and the isotope reading are Introduction to the General Quaternionic Algebra of Quaternions and Norm and Invertibility in the General Quaternionic Algebra; the roots of the three central values are Square Roots of Minus One, Zero and Plus One in the General Quaternionic Algebra; the zero divisors are Zero Divisors of the General Quaternionic Algebra, above this article in this category, and the nilpotents are Nilpotents of the General Quaternionic Algebra, below it; the structure-level comparison of the six idempotent sets is Idempotents in the 6 Algebraic Structures over the Quaternion $\mathbb{R}$ Space; the projectors and the operator theory are The Left Multiplications of the Quaternionic Product and the Opposite Monoid and The Specificities of the General Quaternionic Algebra, both below this article in this category; and the complex sibling is Idempotents of the General Quaternionic Algebra.

Conventions

$\mathbb{H}$ is the real quaternion algebra with basis $e_0=1,e_1,e_2,e_3$, the rules $e_k^2=-e_0$ and $e_1e_2=e_3$. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the coefficientwise conjugation is the identity and the Hermitian star coincides with the natural conjugation, so the block carries the single involution

$$ \tilde p^{\natural}=p_0-\mathbf p,\qquad \tilde p=p_0e_0+\mathbf p,\quad \mathbf p=p_1e_1+p_2e_2+p_3e_3 . $$

Juxtaposition is the plain product and $\star$ is the general quaternionic bilinear product $\tilde p\star\tilde q=\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde q$. The algebraic norm is

$$ N(\tilde p)=\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde p=p_0^2+|\mathbf p|^2=\sum_{\mu=0}^{3}p_\mu^2 , $$

positive definite, with polar form $\beta(\tilde p,\tilde q)=p_0q_0+\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf q$. The centre is the scalar line $\mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{H}}=\mathbb{R}e_0$ and the vector subspace is $\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})=\{\mathbf p\}$; the two are supplementary and orthogonal for the norm.

Definition, and the Square

Definition. Let $\star$ be a product on $\mathbb{H}$ and let $\tilde\pi\in\mathbb{H}$. The element $\tilde\pi$ is idempotent of $\star$ when $\tilde\pi\star\tilde\pi=\tilde\pi$; the trivial idempotents are $0$ and the left unit $e_0$, and an idempotent different from both is nontrivial. The definition is Definitions for the Study of the 6 Algebraic Structures, §The Element Vocabulary, and it names the product.

Lemma (the square is central). For every $\tilde p\in\mathbb{H}$,

$$ \tilde p\star\tilde p=\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde p=N(\tilde p)\,e_0 , \qquad N(\tilde p)=p_0^2+p_1^2+p_2^2+p_3^2 . $$

Proof. With $\tilde p=p_0e_0+\mathbf p$ the product has scalar part $p_0^2+\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf p$ and vector part $p_0\mathbf p-p_0\mathbf p-\mathbf p\times\mathbf p=0$ at equal arguments; the square is $N(\tilde p)e_0$ (Introduction to the General Quaternionic Algebra of Quaternions, and Square Roots of Minus One, Zero and Plus One in the General Quaternionic Algebra, §The Reduction of the Equation).

The lemma is the reason the idempotent equation is trivial here and nontrivial for the plain product. The plain square of the same element is $p_0^2e_0+2p_0\mathbf p+\mathbf p^2$, whose vector part is not obliged to vanish; the quaternionic square carries no vector part at all, and the equation is a quadratic in one real variable.

The Classification

Theorem (idempotents). The solutions of $\tilde\pi\star\tilde\pi=\tilde\pi$ are $\tilde\pi=0$ and $\tilde\pi=e_0$; the general quaternionic product has no nontrivial idempotent.

Proof. By the lemma the left-hand side is $N(\tilde\pi)e_0$, an element of the centre $\mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{H}}$, so an idempotent is central, $\tilde\pi=Ae_0$ with $A\in\mathbb{R}$. For a central element the equation reads $A^2e_0=Ae_0$, that is $A^2=A$, or $A(A-1)=0$; the field $\mathbb{R}$ gives $A\in\{0,1\}$. Both values satisfy the equation.

Equivalently, in coordinates: the equality $|p|^2e_0=p_0e_0+\mathbf p$ of the lemma forces the vector part $\mathbf p$ to vanish and the scalar equation $|p|^2=p_0$ to hold; with $\mathbf p=0$ this is $p_0^2=p_0$, whose roots are $0$ and $1$.

Remark (the equation is weaker than centrality). The single scalar equation $N(\tilde\pi)=\pi_0$ is not idempotence; it must be read with centrality attached. Every pure element $\mathbf p$ with $\mathbf p\neq0$ has $N(\mathbf p)=|\mathbf p|^2\neq0$ and is not central, so it fails the equation and is not an idempotent; and the central element $-e_0$, of norm $1$, satisfies $N=1\neq\pi_0=-1$ and is not idempotent either. The scalar equation alone does not decide idempotence.

The Idempotents and the Roots of Plus One

The idempotent equation and the equation of the square roots of a central value are two different equations on the same square, and over $\mathbb{R}$ their solution sets do not match.

Proposition (the roots of $+e_0$). The roots of $+e_0$ are the elements of the norm-one level set, $$ \{\tilde\xi:\tilde\xi\star\tilde\xi=e_0\}=\{\tilde\xi:N(\tilde\xi)=1\} , $$ the unit sphere $\mathrm{Sp}(1)$ of the quaternion algebra, of real dimension three; every root of $+e_0$ is a unit of norm one, with the two-sided inverse $\zeta(\tilde\xi)=\tilde\xi$. The statement is Square Roots of Minus One, Zero and Plus One in the General Quaternionic Algebra, §The Three Root Sets.

Theorem (only $e_0$ is both). Among the roots of $+e_0$ the only idempotent is $e_0$.

Proof. An idempotent of the block is $0$ or $e_0$; $0$ has norm $0$ and is not a root of $+e_0$, while $e_0$ has norm $1$ and is. Hence the intersection of the idempotent set $\{0,e_0\}$ with the norm-one sphere is the single point $e_0$.

Corollary (no bijection). The idempotents of the block and the roots of $+e_0$ do not correspond: the root set is the three-dimensional sphere $\mathrm{Sp}(1)$ and the idempotent set is the two-point set $\{0,e_0\}$, and the two have neither the same cardinality nor the same dimension. The bijection that the plain algebra of the complex chapter carries, from the roots of $-1$ to the idempotents by $\tilde\xi\mapsto\tfrac12(e_0+\tilde\xi i)$ with inverse $\tilde\Pi\mapsto(2\tilde\Pi-e_0)/i$, has no analogue here. The inverse map is affine, $\tilde\Pi\mapsto 2\tilde\Pi-e_0$, followed by the division by the central imaginary unit $i$; over $\mathbb{R}$ the centre is the field $\mathbb{R}$ itself, with no root of $-1$ and no central imaginary unit, and the block has no two-sided unit on which to build the correspondence, so the map cannot be completed and the correspondence has no analogue. The Hermitian family itself, $\tfrac12(e_0+i\hat\mu)$ over the real unit vectors $\hat\mu$, has no real counterpart, and its members are square-zero rather than idempotent for the general quaternionic product even after the central unit $i$ is adjoined (Idempotents of the General Plain Algebra, the complex chapter, for the Hermitian family and its displacement).

The Trivial Family and Orthogonality

The idempotents are $0$ and $e_0$, and the two form the whole family.

Definition. Two idempotents $\tilde\pi,\tilde\rho$ are orthogonal when $\tilde\pi\star\tilde\rho=0$; a family of pairwise orthogonal idempotents is complete when its members sum to $e_0$.

Proposition (the family). The idempotent set is $\{0,e_0\}$; the two are orthogonal, the origin being orthogonal to every element; there is no nontrivial idempotent and no pair of nontrivial orthogonal idempotents. The only complete orthogonal family of nonzero idempotents is $\{e_0\}$.

Proof. The classification gives the set. For orthogonality, $0\star e_0=0$ and $0\star0=0$, while $e_0\star e_0=e_0\neq0$; a nontrivial idempotent does not exist, so no pair of them can be orthogonal. A complete family of nonzero orthogonal idempotents summing to $e_0$ is available only as $\{e_0\}$, since any member other than $e_0$ would be a nontrivial idempotent, and $e_0$ cannot be split, $e_0$ being of norm one and the only idempotent of norm one.

The Idempotents as Projections

On the operator side the idempotents of the plain algebra recover their usual role, with the index conjugated, and over $\mathbb{R}$ the family is as small as the element family.

Theorem (the projector criterion). The left multiplication $L_{\tilde p}(\tilde x)=\tilde p\star\tilde x$ is a projector, $L_{\tilde p}^{2}=L_{\tilde p}$, if and only if $\tilde p$ is an idempotent of the plain product. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the plain idempotents are $0$ and $e_0$ alone, so the only projectors of the form $L_{\tilde p}$ are the zero operator and the identity.

Proof. $L_{\tilde p}=L^{\mathrm{plain}}_{\tilde p^{\natural}}$ by the isotope identity, and the plain left multiplications are faithful; so $L_{\tilde p}^{2}=L_{\tilde p}$ exactly when $(\tilde p^{\natural})^2=\tilde p^{\natural}$, that is when $\tilde p^{\natural}$, equivalently $\tilde p$, is a plain idempotent. The plain idempotents are $0$ and $e_0$, and $L_{e_0}\tilde x=e_0^{\natural}\tilde x=\tilde x$ is the identity while $L_0$ is zero. The theorem and the monoid law $L_{\tilde p}L_{\tilde q}=L_{\tilde q\tilde p}$ are The Left Multiplications of the Quaternionic Product and the Opposite Monoid.

Remark. The complex sibling carries a two-parameter family of projectors, indexed by the plain idempotents, whose elements are not idempotents of the product. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the indexing set is the two-point set $\{0,e_0\}$, so the projector family is the trivial pair, and the displacement it records in the complex block has no real content.

The Idempotents on the Remarkable Subspaces

Since the square is always central, the equation $\tilde\pi\star\tilde\pi=\tilde\pi$ can hold inside a subspace only when the subspace contains the two trivial idempotents; reading it on the two remarkable subspaces records which ones do.

Proposition (the idempotent equation on the two subspaces). On the centre the idempotents are $0$ and $e_0$; on the vector subspace the only idempotent is $0$.

subspace element $\tilde\pi\star\tilde\pi$ idempotents
$\mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{H}}$, the centre $Ae_0$ $A^2e_0$ $0$, $e_0$
$\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})$, the vector subspace $\mathbf p$ $\lvert\mathbf p\rvert^2e_0$ $0$

Proof. Each entry is the algebraic norm on the subspace of the same row, by the lemma. In the centre the equation leaves $A^2=A$, hence $A\in\{0,1\}$. In the vector subspace a central element equals a vector only at $0$; a nonzero pure element has square $|\mathbf p|^2e_0$, a nonzero central element, which differs from the pure $\mathbf p$, so the origin alone is idempotent.

Remark. The centre carries the two trivial idempotents and the vector subspace only $0$; neither remarkable subspace carries a nontrivial idempotent. The reading is the collapse of the complex table, which has six subspaces and carries the Hermitian idempotents in the Hermitian subspace; over $\mathbb{R}$ there is no Hermitian subspace, the two complex subspaces that would carry them having no real trace with a nontrivial idempotent.

The Peirce Decomposition It Does Not Carry

A nonzero idempotent of an associative unital algebra carries the Peirce decomposition $A=A_1\oplus A_{1/2}\oplus A_0$ with $A_\lambda=\{x:\Pi x=\lambda x\}$, and the decomposition is the source of the minimal ideals and the matrix units. The construction uses associativity twice, in reading the two maps as projections and in verifying that their images add.

For the general quaternionic product there is nothing to attach it to. The only nonzero idempotent is the left unit $e_0$, and $L_{e_0}$ is the identity, so the only Peirce piece is the whole algebra. The nontrivial decomposition, the frame of two orthogonal idempotents and its Peirce lines, is a property of the plain algebra alone, and over $\mathbb{R}$ the plain algebra is a division algebra, whose only idempotents are $0$ and $e_0$ and which carries no nontrivial frame either. What the product has in place of the decomposition is the centrality of the square and the trivial projectors of §The Idempotents as Projections.

The Idempotents and the Nilpotents

The two classes the idempotent equation and the square-zero equation select are disjoint.

Theorem (disjoint classes). No element of the block is both an idempotent and a nilpotent; the only idempotent whose square vanishes is the origin.

Proof. The idempotents of $\star$ are $0$ and $e_0$, and the nilpotents are the nonzero square-zero elements; the square-zero set of the block is the origin alone (Nilpotents of the General Quaternionic Algebra, below this article in this category), so no nonzero idempotent is square-zero and the nilpotent class is empty. Of the two idempotents, $e_0\star e_0=e_0\neq0$ and $0\star0=0$, so the origin is the only idempotent whose square vanishes. The two classes meet in no element.

Summary

In the general quaternionic bilinear product the square of every element is the central element $N(\tilde p)e_0$, so the idempotent equation forces centrality and reduces to $A^2=A$ on the scalar line. The idempotents of the product are $0$ and $e_0$ alone, and the product has no nontrivial idempotent; the two form a family of orthogonal idempotents whose only complete nonzero subfamily is $\{e_0\}$. The complex chapter's two-parameter family of Hermitian idempotents has no real counterpart: there is no central imaginary unit to build it and no isotropic cone to carry it, and the idempotents and the roots of $+e_0$, the three-dimensional sphere $\mathrm{Sp}(1)$, do not correspond, the affine map $\tilde\Pi\mapsto2\tilde\Pi-e_0$ of the complex plain block being unavailable. On the operator side $L_{\tilde p}$ is a projector exactly when $\tilde p$ is a plain idempotent, so over $\mathbb{R}$ the only projectors of the form $L_{\tilde p}$ are the zero operator and the identity. Read on the two remarkable subspaces, the idempotent equation leaves the two trivial idempotents in the centre and only $0$ in the vector subspace, no element is both an idempotent and a nilpotent, and the origin is the only idempotent whose square vanishes.
