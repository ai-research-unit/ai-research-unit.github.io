Zero Divisors of the General Quaternionic Algebra

Introduction

This article studies the zero divisors of the quaternion algebra $\mathbb{H}$ read through the general quaternionic bilinear product, and it is the real member of the general quaternionic family whose complex sibling is Zero Divisors of the General Quaternionic Algebra.

A divisor is relative to a product, and the definition is stated once in Definitions for the Study of the 6 Algebraic Structures, §The Element Vocabulary; the unqualified "zero divisor" means the zero divisor of the general quaternionic product throughout, and every other product is named. The article establishes four things. First, the criterion: a nonzero $\tilde p$ is a zero divisor of $\star$ exactly when its algebraic norm vanishes, $N(\tilde p)=0$, the criterion of the complex counterpart read letter for letter. Second, the criterion is never met over $\mathbb{R}$: the quaternionic norm is a sum of four squares, so it is positive definite and vanishes at $\tilde p=0$ alone, and the zero-divisor set is empty. Third, the solution set of the norm equation, which is the zero-divisor cone of real dimension six over $\mathbb{C}$, degenerates to its apex $\{0\}$ over $\mathbb{R}$; the striking object of the complex block is a single point here. Fourth, every nonzero element is regular, with the single two-sided inverse $\zeta(\tilde p)=\tilde p/N(\tilde p)$ against the left unit, so the two annihilators of an element are $\{0\}$ at every nonzero element and the whole space at the origin.

The contrast with the complex sibling organises the article. There the norm is isotropic, the zero-divisor set is the punctured cone $\{N=0\}$ of real dimension six, and the two families of divisors, pure and non-pure, fill it; here the norm is definite, the cone shrinks to its apex, and both families are empty. The difference is not a difference of the product, which is the same rule read over the two fields, but of the base field: over $\mathbb{R}$ a sum of squares has no nontrivial zero.

Boundaries. The norm, its multiplicativity and the two one-sided inverse equations are Norm and Invertibility in the General Quaternionic Algebra; the element vocabulary is Definitions for the Study of the 6 Algebraic Structures; the annihilators and their coincidence are Annihilators of the General Quaternionic Algebra; the definiteness of the form and the emptiness of the isotropic structure are The Isotropic Structure of the General Quaternionic Algebra; the nilpotents of the product are Nilpotents of the General Quaternionic Algebra and the idempotents Idempotents of the General Quaternionic Algebra, both below this article in this category; the structure-level comparison of the six zero-divisor sets is Zero Divisors in the 6 Algebraic Structures over the Quaternion $\mathbb{R}$ Space; and the complex sibling, which carries the cone and the two families, is Zero Divisors of the General Quaternionic Algebra.

Conventions

$\mathbb{H}$ is the real quaternion algebra with basis $e_0=1,e_1,e_2,e_3$, the rules $e_k^2=-e_0$ and $e_1e_2=e_3$. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the coefficientwise conjugation is the identity and the Hermitian star coincides with the natural conjugation, so the block carries the single involution

$$ \tilde p^{\natural}=p_0-\mathbf p,\qquad \tilde p=p_0e_0+\mathbf p,\quad \mathbf p=p_1e_1+p_2e_2+p_3e_3 . $$

Juxtaposition is the plain product $\tilde p\tilde q$ of the general plain algebra of the same space and $\star$ is the general quaternionic bilinear product

$$ \tilde p\star\tilde q=\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde q , $$

the plain product with the first factor read through the conjugation. The quaternionic bilinear algebraic norm is

$$ N(\tilde p)=\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde p=p_0^2+|\mathbf p|^2=\sum_{\mu=0}^{3}p_\mu^2 , $$

and $\beta(\tilde p,\tilde q)=p_0q_0+\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf q$ is its polar form, the quaternionic bilinear form of the space. An element is pure when $p_0=0$ and non-pure when $p_0\neq0$; the centre is the scalar line $\mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{H}}=\mathbb{R}e_0$ and the vector subspace is $\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})=\{\mathbf p\}$.

Definition

The word names a product, so the definition is stated for a general product and then read on $\star$.

Definition. Let $\star$ be a product on $\mathbb{H}$ and let $\tilde p\in\mathbb{H}$. The element $\tilde p$ is a left zero divisor of $\star$ when $\tilde p\neq0$ and there is a nonzero $\tilde q$ with $\tilde p\star\tilde q=0$; a right zero divisor of $\star$ when $\tilde p\neq0$ and there is a nonzero $\tilde q$ with $\tilde q\star\tilde p=0$; and a zero divisor of $\star$ when it is one or the other. It is a two-sided zero divisor when it is both, and regular when it is neither. The definition is Definitions for the Study of the 6 Algebraic Structures, §The Element Vocabulary; the origin is not a zero divisor, both factors being required to be nonzero.

Throughout this article the product is the general quaternionic bilinear product $\tilde p\star\tilde q=\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde q$. On it the two defining equations are

$$ \tilde p^{\natural}\tilde q=0 \quad\text{(left)},\qquad \tilde q^{\natural}\tilde p=0 \quad\text{(right)} , $$

which are plain-product equations in the conjugated first factor.

The Criterion

Theorem (the criterion). For a nonzero $\tilde p\in\mathbb{H}$, $\tilde p$ is a zero divisor of $\star$ if and only if

$$ N(\tilde p)=\sum_{\mu=0}^{3}p_\mu^2=0 . $$

Proof. Suppose that $\tilde p$ is a zero divisor. If it is a left one, there is a nonzero $\tilde q$ with $\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde q=0$; taking norms and using the multiplicativity of the norm,

$$ 0=N(\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde q)=N(\tilde p^{\natural})N(\tilde q)=N(\tilde p)N(\tilde q) , $$

so $N(\tilde p)=0$ or $N(\tilde q)=0$. The factor $N(\tilde q)$ is positive, $\tilde q$ being nonzero and the norm positive definite, so $N(\tilde p)=0$. The mirror computation with the factors exchanged treats the right equation, $\tilde q^{\natural}\tilde p=0$. Conversely, if $N(\tilde p)=0$ then

$$ \tilde p\star\tilde p=\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde p=N(\tilde p)e_0=0 , $$

so $\tilde q=\tilde p$ is a nonzero witness for the left equation and $\tilde p$ is a left zero divisor; the mirror computation gives the right statement.

The criterion is the criterion of the complex sibling, and it is the norm equation read on the product whose square is the norm. The two products that share the norm are distinguished by the object that turns the equation into a statement: in the plain algebra it is the plain inverse, here it is the conjugated one-sided solution of §Every Nonzero Element Is Regular.

Corollary (the criterion is never met). Over $\mathbb{R}$ the sum of the four squares $N(\tilde p)$ vanishes only at $\tilde p=0$, so a nonzero element never satisfies the criterion. The general quaternionic product has no zero divisor, and its zero-divisor set is the empty set $\varnothing$.

Proof. A sum of squares of real numbers vanishes exactly when every term vanishes, that is when $p_0=p_1=p_2=p_3=0$. The criterion asks of a nonzero element that its norm vanish, and no such element exists. The same statement is the row of the general quaternionic product in Zero Divisors, where the zero-divisor set of the structure is the empty set.

The Zero Set of the Norm

Definition. The zero set of the norm of the quaternionic product is

$$ C=\{\tilde p\in\mathbb{H}:N(\tilde p)=0\}=\{\tilde p:\tilde p\star\tilde p=0\} . $$

Proposition (the zero set is the origin). Over $\mathbb{R}$ the set $C$ is the single point $\{0\}$, of dimension zero, and it is at once the trivial real cone and the trivial real subspace.

Proof. $N$ is positive definite, so $N(\tilde p)=0$ is $\tilde p=0$; the origin is a cone and a subspace of dimension zero, and it is the only one.

Remark (the contrast with the complex block). Over $\mathbb{C}$ the very same equation $N=0$, with $N$ now complex valued, is two independent real equations on the eight real coordinates, and its solution set is the zero-divisor cone of real dimension six that the complex sibling describes. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the equation is a single sum of four real squares, whose only solution is the origin; the cone of the complex block shrinks to its apex, and the punctured set that carries the complex zero divisors becomes empty. The collapse is the definiteness of the quaternionic form (The Isotropic Structure of the General Quaternionic Algebra), and it is the same fact that makes $\mathbb{H}$ a division algebra for the plain product.

The Two Families, Empty

The complex sibling splits its zero divisors into a pure family and a non-pure family: the pure family is the isotropic cone of the vector subspace, of real dimension four, and the non-pure family is the set of complex multiples of the nontrivial idempotents of the plain product. Over $\mathbb{R}$ both families are empty, for the definiteness of the norm in the first case and the division property of the plain algebra in the second.

Proposition (the pure family is absent). On the vector subspace $\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})$ the norm is $N(\mathbf p)=|\mathbf p|^2$, positive definite, so the isotropic equation $(\mathbf p,\mathbf p)=0$ has the single solution $\mathbf p=0$ and the pure family of zero divisors is empty.

Proof. A sum of three real squares vanishes only at the origin; the criterion then excludes every nonzero pure element.

Proposition (the non-pure family is absent). The non-pure zero divisors of the complex block are the nonzero multiples of the nontrivial idempotents of the plain product. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the plain product has no nontrivial idempotent: its idempotents are $0$ and $e_0$ alone, so the non-pure family is empty.

Proof. The plain algebra is a division algebra. If $\tilde p^2=\tilde p$ and $\tilde p\neq0$, left multiplication by the plain inverse $\tilde p^{-1}$ gives $\tilde p=e_0$. The Hermitian family $\tfrac12(e_0+i\hat\mu)$ that carries the complex family does not exist over $\mathbb{R}$, there being no central imaginary unit; the idempotents of the block and the contrast with the complex counterpart are Idempotents of the General Quaternionic Algebra, below this article in this category.

Every Nonzero Element Is Regular

Theorem (the two one-sided equations). Let $\tilde p\in\mathbb{H}$ with $N(\tilde p)\neq0$, and put $\zeta(\tilde p)=\tilde p/N(\tilde p)$. Then $\zeta(\tilde p)$ is the unique solution of each of the equations $\tilde p\star\tilde x=e_0$ and $\tilde y\star\tilde p=e_0$, and

$$ \tilde p\star\zeta(\tilde p)=e_0=\zeta(\tilde p)\star\tilde p . $$

The statement is Norm and Invertibility in the General Quaternionic Algebra, §The One-Sided Inverse Equations.

The remark is the one the block demands, and it is stated in the block's own terms. The algebra has the left unit $e_0$ and no right one, so the two-sided inverse of the unital theory has no referent; what the two equations compute is an element that inverts $\tilde p$ against the one-sided unit. Their content is that the left and the right such elements are not merely both present, they are the same, and that they exist exactly when the norm does not vanish.

Corollary (regularity). Every nonzero element of $\mathbb{H}$ is regular, with the single two-sided inverse $\zeta(\tilde p)$ against the left unit $e_0$. The criterion of §The Criterion is the statement that an element with a one-sided inverse is not a zero divisor.

Proof. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the criterion $N(\tilde p)\neq0$ is $\tilde p\neq0$, so the theorem applies to every nonzero element. The last sentence is the contrapositive of the criterion, read with the one-sided equations in place of the plain inverse.

Remark (the invertibility is that of the parent algebra). The invertible elements of the block are therefore all the nonzero elements, the same set as the unit group of the parent plain algebra; the left multiplication $L_{\tilde p}$ by such an element is the plain left multiplication by the nonzero element $\tilde p^{\natural}$ and is a bijection of $\mathbb{H}$, so the unit group acts on the elements of the block by the left multiplications. The block adds no inverse of its own: its single inverse $\zeta(\tilde p)=\tilde p/N(\tilde p)$ is the plain inverse of $\tilde p^{\natural}$, and the criterion it inverts is the criterion of the parent norm.

The Block and the Division Property

The general quaternionic product and the general plain product are the two division structures of the space, the two with an empty zero-divisor set (Zero Divisors in the 6 Algebraic Structures over the Quaternion $\mathbb{R}$ Space, §The General Plain Algebra (GPA) and §The General Quaternionic Algebra (GQA)); they are the only ones of the six whose product leaves no zero divisor, the symmetric structures having the pure cone and the punctured space and the two antisymmetric structures having the whole punctured space.

Remark (the unit is one-sided). In the strict sense of Definitions for the Study of the 6 Algebraic Structures, §The Element Vocabulary, a division rule asks beside the absence of zero divisors for a two-sided unit, and only the general plain product of the six has one; the general quaternionic product has the left unit $e_0$ alone. Over the complex block the product fails the division property for the stronger reason that it contains zero divisors; over the real block the zero divisors are absent and the only defect is the missing right unit (The Specificities of the General Quaternionic Algebra). The two defects are independent, and this article exhibits the second alone.

The Annihilators

The annihilator of an element is the kernel of the left or the right multiplication, and it is the finer object than the zero divisor: it names the partners. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the two annihilators coincide, and the answer is the smallest possible away from the origin.

Theorem (the common annihilator). For every $\tilde p\in\mathbb{H}$,

$$ A_\star(\tilde p)=\{\tilde x:\tilde p\star\tilde x=0\},\qquad B_\star(\tilde p)=\{\tilde x:\tilde x\star\tilde p=0\} , $$

coincide, and

$$ A_\star(\tilde p)=B_\star(\tilde p)=\{0\}\ \text{for}\ \tilde p\neq0 ,\qquad A_\star(\tilde p)=B_\star(\tilde p)=\mathbb{H}\ \text{for}\ \tilde p=0 . $$

The statement and its proof are Annihilators of the General Quaternionic Algebra; the coincidence is the anti-automorphism property of ${}^{\natural}$, the same reason as over $\mathbb{C}$, and over $\mathbb{R}$ it is vacuous, both sides being $\{0\}$ at every nonzero element. The operator behind the annihilator is the left multiplication $L_{\tilde p}\tilde x=\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde x$, of determinant $\det L_{\tilde p}=N(\tilde p)^2$, nonzero at every nonzero element.

The Zero Divisors and the Remarkable Subspaces

The element theory of the block is read on the two remarkable real subspaces of $\mathbb{H}$, the scalar line $\mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{H}}=\mathbb{R}e_0$, which is the centre, and the vector subspace $\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})$ (Introduction to the Remarkable Subspaces of the Quaternions). Neither carries a zero divisor.

remarkable subspace element norm zero divisors
$\mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{H}}$, the centre $se_0$ $s^2$ none
$\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})$, the vector subspace $\mathbf p$ $\lvert\mathbf p\rvert^2$ none

Proposition. On the centre and on the vector subspace the norm of a nonzero element is strictly positive, so neither subspace contains an isotropic direction and neither contains a zero divisor; in each the origin alone is of norm zero.

Proof. $N(se_0)=s^2$ vanishes only at $s=0$ and $N(\mathbf p)=|\mathbf p|^2$ only at $\mathbf p=0$, and a nonzero central element is a unit, $\zeta(se_0)=e_0/s$; the criterion then excludes every nonzero element of either subspace. The definiteness of the form on the two subspaces is The Isotropic Structure of the General Quaternionic Algebra.

The Zero Divisors and the Other Structures of the Space

The comparison of the six zero-divisor sets is Zero Divisors in the 6 Algebraic Structures over the Quaternion $\mathbb{R}$ Space, and its verdict for the block is the empty set; the article states the contrast in one theorem and does not reproduce the other rows.

Theorem. Among the six structures of the quaternion real space the zero-divisor set is empty for the general plain algebra and for the general quaternionic algebra, is the pure cone $\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})\setminus\{0\}$ for the symmetric plain algebra, is the punctured space $\mathbb{H}\setminus\{0\}$ for the symmetric quaternionic algebra, and is the punctured space for the two antisymmetric structures.

Proof. The two general structures are the theorem of Zero Divisors in the 6 Algebraic Structures over the Quaternion $\mathbb{R}$ Space, §The Two General Products Coincide; the symmetric rows are its §The Symmetric Plain Algebra (SPA) and §The Symmetric Quaternionic Algebra (SQA), where the Jordan product kills a pair of orthogonal pure vectors and the scalar-valued product kills the orthogonal hyperplane of every nonzero element; the antisymmetric rows are its §The Antisymmetric Plain Algebra (APA) and §The Antisymmetric Quaternionic Algebra (AQA), the products being alternating. The two division structures are the two bilinear general products of the space.

Remark. The complex block carries four general products, and their zero-divisor sets coincide at the punctured cone; the real block carries two bilinear general products, and their zero-divisor sets coincide at the empty set. The coincidence is the same in form and opposite in content, and in both cases the product is not what selects the set: the norm is.

Summary

For the general quaternionic bilinear product $\tilde p\star\tilde q=\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde q$ over $\mathbb{R}$, a nonzero element is a zero divisor exactly when its algebraic norm vanishes, $N(\tilde p)=\sum_\mu p_\mu^2=0$. The criterion is the criterion of the complex sibling, but over $\mathbb{R}$ the norm is a sum of four squares, hence positive definite, so the criterion is never met: the zero-divisor set is the empty set, and the zero-divisor cone of the complex block, of real dimension six, shrinks to its apex $\{0\}$. The two families of the complex block, the pure family of the isotropic vector subspace and the non-pure family of the multiples of the nontrivial plain idempotents, are both empty, for the norm is definite on the vector subspace and the plain algebra is a division algebra with the idempotents $0$ and $e_0$ alone. Every nonzero element is regular, with the single two-sided inverse $\zeta(\tilde p)=\tilde p/N(\tilde p)$ against the left unit $e_0$; the two annihilators of an element coincide and are $\{0\}$ at every nonzero element and the whole space at the origin; and neither remarkable subspace, the centre nor the vector subspace, carries a zero divisor. The general quaternionic product is one of the two division structures of the space, the other being the general plain product, and it differs from it in the unit, which is one-sided here and two-sided there.
