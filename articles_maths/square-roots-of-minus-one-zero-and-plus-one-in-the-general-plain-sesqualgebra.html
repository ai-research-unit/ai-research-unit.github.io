Square Roots of Minus One, Zero and Plus One in the General Plain Sesqualgebra

Introduction

The general plain sesqualgebra is the operation $\tilde P\star\tilde\Xi=\tilde P\tilde\Xi^{*}$ on the biquaternion space (Introduction to the General Plain Sesqualgebra of Biquaternions). This article determines the square roots of the three central values $-1$, $0$ and $+1$ in the block, that is, the elements $\tilde\Xi$ satisfying

$$ \tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=-e_0,\qquad \tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=0,\qquad \tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=e_0 . $$

A root names a product, and the definition is relative; it is stated once for a general product in Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures, §Powers, the Square and the Roots, and is read here on the block. The block's square is the Hermitian square $\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=\tilde\Xi\tilde\Xi^{*}$, whose scalar part is the definite quantity $\sum_\mu\lvert \Xi_\mu\rvert^{2}$ (Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Sesqualgebra). One consequence decides the whole article and it is a positivity: the scalar part of the square is never negative, so the value $-1$ is unattainable and the value $0$ is attained only at the origin. The block therefore carries no root of $-1$ and no root of $0$, and it carries the roots of $+1$, which are the element cost of that positivity. The relation to the idempotents is not the parent's bijection with the roots of $-1$ — the block has none — but a bijection with the Hermitian roots of $+1$.

Placement. The article is the fourth entry of the Common Theory section of the block, after the zero divisors and before the square roots of a general element. It uses the product and the class of the introduction, the algebraic norm and the scalar part of the square from Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Sesqualgebra, and the zero-divisor set of Zero Divisors of the General Plain Sesqualgebra; it feeds the idempotent article of the block, Idempotents of the General Plain Sesqualgebra, and the nilpotent article, Nilpotents of the General Plain Sesqualgebra.

Boundaries. The product, its class and its placement are Introduction to the General Plain Sesqualgebra of Biquaternions; the relative definition of the root and of the square root, and the designation of the three central values, are Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures. The three sets of the parent are Square Roots of Minus One, Zero and Plus One in the General Plain Algebra, and the general reduction is Square Roots of a General Element in the General Plain Algebra; the general reduction of the block is Square Roots of a General Element in the General Plain Sesqualgebra. The idempotents and the nilpotents of the parent are Idempotents of the General Plain Algebra and Nilpotents of the General Plain Algebra, and the roots read on the idempotents are Idempotents of the General Plain Sesqualgebra and Idempotents of the General Plain Algebra, §The Relation to the Idempotents. Nothing topological and nothing metric appears.

Conventions. $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$ has basis $e_0,e_1,e_2,e_3$ with $e_1e_2=e_3$ and central scalar imaginary $i$. A general element is $\tilde\Xi=\sum_{\mu=0}^{3}\Xi_\mu e_\mu$ with $\Xi_\mu\in\mathbb{C}$; the natural conjugation is $\tilde\Xi^{\natural}=\Xi_0-\mathbf\Xi$, the coefficientwise conjugation is $\overline{\tilde\Xi}=\overline{\Xi_0}e_0+\overline{\mathbf\Xi}$, and the Hermitian conjugation is $\tilde\Xi^{*}=\overline{\tilde\Xi^{\natural}}=\overline{\Xi_0}-\overline{\mathbf\Xi}$. The product is the block $\tilde P\star\tilde\Xi=\tilde P\tilde\Xi^{*}$. A root of $\lambda$ is a nonzero element with $\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=\lambda e_0$; the three central values are $-1$, $+1$ and $0$, the last being the nonzero square-zero elements. Throughout, an element is written in the halves $\tilde\xi,\tilde\xi'$ of the imaginary decomposition of the algebra, $\tilde\Xi=\tilde\xi+i\tilde\xi'$ with $\tilde\xi,\tilde\xi'$ real quaternions, and $\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}'$ are the vector parts of $\tilde\xi,\tilde\xi'$. The central square is $c(\tilde\Xi)=\tilde\Xi\tilde\Xi^{\natural}=\sum_\mu \Xi_\mu^{2}$, and the algebraic norm of the block is $H(\tilde\Xi)=\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi)=\sum_\mu\lvert \Xi_\mu\rvert^{2}$.

The Reduction

Theorem (the Hermitian square and its scalar part). For $\tilde\Xi=\tilde\xi+i\tilde\xi'$ with $\tilde\xi,\tilde\xi'$ real quaternions,

$$ \tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=\tilde\Xi\tilde\Xi^{*}=\bigl(\lvert \tilde\xi\rvert^{2}+\lvert \tilde\xi'\rvert^{2}\bigr)e_0+2i\Bigl(\xi_0\boldsymbol{\xi}'-\xi'_0\boldsymbol{\xi}+\boldsymbol{\xi}\times\boldsymbol{\xi}'\Bigr), $$

where $\xi_0,\xi'_0$ are the real scalar parts of $\tilde\xi,\tilde\xi'$ and $\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}'$ their vector parts. The square is Hermitian, its scalar part is

$$ \mathrm{Sc}\bigl(\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi\bigr)=\lvert \tilde\xi\rvert^{2}+\lvert \tilde\xi'\rvert^{2}=\sum_{\mu=0}^{3}\lvert \Xi_\mu\rvert^{2}\geq0, $$

a real non-negative number vanishing only at $\tilde\Xi=0$.

Proof. The involution is $(\ )^{*}=\overline{\ }\,\circ\,{}^{\natural}$, and $\tilde\xi,\tilde\xi'$ are real, so $\tilde\Xi^{*}=\tilde\xi^{\natural}-i\tilde\xi'^{\natural}$. Expanding $\tilde\Xi\tilde\Xi^{*}=(\tilde\xi+i\tilde\xi')(\tilde\xi^{\natural}-i\tilde\xi'^{\natural})$ and using $\tilde\xi\tilde\xi^{\natural}=\lvert \tilde\xi\rvert^{2}$, $\tilde\xi'\tilde\xi'^{\natural}=\lvert \tilde\xi'\rvert^{2}$, and the product rule of the real quaternions for $\tilde\xi'\tilde\xi^{\natural}-\tilde\xi\tilde\xi'^{\natural}$ — whose scalar part vanishes and whose vector part is $-2\xi'_0\boldsymbol{\xi}+2\xi_0\boldsymbol{\xi}'+2\boldsymbol{\xi}\times\boldsymbol{\xi}'$, giving the display after a sign change — yields the formula. The Hermitianness is $(\tilde\Xi\tilde\Xi^{*})^{*}=\tilde\Xi\tilde\Xi^{*}$, and the scalar part is read off the display.

Corollary (the scalar trichotomy). The equation $\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=\lambda e_0$ requires, for a complex scalar $\lambda$,

$$ \sum_{\mu=0}^{3}\lvert \Xi_\mu\rvert^{2}=\lambda\qquad\text{and}\qquad \xi_0\boldsymbol{\xi}'-\xi'_0\boldsymbol{\xi}+\boldsymbol{\xi}\times\boldsymbol{\xi}'=0 . $$

Consequently a central value $\lambda$ has a root only when $\lambda$ is a non-negative real number, and the three values separate: no root of $-1$, no root of $0$, roots of $+1$.

Proof. The two sides are equal exactly when the scalar and the vector parts are equal, and the scalar part of $\lambda e_0$ is $\lambda$. The scalar equation forces $\lambda=\sum_\mu\lvert \Xi_\mu\rvert^{2}$, a real number $\geq0$, so $\lambda=-1$ is excluded and $\lambda=0$ forces $\tilde\Xi=0$, which the definition excludes.

The Roots of Minus One

Theorem (no root of $-1$). There is no element $\tilde\Xi$ with $\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=-e_0$.

Proof. The scalar part of the square is $\sum_\mu\lvert \Xi_\mu\rvert^{2}\geq0$ by the reduction, while the scalar part of $-e_0$ is $-1$; no element has a negative sum of modulus squares.

Remark (the rootlessness is the sign of the form). The parent algebra has roots of $-1$ for every splitting, and they are in bijection with its idempotents (Square Roots of Minus One, Zero and Plus One in the General Plain Algebra); the block has none, because its scalar form is definite. The bijection of the parent is therefore not available here, and the block's idempotent theory is read on the Hermitian roots of $+1$ instead, in §The Relation to the Idempotents.

The Roots of Zero

Theorem (no root of $0$). The equation $\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=0$ has the single solution $\tilde\Xi=0$, so the block has no nonzero square-zero element and the root set of $0$ is empty.

Proof. The scalar part of the square is $\sum_\mu\lvert \Xi_\mu\rvert^{2}$ by the reduction, and it vanishes only when every coordinate vanishes; a root is required to be nonzero, so the set is empty.

Remark (square-zero and nilpotent are not the same here). The block has no square-zero element, so it has no nilpotent of index two; its nilpotents have index three, and they are exactly the pure zero divisors, the elements with $\Xi_0=0$ and $(\mathbf\Xi,\mathbf\Xi)=0$ (Nilpotents of the General Plain Sesqualgebra). The empty root set of $0$ is therefore not the empty nilpotent set: the root of $0$ is the square-zero element, and the block has none, while the block has plenty of nilpotents at the next index. The distinction between "square-zero" and "nilpotent" is thus live in the block and not a coincidence of definitions.

Remark (the pure zero divisors are not square-zero). The parent's pure zero divisors, $\Xi_0=0$ with $(\mathbf\Xi,\mathbf\Xi)=0$, are its nonzero nilpotents of index two; the block's square does not vanish on them, $\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=H(\tilde\Xi)e_0+\mathrm{Vect}(\tilde\Xi\tilde\Xi^{*})\neq0$, as Zero Divisors of the General Plain Sesqualgebra records, and the block's nilpotency index is raised to three. The root set of $0$ is empty although the zero-divisor set is not, and although the nilpotent set is not: the three classes of the block are distinct and they must not be conflated.

The Roots of Plus One

Theorem (the roots of $+1$). The roots of $+1$ are exactly the elements

$$ \tilde\Xi=e^{i\theta}w,\qquad \theta\in\mathbb{R},\ w\in\mathbb{H}\ \text{real with}\ \lvert w\rvert=1, $$

that is, the product of the unit circle of the central line with the unit sphere of the real quaternion subalgebra; the root set is $\{e^{i\theta}w\}=U(1)\cdot S^{3}$, of real dimension four, and it contains $e_0$, $-e_0$ and the pure imaginary unit vectors $i\hat\mu$ over the real unit vectors $\hat\mu$.

Proof. By the reduction the square is $e_0$ exactly when $\lvert \tilde\xi\rvert^{2}+\lvert \tilde\xi'\rvert^{2}=1$ and $\xi_0\boldsymbol{\xi}'-\xi'_0\boldsymbol{\xi}+\boldsymbol{\xi}\times\boldsymbol{\xi}'=0$. Dot the vector equation with $\boldsymbol{\xi}\times\boldsymbol{\xi}'$: the first two terms are orthogonal to $\boldsymbol{\xi}\times\boldsymbol{\xi}'$ and the third is $\lvert\boldsymbol{\xi}\times\boldsymbol{\xi}'\rvert^{2}$, so $\boldsymbol{\xi}\times\boldsymbol{\xi}'=0$; hence $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ and $\boldsymbol{\xi}'$ are parallel, and the vector equation reads $\xi_0\boldsymbol{\xi}'=\xi'_0\boldsymbol{\xi}$. If $\boldsymbol{\xi}\neq0$ then $\boldsymbol{\xi}'=\lambda\boldsymbol{\xi}$ for a real $\lambda$, the equation gives $\xi'_0=\lambda \xi_0$ and therefore $\tilde\xi'=\xi'_0+\boldsymbol{\xi}'=\lambda(\xi_0+\boldsymbol{\xi})=\lambda \tilde\xi$: the halves are parallel as full quaternions, $\tilde\Xi=\tilde\xi+i\tilde\xi'=(1+i\lambda)\tilde\xi$, and writing $1+i\lambda=\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\,e^{i\theta}$ and $w=\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\,\tilde\xi$, a real quaternion since $\tilde\xi$ is one, gives $\tilde\Xi=e^{i\theta}w$ with $\lvert w\rvert=1$ from the scalar equation. If $\boldsymbol{\xi}=0$ then $\tilde\xi=\xi_0$ is a real scalar and the equation reads $\xi_0\boldsymbol{\xi}'=0$: either $\boldsymbol{\xi}'=0$, and $\tilde\Xi=(\xi_0+i\xi'_0)e_0=e^{i\theta}e_0$ with $\xi_0^{2}+\xi'^{2}_0=1$ is already of the required form with $w=e_0$, or $\xi_0=0$, and $\tilde\Xi=i\tilde\xi'=i(\xi'_0+\boldsymbol{\xi}')$ is of the required form with $\theta=\pi/2$ and the real unit $w=\xi'_0+\boldsymbol{\xi}'$. In both cases $\tilde\Xi=e^{i\theta}w$ with $w$ a real unit quaternion. Conversely every $e^{i\theta}w$ with $w$ real unit has $\tilde\xi=\cos\theta\,w$, $\tilde\xi'=\sin\theta\,w$, hence $\lvert \tilde\xi\rvert^{2}+\lvert \tilde\xi'\rvert^{2}=1$ and $\xi_0\boldsymbol{\xi}'-\xi'_0\boldsymbol{\xi}+\boldsymbol{\xi}\times\boldsymbol{\xi}'=\cos\theta\sin\theta(w_0\mathbf w-\mathbf w_0 w)=0$, so it is a root. The parametrisation $(\theta,w)\mapsto e^{i\theta}w$ has kernel $\{(0,e_0),(\pi,-e_0)\}$, of order two, so the root set is a single real four-dimensional family, and the named elements are the cases $\theta=0$, $\theta=\pi$ and $\theta=\pi/2$ with $w=\hat\mu$.

Corollary (the roots of $+1$ are never zero divisors). Every root of $+1$ has $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi)=1$, hence $H(\tilde\Xi)=1$, and its central square lies on the unit circle of the centre:

$$ c(e^{i\theta}w)=e^{2i\theta},\qquad\text{in particular}\qquad c(\pm e_0)=1,\qquad c(i\hat\mu)=-1 . $$

Every root of $+1$ is therefore one-sidedly invertible, and the root set of $+1$ is disjoint from the zero-divisor cone.

Proof. The normalisation is the scalar equation $\mathrm{Sc}=1$ and the algebraic norm $H$ of the reduction. For the central square, $c(\tilde\Xi)=\sum_\mu \Xi_\mu^{2}$ with $\Xi_\mu=e^{i\theta}w_\mu$ and $w_\mu$ real, so $c(\tilde\Xi)=e^{2i\theta}\sum_\mu w_\mu^{2}=e^{2i\theta}$, of modulus one and never zero; the named values are the cases $\theta=0,\pi$ and $\theta=\pi/2$. The zero-divisor criterion is $c=0$ (Zero Divisors of the General Plain Sesqualgebra), so the root set misses the cone; the one-sided invertibility of the roots is Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Sesqualgebra.

Remark (roots and zero divisors are separated). The root set of $+1$ and the zero-divisor cone are the two subsets of the block the scalar part of the square controls, and they are disjoint: the roots have $H(\tilde\Xi)=1$ and $c$ on the unit circle, the zero divisors have $c=0$. The separation is a fact of the block's definite square, and it is read on the central square as $c(e^{i\theta}w)=e^{2i\theta}\neq0$.

Remark (the dimension and the shape). The parent's root set of $-1$ is two isolated elements together with a two-parameter family and a four-parameter family, and its root set of $+1$ is the bijective image of that set; the block has its roots of $+1$ in a single family $U(1)\cdot S^{3}$, of real dimension four, and no roots of the other two values. The block's set is the single orbit $U(1)\cdot S^{3}$ and the parent's is not, and the parent's two isolated roots $\pm i$ have no counterpart in the block.

The Relation to the Idempotents

Definition (the Hermitian roots of $+1$). A root of $+1$ is Hermitian when $\tilde\Xi^{*}=\tilde\Xi$. The Hermitian roots of $+1$ are $\pm e_0$ together with the pure imaginary unit vectors $i\hat\mu$ over the real unit vectors $\hat\mu$.

Proof. A Hermitian element is $\xi_0 e_0+i\boldsymbol{\xi}'$ with $\xi_0\in\mathbb{R}$ and $\boldsymbol{\xi}'$ a real vector, and its square is $\xi_0^{2}e_0-\lvert\boldsymbol{\xi}'\rvert^{2}e_0+2\xi_0 i\boldsymbol{\xi}'$; the equation is $(\xi_0^{2}+\lvert\boldsymbol{\xi}'\rvert^{2})e_0+2\xi_0 i\boldsymbol{\xi}'=e_0$, whose solution is $\xi_0=\pm1$ with $\boldsymbol{\xi}'=0$ or $\xi_0=0$ with $\lvert\boldsymbol{\xi}'\rvert=1$. The last is $i\hat\mu$.

Theorem (the idempotents of the block are the halved Hermitian roots of $+1$). The map $\tilde\Xi\mapsto\tfrac12(e_0+\tilde\Xi)$ is a bijection from the Hermitian roots of $+1$ onto the idempotents of the block,

$$ 0=\tfrac12(e_0-e_0),\qquad e_0=\tfrac12(e_0+e_0),\qquad \tfrac12(e_0+i\hat\mu),\ \ \hat\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\ (\hat\mu,\hat\mu)=1 . $$

Proof. A root $\tilde\Xi$ of $+1$ that is Hermitian satisfies $\tilde\Xi^{*}=\tilde\Xi$ and $\tilde\Xi^{2}=\tilde\Xi\tilde\Xi^{*}=e_0$, so $\tfrac12(e_0+\tilde\Xi)$ is Hermitian and has square $\tfrac14(e_0+2\tilde\Xi+\tilde\Xi^{2})=\tfrac14(2e_0+2\tilde\Xi)=\tfrac12(e_0+\tilde\Xi)$: it is a Hermitian idempotent of the algebra, hence an idempotent of the block by Idempotents of the General Plain Sesqualgebra. Conversely an idempotent of the block is a Hermitian idempotent $\tilde\Pi$ of the algebra, and $\tilde\Xi=2\tilde\Pi-e_0$ is Hermitian with square $(2\tilde\Pi-e_0)^{2}=4\tilde\Pi-4\tilde\Pi+e_0=e_0$, a Hermitian root of $+1$. The two maps are inverse, and the list is the classification of §The Roots of Plus One.

Remark (the parent's bijection replaced). The parent bijects its idempotents with its roots of $-1$, $\tilde\Pi(\xi)=\tfrac12(e_0+\xi i)$; the block bijects its idempotents with the Hermitian roots of $+1$, because the block has no root of $-1$. The two statements agree on the elements they share: the Hermitian idempotents $\tfrac12(e_0+i\hat\mu)$ are the parent idempotents with a real root $\xi=\pm\hat\mu$, and they are the block idempotents. The mixed roots of the parent give idempotents that are not Hermitian and not idempotent for the block (Idempotents of the General Plain Sesqualgebra).

The Three Sets Compared

central value $\lambda$ the block the parent
$-1$ no root two isolated elements, a two-parameter family and a four-parameter family
$0$ no root the origin and a four-parameter family, the nilpotent cone
$+1$ $U(1)\cdot S^{3}$, real dimension four the image of the roots of $-1$ under $\tilde P\mapsto\tilde P i$

The block carries exactly one of the three classes, and its class is the single orbit $U(1)\cdot S^{3}$, of real dimension four; the parent carries all three, and its sets are unbounded and include the two isolated elements $\pm i$ and $\pm1$. The difference is the sign of the diagonal of the square: the block's $\mathrm{Sc}(\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi)=\sum_\mu\lvert \Xi_\mu\rvert^{2}$ is definite, and the negative and the zero values are excluded.

Summary

The square of an element of the general plain sesqualgebra is the Hermitian element $\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=\tilde\Xi\tilde\Xi^{*}=(\lvert \tilde\xi\rvert^{2}+\lvert \tilde\xi'\rvert^{2})e_0+2i(\xi_0\boldsymbol{\xi}'-\xi'_0\boldsymbol{\xi}+\boldsymbol{\xi}\times\boldsymbol{\xi}')$ in the real halves $\tilde\Xi=\tilde\xi+i\tilde\xi'$, whose scalar part is the definite quantity $\sum_\mu\lvert \Xi_\mu\rvert^{2}$. The scalar part decides the three central values: minus one has no root, the scalar part being non-negative; zero has no root, the scalar part vanishing only at the origin and the origin being excluded; and plus one has a root. The root set of $+1$ is exactly the family $e^{i\theta}w$ over the real unit quaternions $w$, that is $U(1)\cdot S^{3}$, a single real four-dimensional family containing $e_0$, $-e_0$ and the pure imaginary unit vectors $i\hat\mu$; it is a single family and not the parent's several families together with the isolated elements $\pm i$. The Hermitian roots of $+1$ are $\pm e_0$ and the $i\hat\mu$, and the map $\tilde\Xi\mapsto\tfrac12(e_0+\tilde\Xi)$ is a bijection from them onto the idempotents of the block, which are $0$, $e_0$ and the $\tfrac12(e_0+i\hat\mu)$; the parent's bijection between idempotents and roots of $-1$ has no analogue, because the block has no root of $-1$.
