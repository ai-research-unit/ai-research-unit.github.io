Nilpotents of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra

Introduction

This article classifies the nilpotents of the block, the symmetric quaternionic sesquilinear product $\tilde P\star\tilde Q=\tfrac12(\tilde P^{\natural}\tilde Q^{*}+\tilde Q^{*}\tilde P^{\natural})$ of Introduction to the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra of Biquaternions. It follows Zero Divisors of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra and reads the first named class of the block; the second, the idempotents, is Idempotents of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra.

The nilpotent is the solution of an equation in the product, and the product is part of the definition: it is stated once for a general product in Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures, §The Nilpotent, and read on the products of the space. The unqualified word means the nilpotent of the block. The block is not associative, so a power needs a bracketing, and the left-nested reading is used throughout; the first statement of the article is that the ambiguity does not matter here, because no element of the block has a vanishing cube without having a vanishing square. So the block has nilpotents of index two only, and the nilpotent of the block is the square-zero element.

The product is Introduction to the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra of Biquaternions; the diagonal $\tilde Q\star\tilde Q$ is The Non-Central Diagonal and the Two Halves of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra; the zero divisors are Zero Divisors of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra; the roots of the central value $0$ are read in Square Roots of Minus One, Zero and Plus One in the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra; the nilpotents of the plain product are Nilpotents of the General Plain Algebra; and the square-zero sets of the four general products are The Square-Zero Elements of the Twelve Structures.

Conventions. $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$ with basis $e_0,e_1,e_2,e_3$, $e_k^2=-e_0$ and $e_1e_2=e_3$; $\tilde Q=\sum_\mu Q_\mu e_\mu$ with $Q_\mu\in\mathbb{C}$ and central scalar imaginary $i$; $\mathbf Q=\sum_kQ_ke_k$; $(\mathbf P,\mathbf Q)=\sum_kP_kQ_k$; ${}^{\natural}$ negates $e_1,e_2,e_3$ and fixes the coefficients, $\bar{\cdot}$ conjugates the coefficients, and ${}^{*}={}^{\natural}\circ\bar{\cdot}$; $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ is the real quaternion subspace. The diagonal is $\tilde Q\star\tilde Q=K(\tilde Q,\tilde Q)e_0-Q_0\overline{\mathbf Q}-\overline{Q_0}\mathbf Q$ with $K(\tilde Q,\tilde Q)=|Q_0|^2-\sum_k|Q_k|^2$, and it is a real quaternion for every $\tilde Q$. Throughout this article the product is the block.

Definition

Definition (nilpotent of a product). Let $\star$ be a product on $\mathbb{B}$ and let $\tilde\Upsilon$ be an element. It is nilpotent of $\star$ when some power vanishes,

$$ \tilde\Upsilon^{\star k}=0 \quad\text{for some } k\geq1 , $$

the power being the left-nested reading $\tilde\Upsilon^{\star k}=(\cdots((\tilde\Upsilon\star\tilde\Upsilon)\star\tilde\Upsilon)\cdots\star\tilde\Upsilon)$ of Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures, §Powers, the Square and the Roots. The least such $k$ is the index of nilpotency; an element of index two, $\tilde\Upsilon\star\tilde\Upsilon=0$ with $\tilde\Upsilon\neq0$, is a square-zero element of $\star$.

This is Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures, §The Nilpotent. Unqualified, the product is the block, and the nilpotent of the block is the solution of $\tilde\Upsilon^{\star k}=0$ for some $k$. The origin is the trivial nilpotent and is excluded from the classification.

The Index Is Two

Theorem (the only index is two). For every element $\tilde P$ of the block,

$$ \tilde P\star(\tilde P\star\tilde P)=0 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad (\tilde P\star\tilde P)\star\tilde P=0 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \tilde P\star\tilde P=0 . $$

Proof. Suppose $\tilde P\star(\tilde P\star\tilde P)=0$ and put $\tilde S=\tilde P\star\tilde P$. The diagonal formula gives $\tilde S=\sigma e_0+\mathbf V$ with $\sigma=K(\tilde P,\tilde P)$ a real number and $\mathbf V=-P_0\overline{\mathbf P}-\overline{P_0}\mathbf P$ a real vector, so $\tilde S$ is a real quaternion: $\tilde S^{\natural}=\tilde S$ and $\overline{\tilde S}=\tilde S$. The equation $\tilde P\star\tilde S=0$ is, in the scalar–vector form with $\tilde S$ real,

$$ P_0\sigma=(\mathbf P,\mathbf V), \qquad P_0\mathbf V+\sigma\mathbf P=\mathbf 0 , $$

where $(\mathbf P,\mathbf V)$ is the complex bilinear dot product of the two vectors.

If $P_0=0$ then the second display is $\sigma\mathbf P=\mathbf 0$; if $\mathbf P=\mathbf 0$ then $\tilde P=0$ and $\tilde S=0$, while if $\mathbf P\neq\mathbf 0$ then $\sigma=0$, but for $P_0=0$ the diagonal is $\tilde S=K(\tilde P,\tilde P)e_0=-\lVert\mathbf P\rVert^2e_0$, whose vector part $\mathbf V$ vanishes and whose scalar $\sigma$ is $-\lVert\mathbf P\rVert^2$, so $\sigma=0$ forces $\tilde P=0$. Hence no solution with $P_0=0$ except $\tilde P=0$.

If $P_0\neq0$, substitute $\mathbf V=-P_0\overline{\mathbf P}-\overline{P_0}\mathbf P$ into the second display: it reads $-P_0^2\overline{\mathbf P}+\bigl(\sigma-|P_0|^2\bigr)\mathbf P=\mathbf 0$, that is $P_0^2\overline{\mathbf P}=\bigl(\sigma-|P_0|^2\bigr)\mathbf P=-\lVert\mathbf P\rVert^2\mathbf P$, the last step because $\sigma=K(\tilde P,\tilde P)=|P_0|^2-\lVert\mathbf P\rVert^2$. If $\mathbf P=\mathbf 0$ then $\tilde P=P_0e_0$ is central, the first display reads $P_0|P_0|^2=0$, and $P_0\neq0$ is a contradiction. If $\mathbf P\neq\mathbf 0$, take the squared modulus of both sides of $P_0^2\overline{\mathbf P}=-\lVert\mathbf P\rVert^2\mathbf P$: it gives $|P_0|^4\lVert\mathbf P\rVert^2=\lVert\mathbf P\rVert^6$, hence $|P_0|^2=\lVert\mathbf P\rVert^2$, so $\sigma=0$ and, from the second display, $P_0\mathbf V=-\sigma\mathbf P=\mathbf 0$, that is $\mathbf V=\mathbf 0$; then $\tilde S=\sigma e_0+\mathbf V=0$. Hence $\tilde S=0$ in every case.

So $\tilde P\star(\tilde P\star\tilde P)=0$ forces $\tilde P\star\tilde P=0$; the converse is immediate. The right-nested statement is conjugate-commutative, $(\tilde P\star\tilde P)\star\tilde P=\overline{\tilde P\star(\tilde P\star\tilde P)}$, so it vanishes exactly when the left-nested product does.

Corollary. Every nilpotent of the block has index two, and the nonzero nilpotents of the block are exactly its square-zero elements.

Proof. By the theorem a vanishing left-nested cube is already a vanishing square, so the powers stop at two and "some power vanishes" is the same as "the square vanishes".

The Square-Zero Elements

Theorem (the square-zero set of the block). The square-zero elements of the block are

$$ \alpha\bigl(e_0+i\boldsymbol\mu\bigr), \qquad \alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{\times},\quad \boldsymbol\mu\in\mathbb{R}^3,\quad \lVert\boldsymbol\mu\rVert=1 , $$

together with the origin.

Proof. Let $\tilde P=\alpha e_0+\mathbf x$. If $\alpha=0$ the diagonal is $\tilde P\star\tilde P=K(\tilde P,\tilde P)e_0=-\lVert\mathbf x\rVert^2e_0$, which vanishes only for $\mathbf x=\mathbf 0$. If $\alpha\neq0$, the diagonal is central exactly when $\alpha\overline{\mathbf x}\in i\mathbb{R}^3$; write $\alpha\overline{x_k}=-ic_k$ with $c_k\in\mathbb{R}$, so $x_k=i c_k/\overline{\alpha}=i\alpha c_k/|\alpha|^2$ and $\lVert\mathbf x\rVert^2=\lVert\mathbf c\rVert^2/|\alpha|^2$. Then $\tilde P\star\tilde P=K(\tilde P,\tilde P)e_0$ with $K(\tilde P,\tilde P)=|\alpha|^2-\lVert\mathbf x\rVert^2$, which vanishes exactly when $\lVert\mathbf c\rVert^2=|\alpha|^4$. Writing $\mathbf c=|\alpha|^2\boldsymbol\mu$ with $\boldsymbol\mu\in\mathbb{R}^3$ a unit vector, the element is $\tilde P=\alpha e_0+i\alpha\boldsymbol\mu=\alpha(e_0+i\boldsymbol\mu)$, and every such element has $K=|\alpha|^2(1-\lVert\boldsymbol\mu\rVert^2)=0$ and vanishing vector part.

Corollary (the shape of the set). The nonzero nilpotents are a real cone of real dimension four: the parameter $\alpha$ runs over the punctured plane, of real dimension two, and the direction $\boldsymbol\mu$ over the unit sphere, of real dimension two.

Corollary (the diagonal witness). $\tilde P=e_0+ie_1$ is a nilpotent, the simplest one with $\alpha=1$ and $\boldsymbol\mu=e_1$; it has $\lVert\boldsymbol\mu\rVert=1$. The element $e_1$ is not a nilpotent, since $e_1\star e_1=-e_0$, though it is a pure zero divisor.

The Nilpotents and the Zero Divisors

Theorem (a nilpotent is a zero divisor). Every nonzero nilpotent of the block is a zero divisor, and it annihilates itself on both sides: $\tilde\Upsilon\star\tilde\Upsilon=0$, so the pair $(\tilde\Upsilon,\tilde\Upsilon)$ exhibits it as a two-sided zero divisor.

Proof. The relation $\tilde\Upsilon\star\tilde\Upsilon=0$ is the product of $\tilde\Upsilon$ with itself on either side; both factors are nonzero by hypothesis. The two-sidedness is Zero Divisors of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra.

Theorem (the nilpotents are non-pure zero divisors). A nilpotent $\tilde\Upsilon=\alpha(e_0+i\boldsymbol\mu)$ has scalar part $\alpha\neq0$ and central square $c(\tilde\Upsilon)=\alpha^2(1-\lVert\boldsymbol\mu\rVert^2)=0$; so it is a zero divisor of the block by the non-pure criterion, and it is not in the pure family.

Proof. $c(\alpha(e_0+i\boldsymbol\mu))=\alpha^2\bigl(1+\lVert i\boldsymbol\mu\rVert^2\bigr)=\alpha^2(1-\lVert\boldsymbol\mu\rVert^2)=0$ because $\lVert\boldsymbol\mu\rVert=1$.

The inclusion is strict: $c$ vanishes on the whole non-pure family $\{Q_0\neq0,\ c(\tilde Q)=0\}$, and the element

$$ e_0+e_1+i\sqrt2\,e_2 $$

has $Q_0=1$ and $c=1+1-2=0$, so it is a non-pure zero divisor, yet its square is $-2e_0-2e_1\neq0$; equivalently it annihilates the nonzero $e_0-e_1+i\sqrt2\,e_2$ without being square-zero. So the square-zero cone is a proper subset of the non-pure family, and the two must not be identified.

The class is thus strictly smaller than the zero-divisor set: the zero divisors include the whole vector subspace, of real dimension six, while the nilpotents are a real cone of real dimension four. The difference is the subject of §Comparison of Zero Divisors of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra.

The Nilpotents and the Idempotents

Theorem (the two classes meet only at the origin). No nonzero element of the block is both a nilpotent and an idempotent.

Proof. If $\tilde X\star\tilde X=\tilde X$ and $\tilde X\star\tilde X=0$, then $\tilde X=0$.

This is the two-family statement of the block. The zero-divisor set splits into the nilpotents $\alpha(e_0+i\boldsymbol\mu)$, whose square vanishes, and the remaining elements of the non-pure family, $Q_0\neq0$, $c(\tilde Q)=0$, whose square does not vanish (the witness $e_0+e_1+i\sqrt2\,e_2$); while the idempotents $-\tfrac12e_0+\boldsymbol\mu$ are regular and lie outside the zero-divisor set altogether. The contrast with the plain product is exact: there the nontrivial idempotents are zero divisors and the nilpotents are the pure family, while here the nilpotents are non-pure and the idempotents are regular.

The Conjugations

The three conjugations act on the nilpotent cone by permuting its parameters, and the cone is invariant under each of them.

conjugation value on $\tilde\Upsilon=\alpha(e_0+i\boldsymbol\mu)$
natural ${}^{\natural}$ $\alpha(e_0-i\boldsymbol\mu)$, the direction reversed
complex $\bar{\cdot}$ $\overline{\alpha}(e_0-i\boldsymbol\mu)$, the direction reversed and the coefficient conjugated
star ${}^{*}={}^{\natural}\circ\bar{\cdot}$ $\overline{\alpha}(e_0+i\boldsymbol\mu)$, the coefficient conjugated

Every value is again of the form $\alpha'(e_0+i\boldsymbol\mu')$ with $\alpha'\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ and $\boldsymbol\mu'$ a real unit vector, so each conjugation is a bijection of the cone onto itself; the star fixes the direction and conjugates the coefficient, and it fixes a nonzero nilpotent exactly when $\alpha$ is real. The class is a real cone of real dimension four, and it is decomposed into the orbits of the star by the real-valued coefficient.

Worked Examples

The simplest nilpotent. $\tilde\Upsilon=e_0+ie_1$: $\tilde\Upsilon\star\tilde\Upsilon=0$, so the index is two; the left-nested cube is $\tilde\Upsilon\star(\tilde\Upsilon\star\tilde\Upsilon)=\tilde\Upsilon\star0=0$.

A nilpotent with a complex coefficient. $\tilde\Upsilon=(1+i)(e_0+ie_2)$: by the theorem the square vanishes; the scalar part is $1+i$ and the central square is $0$.

A pure element of square zero inside the vector subspace. The origin alone: a pure element $\mathbf P\neq0$ has $\tilde P\star\tilde P=-\lVert\mathbf P\rVert^2e_0\neq0$, so the vector subspace contains no nonzero nilpotent of the block, in contrast with the plain product, whose nilpotent cone lies there.

A pure zero divisor that is not a nilpotent. $\tilde P=e_1$: it is a zero divisor of the block, since $e_1\star e_2=0$, and it is not a nilpotent, since $e_1\star e_1=-e_0$.

Summary

The nilpotent of the block has index two and no other: $\tilde P\star(\tilde P\star\tilde P)=0$ holds exactly when $\tilde P\star\tilde P=0$, so the nonzero nilpotents are the square-zero elements, and they are

$$ \alpha(e_0+i\boldsymbol\mu), \qquad \alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{\times},\quad \boldsymbol\mu\in\mathbb{R}^3,\quad \lVert\boldsymbol\mu\rVert=1 , $$

a real cone of real dimension four. Every nonzero nilpotent is a non-pure zero divisor that annihilates itself on both sides; the nilpotents are strictly smaller than the zero-divisor set, whose pure family is the whole vector subspace. No nonzero nilpotent is an idempotent, the two named classes of the block meeting only at the origin; the nontrivial idempotents are regular and lie outside the zero-divisor set. The three conjugations permute the cone, the star conjugating the coefficient and fixing the direction.
