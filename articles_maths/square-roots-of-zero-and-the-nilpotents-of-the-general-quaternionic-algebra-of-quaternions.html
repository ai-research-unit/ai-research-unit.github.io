Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents of the General Quaternionic Algebra

Introduction

This article classifies the square roots of the central value $0$ in the general quaternionic bilinear product, and then the nilpotents of that product, the elements some power of which vanishes in the same product.

Both notions name a product, and throughout this article the product is the general quaternionic bilinear product $\tilde p\star\tilde q=\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde q$ of the general quaternionic algebra, the $\mathrm{GQA}$ of The Algebraic Structures over the Quaternion $\mathbb{R}$ Space; the square of the article is the square of $\star$, and an unqualified nilpotent is a nilpotent of $\star$. The general definition of the square and of the roots of a central value, and the general definition of a nilpotent, are Definitions for the Study of the Algebraic Structures over the Quaternion $\mathbb{R}$ Space, §The Element Vocabulary.

The whole article is settled by one computation, the square of every element being the central element $N(\tilde p)e_0$. The root equation of the value $0$ therefore reads $N(\tilde p)=0$, and the nilpotent equation is decided by the same scalar. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the norm is a sum of four squares, so it vanishes only at the origin; the roots of $0$ are the single element $0$, and the product has no nilpotent at all, of index two or of any larger index. The absence is the collapse of the complex sibling, where the root set of $0$ is the whole zero-divisor cone.

The results. The root of $0$ in the general quaternionic product is the origin alone, $\{\tilde\xi:\tilde\xi\star\tilde\xi=0\}=\{\tilde\xi:N(\tilde\xi)=0\}=\{0\}$, and the root set coincides with the zero set of the norm and with the square-zero set. The square-zero set is the origin alone, so the product has no square-zero element and no nilpotent; the absence is proved for every index, through the power identity $\tilde p^{k}=N(\tilde p)(\tilde p^{\natural})^{(k-2)}$ and the plain division property, and not for the index two alone. Neither remarkable subspace, the centre nor the vector subspace, carries a square-zero element. Against the six structures of the real chapter the general quaternionic structure is one of the four whose square-zero set is the origin, the other two being the whole space; against the twelve structures of the complex chapter, whose square-zero sets are ordered through the cone, the real block records the collapse of that cone to its apex.

Boundaries. The product, the square and the reduction of the root equation are Introduction to the General Quaternionic Algebra of Quaternions and Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the General Quaternionic Algebra; the norm, its definiteness and the plain division property are Norm and Invertibility in the General Quaternionic Algebra and Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Algebra; the zero divisors are Zero Divisors of the General Quaternionic Algebra; the idempotents are Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the General Quaternionic Algebra; the structure-level comparison of the six square-zero sets is Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents in the Algebraic Structures over the Quaternion $\mathbb{R}$ Space, and the ordering of the twelve complex sets is quoted from Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents in the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space; the definiteness of the form is The Isotropic Structure of the General Quaternionic Algebra; the general root problem for an arbitrary radicand is Square Roots of a General Element in the General Quaternionic Algebra.

Conventions. $\mathbb{H}$ is the real quaternion algebra with basis $e_0=1,e_1,e_2,e_3$, the rules $e_k^2=-e_0$ and $e_1e_2=e_3$. Juxtaposition is the plain product and $\star$ the general quaternionic bilinear product $\tilde p\star\tilde q=\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde q$, with the conjugation $\tilde p^{\natural}=p_0-\vec p$ and $\tilde p=p_0e_0+\vec p$, $\vec p=p_1e_1+p_2e_2+p_3e_3$. The powers are the left-nested products $\tilde p^{1}=\tilde p$ and $\tilde p^{k+1}=\tilde p\star\tilde p^{k}$, and the plain powers are the same nestings in the plain product, written $\tilde p^{(k)}$, with $\tilde p^{(0)}=e_0$. The algebraic norm is

$$ N(\tilde p)=\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde p=p_0^2+\lvert\vec p\rvert^2=\sum_{\mu=0}^{3}p_\mu^2 , $$

positive definite, with polar form $\beta(\tilde p,\tilde q)=p_0q_0+\vec p\cdot\vec q$. The centre is the scalar line $\mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{H}}=\mathbb{R}e_0$ and the vector subspace is $\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})=\{\vec p\}$.

The Roots of Zero

The root of $0$ is the origin alone, and the root set is the zero set of the norm and the square-zero set of the product: the cone of the complex sibling has collapsed to its apex.

The Root Set

Theorem (the root set of $0$). The roots of $0$ in the general quaternionic product are the solutions of

$$ \tilde\xi\star\tilde\xi=0 \iff N(\tilde\xi)=0 , $$

and the solution set is the single element $\tilde\xi=0$.

Proof. The reduction of the root equation of a central value is Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the General Quaternionic Algebra, §The Root Equation and Its Reduction, which reads $\tilde\xi\star\tilde\xi=\lambda e_0$ as the single equation $N(\tilde\xi)=\lambda$; at $\lambda=0$ this is $N=0$. The norm is a sum of four real squares, so $N(\tilde\xi)=0$ forces every coordinate to vanish, that is $\tilde\xi=0$; the norm is positive definite (Norm and Invertibility in the General Quaternionic Algebra), and the origin is its only zero.

Corollary (the coincidence). The root set of $0$, the zero set of the norm and the square-zero set of the product coincide, and the common set is the origin:

$$ \{\tilde\xi:\tilde\xi\star\tilde\xi=0\}=\{\tilde\xi:N(\tilde\xi)=0\}=\{0\} . $$

Proof. The first equality is the theorem and the second is the definiteness of the norm; the square-zero set is by definition the set $\{\tilde\xi:\tilde\xi\star\tilde\xi=0\}$, so it is the same set.

The Comparison with the Plain Product

The same level set is read side by side with the plain product, and over $\mathbb{R}$ the two agree.

value $\lambda$ quaternionic $\tilde\xi\star\tilde\xi$ plain $\tilde\xi^2$
$0$ $\{0\}$ alone $\{0\}$ alone

Proposition. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the root set of $0$ is the origin for the quaternionic product and for the plain product alike; both algebras are division algebras, and neither has a square-zero element.

Proof. The quaternionic column is §The Root Set. For the plain column the plain square is $(\xi_0^2-\lvert\vec\xi\rvert^2)e_0+2\xi_0\vec\xi$; the equation $\tilde\xi^2=0$ is the pair $\xi_0^2=\lvert\vec\xi\rvert^2$ and $\xi_0\vec\xi=0$. If $\xi_0\neq0$ then $\vec\xi=0$ and $\xi_0^2=0$, a contradiction; hence $\xi_0=0$ and then $\lvert\vec\xi\rvert^2=0$, so $\tilde\xi=0$. The plain column is Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents of the General Plain Algebra.

Remark (the collapse of the complex root set). In the complex chapter the root set of $0$ of the quaternionic product is the whole zero-divisor cone $\{N=0\}$, of real dimension six, whose pure part is a cone of real dimension four and whose non-pure part is the family of the plain-idempotent multiples. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the cone has no nonzero point and shrinks to its apex, the origin; the collapse is the definiteness of the quaternionic form (The Isotropic Structure of the General Quaternionic Algebra), and it is the same fact that removes the zero divisors (Zero Divisors of the General Quaternionic Algebra) and the isotropic directions. The complex sibling is Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents of the General Quaternionic Algebra.

The Definition and the Criterion

The square-zero set of the product is the origin alone, so the product has no square-zero element and no nilpotent of index two.

Definition. Let $\star$ be a product on $\mathbb{H}$ and let $\tilde\nu\in\mathbb{H}$. The element $\tilde\nu$ is nilpotent of $\star$ when $\tilde\nu\neq0$ and $\tilde\nu^{k}=0$ for some positive integer $k$; the least such $k$ is the index. The case $k=2$ is a square-zero element, and the square-zero set of the product is the solution set of $\tilde\nu\star\tilde\nu=0$. The definition is Definitions for the Study of the Algebraic Structures, §The Element Vocabulary; the origin is not a nilpotent, the class taking its nonzero members. Throughout this article the product is $\star$.

Lemma (the square is central). For every $\tilde p\in\mathbb{H}$,

$$ \tilde p\star\tilde p=\tilde p^{\natural}\tilde p=N(\tilde p)\,e_0 , \qquad N(\tilde p)=p_0^2+p_1^2+p_2^2+p_3^2 . $$

Proof. At equal arguments the vector part of the product is $p_0\vec p-p_0\vec p-\vec p\times\vec p=0$, so the square is the scalar part $p_0^2+\vec p\cdot\vec p$ times $e_0$ (Introduction to the General Quaternionic Algebra of Quaternions, and Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the General Quaternionic Algebra).

Theorem (the criterion). For $\tilde p\in\mathbb{H}$, $\tilde p\star\tilde p=0$ if and only if $N(\tilde p)=0$, that is if and only if $\tilde p=0$. The square-zero set of the general quaternionic product is the single point $\{0\}$, and the product has no square-zero element.

Proof. By the lemma $\tilde p\star\tilde p=N(\tilde p)e_0$, which vanishes exactly when $N(\tilde p)=0$. The norm is a sum of four real squares, so it vanishes only at $\tilde p=0$; the norm is positive definite, and the criterion is met by the origin alone. The origin has vanishing square but is not a nilpotent, the nilpotent class taking the nonzero members.

No Nilpotent of Any Index

Every element with a vanishing power is the origin, so the general quaternionic product has no nilpotent of any index and its nilpotent class is empty.

The square-zero case does not by itself exclude a nilpotent of larger index, since the definition asks for some power, not the second. The following computation closes the question.

Lemma (the powers). For every $\tilde p\in\mathbb{H}$ and every $k\geq2$,

$$ \tilde p^{k}=N(\tilde p)\,\bigl(\tilde p^{\natural}\bigr)^{(k-2)} , $$

where the factor on the right is the plain power of order $k-2$ of $\tilde p^{\natural}$.

Proof. By induction on $k$. For $k=2$ the identity is the central square $\tilde p^{2}=N(\tilde p)e_0=N(\tilde p)(\tilde p^{\natural})^{(0)}$. Assume it for $k$; then, using the plain associativity inside the power,

$$ \tilde p^{k+1}=\tilde p\star\tilde p^{k} =\tilde p^{\natural}\bigl(N(\tilde p)(\tilde p^{\natural})^{(k-2)}\bigr) =N(\tilde p)\,(\tilde p^{\natural})^{(k-1)} , $$

which is the identity of order $k+1$.

Theorem (no nilpotent). Every $\tilde p\in\mathbb{H}$ with $\tilde p^{k}=0$ for some positive integer $k$ is $\tilde p=0$. The general quaternionic product has no nilpotent of any index, and its nilpotent class is empty.

Proof. For $k=1$ the assertion is the definition. For $k=2$ it is the criterion of §The Definition and the Criterion. Let $k\geq3$. By the power lemma $\tilde p^{k}=N(\tilde p)(\tilde p^{\natural})^{(k-2)}=0$, so either $N(\tilde p)=0$ or $(\tilde p^{\natural})^{(k-2)}=0$. In the first case $\tilde p=0$ by definiteness. In the second, the plain powers of a nonzero quaternion do not vanish: if $\tilde x^{(m)}=0$ with $m\geq1$ and $\tilde x\neq0$, left multiplication by the plain inverse $\tilde x^{-1}$ lowers the exponent, and iteration gives $\tilde x=0$, a contradiction. Hence $\tilde p^{\natural}=0$ and $\tilde p=0$. The plain division property used here is Norm and Invertibility in the General Plain Algebra.

Corollary (the index). No element of the block has nilpotency index two or more, and the square-zero set, the nilpotent class and the class of elements of finite nilpotency index are $\{0\}$, $\varnothing$ and $\{0\}$ respectively.

Remark (the absence stated). The class of nilpotents of the block is empty, and the article states the absence and its reason and stops there. It does not import the nilpotent cone of the complex sibling, whose square-zero set is the whole cone $\{N=0\}$: over $\mathbb{R}$ the cone has collapsed to a point, and the class has no content. The empty nilpotent class and the empty zero-divisor set of Zero Divisors of the General Quaternionic Algebra are the same definiteness, read on the equation $N=0$.

The Nilpotents and the Roots of the Three Central Values

The root set of a central value is the solution set of $\tilde\xi\star\tilde\xi=c$ with $c$ central; the square-zero set is the value $0$, and the value $0$ is the only central value whose root set degenerates to the origin.

Theorem (the three central values). The root sets of the central values $-e_0$, $0$ and $+e_0$ are

$$ \{\tilde\xi:\tilde\xi\star\tilde\xi=-e_0\}=\varnothing,\qquad \{\tilde\xi:\tilde\xi\star\tilde\xi=0\}=\{0\},\qquad \{\tilde\xi:\tilde\xi\star\tilde\xi=+e_0\}=\mathrm{Sp}(1) , $$

the first empty, the third the unit sphere of real dimension three. The statement and the reduction are Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the General Quaternionic Algebra, §The Roots of Plus One; the root set of $0$ is §The Root Set of this article.

Proof. The three equations are $N(\tilde\xi)=-1$, $N(\tilde\xi)=0$ and $N(\tilde\xi)=1$; the norm is positive definite, so the first has no solution, the second has the origin alone, and the third is the norm-one level set, the sphere $\mathrm{Sp}(1)$.

Corollary. The square-zero set is the root set of $0$ and is strictly smaller than the root set of $+e_0$, and the nilpotents, which are its nonzero members, do not exist; the element $0$ is at the same time the only root of $0$ and the only idempotent that is not the left unit.

The Nilpotents on the Remarkable Subspaces

The square-zero equation is the vanishing of the norm, and the norm is read on the two remarkable subspaces of $\mathbb{H}$, the scalar line $\mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{H}}=\mathbb{R}e_0$, the centre, and the vector subspace $\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})$.

Proposition. On the centre and on the vector subspace the square vanishes only at the origin, so neither subspace carries a square-zero element and neither carries a nilpotent; in the centre the square is $A^2e_0$, and in the vector subspace it is the central element $\lvert\vec p\rvert^2e_0\neq0$ for every nonzero pure $\vec p$.

subspace element square nilpotents
$\mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{H}}$, the centre $Ae_0$ $A^2e_0$ none
$\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})$, the vector subspace $\vec p$ $\lvert\vec p\rvert^2e_0$ none

Proof. The square is the norm on the subspace of each row, by the lemma; $A^2$ vanishes only at $A=0$ and $\lvert\vec p\rvert^2$ only at $\vec p=0$. A nonzero pure element has a central nonzero square, so no pure element is square-zero, and no central element is either.

The Nilpotents and the Other Structures of the Space

The comparison of the six square-zero sets is Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents in the Algebraic Structures over the Quaternion $\mathbb{R}$ Space, the structure-level article of the real chapter, and its verdict for the block is the origin alone.

Theorem (the six square-zero sets). Among the six structures of the quaternion real space the square-zero set is $\{0\}$ for the general plain algebra, the symmetric plain algebra, the general quaternionic algebra and the symmetric quaternionic algebra, and it is the whole space $\mathbb{H}$ for the two antisymmetric structures. Only two square-zero sets occur, and the general quaternionic structure is one of the four with the origin alone.

Proof. The four rows are the theorem of Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents in the Algebraic Structures over the Quaternion $\mathbb{R}$ Space, §The Six Root Sets of $0$, Which Are the Six Square-Zero Sets, where the plain square is anisotropic and the quaternionic square is the definite norm; the two alternating rows are the same theorem, the products being alternating. The four structures with the origin alone are exactly those whose square is the plain square or the central element $N(\tilde x)e_0$, neither of which vanishes off the origin over $\mathbb{R}$.

Remark (the complex ordering and the real collapse). The complex chapter carries twelve structures and its comparison article orders the square-zero sets through the cone, $Z_3=\{0\}\subsetneq Z_1\subsetneq Z_2$ and $Z_3\subsetneq Z_4\subsetneq Z_2$ for the four general products (Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents in the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space, §The Order of the Four Sets), and it places the general quaternionic and the symmetric quaternionic structures at the whole cone. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the cone has become a point, so the general quaternionic set drops from the whole cone to the origin and joins the three real structures whose square vanishes only at the origin; the ordering through the cone disappears with the cone, and only the two extreme sets, the origin and the whole space, survive. The collapse is the same definiteness that carries the zero-divisor set of the block from the punctured cone to the empty set.

Summary

In the general quaternionic bilinear product the square of every element is the central element $N(\tilde p)e_0$, and the square vanishes exactly when the norm does. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the norm is a sum of four squares, hence positive definite, so the root set of $0$ is the origin alone, the square-zero set is the origin alone, and the product has no nilpotent, of index two or of any larger index. The absence is not a consequence of the square-zero case alone: the powers satisfy $\tilde p^{k}=N(\tilde p)(\tilde p^{\natural})^{(k-2)}$ for $k\geq2$, so a vanishing power forces either a vanishing norm or a vanishing plain power, and neither occurs off the origin because the plain algebra is a division algebra. The root set of $0$ coincides with the zero set of the norm and is strictly smaller than the root set of $+e_0$, the sphere $\mathrm{Sp}(1)$; the root set of $-e_0$ is empty. Neither remarkable subspace, the centre nor the vector subspace, carries a square-zero element. Against the six structures of the real chapter the general quaternionic structure is one of the four whose square-zero set is the origin, the other two being the whole space; against the twelve structures of the complex chapter, whose square-zero sets are ordered through the cone, the real block records the collapse of that cone to a point, the same definiteness that removes the zero divisors.
