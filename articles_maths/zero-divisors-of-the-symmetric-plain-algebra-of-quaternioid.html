Zero Divisors of the Symmetric Plain Algebra

Introduction

The symmetric plain algebra of $\mathbb{H}$ carries the Jordan product $\tilde p\bullet\tilde q=\tfrac12(\tilde p\tilde q+\tilde q\tilde p)$ of Introduction to the Symmetric Plain Algebra of Quaternions. Unlike the plain algebra, which is a division algebra, the block has zero divisors: the product of two nonzero elements can vanish. This article determines them.

This is not the algebra $\mathbb{H}$. The two algebras share the space, the addition, the real scalars and the unit, but $\bullet$ is a different multiplication and the projection onto the scalar line is not multiplicative. Throughout, the natural sign ${}^{\natural}$ is the quaternion conjugation, the bar is the identity because $\mathbb{R}$ has no nontrivial involution, and the star coincides with the natural sign and gives nothing new.

The invertibility criterion is Norm and Invertibility in the Symmetric Plain Algebra, where the contrast between invertibility and division is stated; the multiplication operators, of which the zero divisors are exactly the singular ones with nonzero parameter, are The Multiplication Operators of the Symmetric Plain Algebra, below this article in this category.

Conventions. The algebra is $\mathbb{H}$ with basis $e_0=1,e_1,e_2,e_3$, $e_1e_2=e_3$ and $e_1^2=e_2^2=e_3^2=-e_0$. A generic element is $\tilde q=q_0e_0+\vec q$ with $\vec q=\sum_{k=1}^{3}q_ke_k$ and $q_\mu\in\mathbb{R}$; $\vec p\cdot\vec q$ is the Euclidean dot product and $\vec p\times\vec q$ the vector product of the vector parts; the norm is $N(\tilde q)=\tilde q\tilde q^{\natural}=q_0^2+|\vec q|^2$ and the scalar line and the vector subspace $\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})=\operatorname{Im}\mathbb{H}$ are those of Introduction to the Remarkable Subspaces of the Quaternions.

The Zero Divisors of the Block

The Definition

Definition. A nonzero element $\tilde p$ is a zero divisor of the block when there is a nonzero element $\tilde q$ with

$$ \tilde p\bullet\tilde q=0 . $$

Since the product is commutative, the left and the right zero divisors are the same, and the element $\tilde q$ is a partner of $\tilde p$.

The Criterion

Theorem (criterion). A nonzero element is a zero divisor if and only if it is pure:

$$ \tilde p\ \text{is a zero divisor}\iff p_0=0\iff \tilde p\in\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})\setminus\{0\} . $$

Proof. Write $\tilde p=p_0e_0+\vec p$ and $\tilde q=q_0e_0+\vec q$, so that

$$ \tilde p\bullet\tilde q=\bigl(p_0q_0-\vec p\cdot\vec q\bigr)e_0+p_0\vec q+q_0\vec p . $$

If $p_0=0$ and $\tilde p\neq0$, choose a nonzero pure $\vec v$ with $\vec v\perp\vec p$ and $\tilde q=\vec v$; then $\tilde p\bullet\tilde q=-\vec p\cdot\vec v\,e_0=\mathbf 0$. If $p_0\neq0$, suppose $\tilde p\bullet\tilde q=0$; the vector part gives $p_0\vec q=-q_0\vec p$, so $\vec q$ is a multiple of $\vec p$, say $\vec q=\lambda\vec p$; the scalar part then gives $p_0q_0=\lambda|\vec p|^2$, and combining the two, $p_0q_0=\lambda|\vec p|^2$ with $\lambda=-q_0/p_0$ yields $p_0^2q_0=-q_0|\vec p|^2$, that is $q_0(p_0^2+|\vec p|^2)=0$, so $q_0=0$; then $\vec q=\lambda\vec p$ and the scalar part is $-\lambda|\vec p|^2=0$, so $\lambda=0$ and $\tilde q=0$, a contradiction. Hence a nonzero element with $p_0\neq0$ has no nonzero partner.

Corollary (the operator form of the criterion). With the left multiplication operator $L^{\bullet}_{\tilde p}\tilde q=\tilde p\bullet\tilde q$ of The Multiplication Operators of the Symmetric Plain Algebra, a nonzero element is a zero divisor exactly when $L^{\bullet}_{\tilde p}$ is singular, and

$$ \det L^{\bullet}_{\tilde p}=p_0^2\,N(\tilde p), $$

which vanishes, for $\tilde p\neq0$, exactly when $p_0=0$.

The set of partners of a pure element is its annihilator, whose two sides, whose operator and whose rank are the companion article's (Annihilators of the Symmetric Plain Algebra, below this article in this category).

The Pairs That Multiply to Zero

The Orthogonality Criterion

Theorem. For nonzero $\tilde p,\tilde q$,

$$ \tilde p\bullet\tilde q=0\iff \tilde p=\vec u,\ \tilde q=\vec v\ \text{with}\ \vec u,\vec v\ \text{pure},\ \text{nonzero and orthogonal},\ \vec u\cdot\vec v=0 . $$

Proof. By the criterion both factors are pure, and the product of two pure elements is $\vec u\bullet\vec v=-\vec u\cdot\vec v\,e_0$, which vanishes exactly when $\vec u\cdot\vec v=0$.

So the zero-divisor pairs are exactly the pairs of orthogonal nonzero vectors of the three-dimensional vector subspace.

The Families and the Dimension Count

Proposition. The zero-divisor set of the block is the vector subspace with the origin removed, of real dimension three; the set of zero-divisor pairs is the set of ordered pairs of orthogonal nonzero vectors, of real dimension five; and the set of unordered pairs, equivalently the set of two-planes through the origin of the vector subspace, is the Grassmannian $\mathrm{Gr}(2,3)\cong\mathbb{RP}^2$ of dimension two.

Proof. The zero-divisor set is $\mathrm{Vect}(\mathbb{H})\setminus\{0\}$, of real dimension three. A pair is an ordered pair $(\vec u,\vec v)$ with $\vec u\neq0$, $\vec v\neq0$, $\vec u\cdot\vec v=0$: choosing $\vec u$ is three parameters, then $\vec v$ ranges over the two-dimensional plane $\vec u^{\perp}$ minus the origin, two parameters, so the ordered pairs form a set of dimension five. The unordered pair determines the plane $\mathbb{R}\vec u\oplus\mathbb{R}\vec v=\vec u^{\perp}$, and every two-plane of $\mathbb{R}^3$ arises, so the unordered pairs of an orthonormal pair are the two-planes, the Grassmannian $\mathrm{Gr}(2,3)$ of dimension two.

The Squares of the Elements

The Square of a Pure Element

Proposition. The square of a pure element is a non-positive scalar:

$$ \vec u\bullet\vec u=\vec u^2=-|\vec u|^2e_0 . $$

In particular a pure element is a zero divisor and has a central square, and $\vec u\bullet\vec u=0$ only for $\vec u=0$.

Proof. The square is the plain square, and $\vec u^2=-\vec u\cdot\vec u\,e_0$ because the cross term of a vector with itself vanishes. The scalar $-|\vec u|^2$ is zero only for $\vec u=0$.

The Square of a Non-Pure Element

Proposition. The square of a general element is

$$ \tilde q\bullet\tilde q=\bigl(q_0^2-|\vec q|^2\bigr)e_0+2q_0\vec q, $$

with scalar part $q_0^2-|\vec q|^2$ and vector part $2q_0\vec q$. It has a nonzero vector part exactly when $\tilde q$ is non-pure and nonzero.

Proof. The square is the plain square, displayed by the scalar–vector rule with $\tilde p=\tilde q$. The vector part $2q_0\vec q$ vanishes when $q_0=0$ or $\vec q=0$, that is, for a pure element or a scalar.

The Associated Idempotents

Proposition. The idempotents of the block are exactly $0$ and $e_0$, and no nontrivial idempotent is attached to a zero divisor:

$$ \tilde\pi\bullet\tilde\pi=\tilde\pi\iff \tilde\pi\in\{0,e_0\}. $$

Proof. The equation is the plain equation $\tilde\pi^2=\tilde\pi$, since the square of the block is the plain square. From the vector part, $(2\pi_0-1)\vec\pi=0$: either $\vec\pi=0$, giving $\pi_0^2=\pi_0$ and $\tilde\pi\in\{0,e_0\}$, or $\pi_0=\tfrac12$, and then the scalar part reads $\tfrac14-|\vec\pi|^2=\tfrac12$, that is $|\vec\pi|^2=-\tfrac14$, which has no real solution.

Remark (why no idempotent is built from a zero divisor). In an algebra with a pure root of $+e_0$ the element $\tfrac12(e_0+\vec u)$ is a nontrivial idempotent; here the square of a pure element is $-|\vec u|^2e_0$, the negative of what is needed, so the construction never closes and the zero divisors of the block carry no idempotent with them.

Summary

A nonzero element of the block is a zero divisor, that is, has a nonzero partner $\tilde q$ with $\tilde p\bullet\tilde q=0$, if and only if it is pure: the criterion is $p_0=0$, equivalently the vanishing of the left multiplication operator, whose determinant is $p_0^2N(\tilde p)$. The partners of a nonzero pure $\vec u$ are the elements of the two-dimensional plane $\vec u^{\perp}$ inside the vector subspace, and the zero-divisor pairs are exactly the pairs of orthogonal nonzero pure elements, an ordered family of real dimension five whose unordered version is the Grassmannian $\mathrm{Gr}(2,3)\cong\mathbb{RP}^2$. A pure element has the negative scalar square $-|\vec u|^2e_0$; a non-pure element has the square $(q_0^2-|\vec q|^2)e_0+2q_0\vec q$ with nonzero vector part; and the idempotents are only $0$ and $e_0$, so no nontrivial idempotent is attached to a zero divisor. The definiteness of the norm collapses the criterion to $q_0=0$ and the idempotents to $\{0,e_0\}$.
