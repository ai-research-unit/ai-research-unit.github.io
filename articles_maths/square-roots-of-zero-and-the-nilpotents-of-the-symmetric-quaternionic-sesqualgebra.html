Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra

Introduction

This article reads two subjects of the block, the symmetric quaternionic sesquilinear product $\tilde P\star\tilde Q=\tfrac12(\tilde P^{\natural}\tilde Q^{*}+\tilde Q^{*}\tilde P^{\natural})$ of Introduction to the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra of Biquaternions, and in this order. The first is the square roots of the central value $0$, the elements $\tilde\Xi$ of $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$ with

$$ \tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=0 . $$

The second is the nilpotents of the block, the nonzero elements whose square vanishes. The two subjects are one: the roots of $0$ are the origin together with the square-zero elements, and the nonzero square-zero elements are exactly the nilpotents.

The nilpotent is the solution of an equation in the product, and the product is part of the definition: it is stated once for a general product in Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures, §Powers, the Square and the Roots and §The Nilpotent, and read on the products of the space. The unqualified word means the block. The block is not associative, so a power needs a bracketing, and the left-nested reading is used throughout; the first statement of the article is that the ambiguity does not matter here, because no element of the block has a vanishing cube without having a vanishing square. So the block has nilpotents of index two only, and the nilpotent of the block is the square-zero element.

The product is Introduction to the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra of Biquaternions; the diagonal $\tilde Q\star\tilde Q$ is The Non-Central Algebraic Norm and the Two Halves of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra; the roots of $+1$ and the idempotents are Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra; the roots of a general value are Square Roots of a General Element in the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra; the zero divisors and the criterion are Zero Divisors of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra; the isotropic elements of the Krein form are The Isotropic Structure of the General Quaternionic Sesqualgebra; the nilpotents of the plain product are Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents of the General Plain Algebra; and the square-zero sets of the four general products are Zero Divisors in the 12 Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space.

Conventions. $\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$ with basis $e_0,e_1,e_2,e_3$, $e_k^2=-e_0$ and $e_1e_2=e_3$; $\tilde Q=\sum_\mu Q_\mu e_\mu$ with $Q_\mu\in\mathbb{C}$ and central scalar imaginary $i$; $\vec Q=\sum_kQ_ke_k$; $(\vec P,\vec Q)=\sum_kP_kQ_k$; ${}^{\natural}$ negates $e_1,e_2,e_3$ and fixes the coefficients, $\bar{\cdot}$ conjugates the coefficients, and ${}^{*}={}^{\natural}\circ\bar{\cdot}$; $\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{B}}$ is the real quaternion subspace. The diagonal is $\tilde Q\star\tilde Q=K(\tilde Q,\tilde Q)e_0-Q_0\overline{\vec Q}-\overline{Q_0}\vec Q$ with $K(\tilde Q,\tilde Q)=|Q_0|^2-\sum_k|Q_k|^2$, and it is a real quaternion for every $\tilde Q$. The central square is $C(\tilde Q)=\sum_\mu Q_\mu^2$, the scalar of $\tilde Q\tilde Q^{\natural}=C(\tilde Q)e_0$. A root of a central value is written $\tilde\Xi$ (§The Root Convention of Conventions in Mathematics), a nilpotent $\tilde\Upsilon$ (§The Nilpotent Convention), and a generic element $\tilde P$ or $\tilde Q$. Throughout this article the product is the block.

The results. Every nilpotent of the block has index two, so $\tilde P\star(\tilde P\star\tilde P)=0$ exactly when $\tilde P\star\tilde P=0$, and the nonzero nilpotents are the square-zero elements $\alpha(e_0+i\vec n)$ with $\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ and $\vec n\in\mathbb{R}^3$ a unit vector. They form a real cone of real dimension four, and it contains no pure element. Every nonzero nilpotent is a non-pure zero divisor that annihilates itself on both sides, and the nilpotents are strictly smaller than the non-pure family, whose element $e_0+e_1+i\sqrt2\,e_2$ is not square-zero. No nonzero nilpotent is an idempotent, the two classes meeting only at the origin, and the three conjugations permute the cone.

The Roots of Zero

The roots of $0$ in the block are the origin and the elements $\alpha(e_0+i\vec n)$ with $\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ and $\vec n\in\mathbb{R}^3$ a unit vector; they form a real cone of dimension four that contains no pure element.

A root of $0$ is an element $\tilde\Xi\in\mathbb{B}$ with $\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=0$, the square being the one of the block.

The Reduction

Let $\tilde\Xi=\alpha e_0+\vec x$ with $\alpha\in\mathbb{C}$ and $\vec x\in\mathbb{C}^3$. The diagonal formula of the block is

$$ \tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=K(\tilde\Xi,\tilde\Xi)e_0-2\,\mathrm{Re}\bigl(\alpha\overline{\vec x}\bigr), \qquad K(\tilde\Xi,\tilde\Xi)=|\alpha|^2-\lVert\vec x\rVert^2, $$

whose scalar part is real and whose vector part is a real vector. The right-hand side $0$ is central, so the equation splits into the vector condition $\alpha\overline{\vec x}\in i\mathbb{R}^3$ and the scalar condition $K(\tilde\Xi,\tilde\Xi)=0$. When the vector condition holds, $\alpha\overline{x_k}=-ic_k$ with $c_k\in\mathbb{R}$, that is $x_k=i\alpha c_k/|\alpha|^2$ for $\alpha\neq0$, so $\lVert\vec x\rVert^2=\lVert\vec c\rVert^2/|\alpha|^2$ and the scalar condition reads

$$ \lVert\vec c\rVert^2=|\alpha|^4 . $$

This is the same reduction as for the roots of $+1$ (Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra), read at the central value $0$.

The Zero Element

If $\alpha=0$ the vector condition holds automatically, the diagonal is $\tilde\Xi\star\tilde\Xi=K(\tilde\Xi,\tilde\Xi)e_0=-\lVert\vec x\rVert^2e_0$, and it vanishes only for $\vec x=\mathbf 0$. So the origin is a root of $0$, and it is the only pure root: a pure element $\vec P\neq0$ has $\tilde P\star\tilde P=-\lVert\vec P\rVert^2e_0\neq0$, in contrast with the plain product, whose nilpotent cone lies in the vector subspace.

The Square-Zero Cone

Let $\alpha\neq0$. The equation $\lVert\vec c\rVert^2=|\alpha|^4$ has the real solutions $\vec c$ of modulus $|\alpha|^2$, and every one of them is written

$$ \vec c=|\alpha|^2\vec n, \qquad \vec n\in\mathbb{R}^3,\quad \lVert\vec n\rVert=1 , $$

so the root is

$$ \tilde\Xi=\alpha e_0+\frac{i\alpha}{|\alpha|^2}\vec c =\alpha e_0+i\alpha\vec n =\alpha\bigl(e_0+i\vec n\bigr). $$

For a fixed direction $\vec n$ the coefficient $\alpha$ runs over the whole punctured plane, so the generator $e_0+i\vec n$ spans a complex line of square-zero elements; the union over the unit sphere is the real cone of the next theorem.

Statement of the Theorem

Theorem (the roots of $0$). The roots of $0$ in the block are the origin and the elements

$$ \alpha\bigl(e_0+i\vec n\bigr), \qquad \alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{\times},\quad \vec n\in\mathbb{R}^3,\quad \lVert\vec n\rVert=1 . $$

Verification

Each element of the display is a root of $0$: its scalar condition holds because

$$ K\bigl(\alpha(e_0+i\vec n),\alpha(e_0+i\vec n)\bigr) =|\alpha|^2-\lVert i\alpha\vec n\rVert^2 =|\alpha|^2\bigl(1-\lVert\vec n\rVert^2\bigr)=0 , $$

and its vector condition holds because $\alpha\overline{(i\alpha n_k)}=-i|\alpha|^2n_k$ is purely imaginary for each $k$. Conversely the reduction shows that no other element is a root.

The Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents

The nonzero roots of $0$ are exactly the square-zero elements, that is the nilpotents of the block.

A nonzero element is a root of $0$ when its square vanishes and it is not the origin; that is the definition of a square-zero element, and §Index Two shows that a square-zero element is the only kind of nilpotent the block has. So the root set of $0$ is the origin together with the nilpotent cone, and the two names denote the same objects.

The Nilpotents

The nonzero nilpotents of the block are the elements $\alpha(e_0+i\vec n)$ with $\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ and $\vec n\in\mathbb{R}^3$ a unit vector; they form a real cone of real dimension four, every one has index two, and none is an idempotent.

The Definition

Definition (nilpotent of a product). Let $\star$ be a product on $\mathbb{B}$ and let $\tilde\Upsilon$ be an element. It is nilpotent of $\star$ when some power vanishes,

$$ \tilde\Upsilon^{\star k}=0 \quad\text{for some } k\geq1 , $$

the power being the left-nested reading $\tilde\Upsilon^{\star k}=(\cdots((\tilde\Upsilon\star\tilde\Upsilon)\star\tilde\Upsilon)\cdots\star\tilde\Upsilon)$ of Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures, §Powers, the Square and the Roots. The least such $k$ is the index of nilpotency; an element of index two, $\tilde\Upsilon\star\tilde\Upsilon=0$ with $\tilde\Upsilon\neq0$, is a square-zero element of $\star$.

This is Definitions for the Study of the 12 Algebraic Structures, §The Nilpotent. Unqualified, the product is the block, and the nilpotent of the block is the solution of $\tilde\Upsilon^{\star k}=0$ for some $k$. The origin is the trivial nilpotent and is excluded from the classification.

Index Two

Every nilpotent of the block has index two: $\tilde P\star(\tilde P\star\tilde P)=0$ holds exactly when $\tilde P\star\tilde P=0$, and the block carries no nilpotent of higher index.

Theorem (the only index is two). For every element $\tilde P$ of the block,

$$ \tilde P\star(\tilde P\star\tilde P)=0 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad (\tilde P\star\tilde P)\star\tilde P=0 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \tilde P\star\tilde P=0 . $$

Proof. Suppose $\tilde P\star(\tilde P\star\tilde P)=0$ and put $\tilde S=\tilde P\star\tilde P$. The diagonal formula gives $\tilde S=\sigma e_0+\vec V$ with $\sigma=K(\tilde P,\tilde P)$ a real number and $\vec V=-P_0\overline{\vec P}-\overline{P_0}\vec P$ a real vector, so $\tilde S$ is a real quaternion: $\tilde S^{\natural}=\tilde S$ and $\overline{\tilde S}=\tilde S$. The equation $\tilde P\star\tilde S=0$ is, in the scalar–vector form with $\tilde S$ real,

$$ P_0\sigma=(\vec P,\vec V), \qquad P_0\vec V+\sigma\vec P=\mathbf 0 . $$

If $P_0=0$ then the second display is $\sigma\vec P=\mathbf 0$; if $\vec P=\mathbf 0$ then $\tilde P=0$ and $\tilde S=0$, while if $\vec P\neq\mathbf 0$ then $\sigma=0$, but for $P_0=0$ the diagonal is $\tilde S=K(\tilde P,\tilde P)e_0=-\lVert\vec P\rVert^2e_0$, whose vector part $\vec V$ vanishes and whose scalar $\sigma$ is $-\lVert\vec P\rVert^2$, so $\sigma=0$ forces $\tilde P=0$. Hence there is no solution with $P_0=0$ except $\tilde P=0$.

If $P_0\neq0$, substitute $\vec V=-P_0\overline{\vec P}-\overline{P_0}\vec P$ into the second display: it reads $-P_0^2\overline{\vec P}+\bigl(\sigma-|P_0|^2\bigr)\vec P=\mathbf 0$, that is $P_0^2\overline{\vec P}=\bigl(\sigma-|P_0|^2\bigr)\vec P=-\lVert\vec P\rVert^2\vec P$, the last step because $\sigma=K(\tilde P,\tilde P)=|P_0|^2-\lVert\vec P\rVert^2$. If $\vec P=\mathbf 0$ then $\tilde P=P_0e_0$ is central, the first display reads $P_0|P_0|^2=0$, and $P_0\neq0$ is a contradiction. If $\vec P\neq\mathbf 0$, take the squared modulus of both sides of $P_0^2\overline{\vec P}=-\lVert\vec P\rVert^2\vec P$: it gives $|P_0|^4\lVert\vec P\rVert^2=\lVert\vec P\rVert^6$, hence $|P_0|^2=\lVert\vec P\rVert^2$, so $\sigma=0$ and, from the second display, $P_0\vec V=-\sigma\vec P=\mathbf 0$, that is $\vec V=\mathbf 0$; then $\tilde S=\sigma e_0+\vec V=0$. Hence $\tilde S=0$ in every case.

So $\tilde P\star(\tilde P\star\tilde P)=0$ forces $\tilde P\star\tilde P=0$; the converse is immediate. The right-nested statement holds by conjugate-commutativity, $(\tilde P\star\tilde P)\star\tilde P=\overline{\tilde P\star(\tilde P\star\tilde P)}$, so it vanishes exactly when the left-nested product does.

Corollary. Every nilpotent of the block has index two, and the nonzero nilpotents of the block are exactly its square-zero elements, the cone of §Statement of the Theorem.

Proof. By the theorem a vanishing left-nested cube is already a vanishing square, so the powers stop at two and "some power vanishes" is the same as "the square vanishes".

Corollary (the shape of the set). The nonzero nilpotents are a real cone of real dimension four: the parameter $\alpha$ runs over the punctured plane, of real dimension two, and the direction $\vec n$ over the unit sphere, of real dimension two.

The Nilpotents and the Zero Divisors

Every nonzero nilpotent is a non-pure zero divisor that annihilates itself on both sides, $\tilde\Upsilon\star\tilde\Upsilon=0$, and the nilpotents are strictly smaller than the non-pure family, hence strictly smaller than the zero-divisor set.

Theorem (a nilpotent is a zero divisor). Every nonzero nilpotent of the block is a zero divisor, and it annihilates itself on both sides: $\tilde\Upsilon\star\tilde\Upsilon=0$, so the pair $(\tilde\Upsilon,\tilde\Upsilon)$ exhibits it as a two-sided zero divisor.

Proof. The relation $\tilde\Upsilon\star\tilde\Upsilon=0$ is the product of $\tilde\Upsilon$ with itself on either side; both factors are nonzero by hypothesis. The two-sidedness is Zero Divisors of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra.

Theorem (the nilpotents are non-pure zero divisors). A nilpotent $\tilde\Upsilon=\alpha(e_0+i\vec n)$ has scalar part $\alpha\neq0$ and central square $C(\tilde\Upsilon)=\alpha^2(1-\lVert\vec n\rVert^2)=0$; so it is a zero divisor of the block by the non-pure criterion, and it is not in the pure family.

Proof. $C(\alpha(e_0+i\vec n))=\alpha^2(1+\lVert i\vec n\rVert^2)=\alpha^2(1-\lVert\vec n\rVert^2)=0$ because $\lVert\vec n\rVert=1$.

The inclusion is strict: $C$ vanishes on the whole non-pure family $\{Q_0\neq0,\ C(\tilde Q)=0\}$, and the element

$$ e_0+e_1+i\sqrt2\,e_2 $$

has $Q_0=1$ and $C=1+1-2=0$, so it is a non-pure zero divisor, yet its square is $-2e_0-2e_1\neq0$; equivalently it annihilates the nonzero $e_0-e_1+i\sqrt2\,e_2$ without being square-zero. So the square-zero cone is a proper subset of the non-pure family, and the two must not be identified. The class is thus strictly smaller than the zero-divisor set, whose pure family is the whole vector subspace, of real dimension six, while the nilpotents are a real cone of real dimension four; the difference is the subject of the comparison of Zero Divisors of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra.

The Nilpotents and the Idempotents

No nonzero element of the block is both a nilpotent and an idempotent: the two classes meet only at the origin.

Proof. If $\tilde X\star\tilde X=\tilde X$ and $\tilde X\star\tilde X=0$, then $\tilde X=0$.

The contrast with the plain product is exact: there the nontrivial idempotents are zero divisors and the nilpotents are the pure family, while here the nilpotents are non-pure and the idempotents are regular and lie outside the zero-divisor set altogether (Square Roots of One and the Idempotents of the Symmetric Quaternionic Sesqualgebra).

The Conjugations

The three conjugations preserve the nilpotent cone and permute its parameters, each acting as a bijection of the cone onto itself: the natural conjugation reverses the direction, the star fixes the direction and conjugates the coefficient, and the complex conjugation does both; the star decomposes the cone into its orbits by the real-valued coefficient.

The three conjugations act on the nilpotent cone by permuting its parameters, and the cone is invariant under each of them.

conjugation value on $\tilde\Upsilon=\alpha(e_0+i\vec n)$
natural ${}^{\natural}$ $\alpha(e_0-i\vec n)$, the direction reversed
complex $\bar{\cdot}$ $\overline{\alpha}(e_0-i\vec n)$, the direction reversed and the coefficient conjugated
star ${}^{*}={}^{\natural}\circ\bar{\cdot}$ $\overline{\alpha}(e_0+i\vec n)$, the coefficient conjugated

Every value is again of the form $\alpha'(e_0+i\vec n')$ with $\alpha'\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ and $\vec n'$ a real unit vector, so each conjugation is a bijection of the cone onto itself; the star fixes the direction and conjugates the coefficient, and it fixes a nonzero nilpotent exactly when $\alpha$ is real. The class is a real cone of real dimension four, and it is decomposed into the orbits of the star by the real-valued coefficient.

Worked Examples

The simplest nilpotent. $\tilde\Upsilon=e_0+ie_1$: $\tilde\Upsilon\star\tilde\Upsilon=0$, so the index is two, and the left-nested cube is $\tilde\Upsilon\star(\tilde\Upsilon\star\tilde\Upsilon)=\tilde\Upsilon\star0=0$.

A nilpotent with a complex coefficient. $\tilde\Upsilon=(1+i)(e_0+ie_2)$: by the theorem the square vanishes; the scalar part is $1+i$ and the central square is $0$.

A pure element of square zero inside the vector subspace. The origin alone: a pure element $\vec P\neq0$ has $\tilde P\star\tilde P=-\lVert\vec P\rVert^2e_0\neq0$, so the vector subspace contains no nonzero nilpotent of the block, in contrast with the plain product, whose nilpotent cone lies there.

A pure zero divisor that is not a nilpotent. $\tilde P=e_1$: it is a zero divisor of the block, since $e_1\star e_2=0$, and it is not a nilpotent, since $e_1\star e_1=-e_0$.

Summary

The roots of $0$ in the block are the origin together with the nilpotent cone, the elements $\alpha(e_0+i\vec n)$ with $\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ and $\vec n\in\mathbb{R}^3$ a unit vector, a real cone of real dimension four; for a fixed direction $\vec n$ the generator $e_0+i\vec n$ spans a complex line of square-zero elements. The vector subspace contains no nonzero root of $0$.

The nilpotent of the block has index two and no other: $\tilde P\star(\tilde P\star\tilde P)=0$ holds exactly when $\tilde P\star\tilde P=0$, so the nonzero nilpotents are exactly the nonzero roots of $0$. Every nonzero nilpotent is a non-pure zero divisor that annihilates itself on both sides; the nilpotents are strictly smaller than the non-pure family, whose element $e_0+e_1+i\sqrt2\,e_2$ is a non-pure zero divisor but not square-zero, and strictly smaller than the zero-divisor set, whose pure family is the whole vector subspace. No nonzero nilpotent is an idempotent, the two named classes of the block meeting only at the origin; the nontrivial idempotents are regular and lie outside the zero-divisor set. The three conjugations permute the cone, the star conjugating the coefficient and fixing the direction.
