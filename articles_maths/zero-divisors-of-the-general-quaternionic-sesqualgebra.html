Zero Divisors of the General Quaternionic Sesqualgebra

Introduction

The biquaternion algebra $\mathbb{B} = \mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$ carries, on its underlying complex space, the four general products of The Four General Products of the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space, and this article treats the zero divisors of the fourth, the general quaternionic sesquilinear product

$$ \tilde P \star \tilde Q = \tilde P^{\natural}\tilde Q^{*} , $$

whose rule, multiplication table and axioms are Introduction to the General Quaternionic Sesqualgebra of Biquaternions, and whose two halves, $\mathrm{SQS}$ and $\mathrm{AQS}$, are The Symmetric and Antisymmetric Parts of a Sesqualgebra Product.

A zero divisor is defined by a product, so the set belongs to the product and not to the space. The article states the definition for a general product $\star$ (quoted from Definitions for the Study of the Algebraic Structures), fixes the product of the block, gives the criterion and the two families, describes the set, and then compares the set with the sets of the other three general products: the four sets coincide, and that is a theorem quoted, not a licence to transfer a classification. The finer structure of the GQS set, its square-zero subfamily and its non-pure members, is the point of the article and is owned by Nilpotents of the General Quaternionic Sesqualgebra; the position of the set in the remarkable subspaces is Remarkable Subspaces under the General Quaternionic Sesqualgebra of Biquaternions.

Conventions. The algebra is $\mathbb{B} = \mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$, with basis $e_0 = 1, e_1, e_2, e_3$, $e_1e_2 = e_3$, and central scalar imaginary $i$. An element is $\tilde Q = Q_0 e_0 + \vec Q$ with $Q_0 \in \mathbb{C}$ and $\vec Q = \sum_{k=1}^{3} Q_k e_k$, and

$$ \tilde Q^{\natural} = Q_0 - \vec Q , \qquad \tilde Q^{*} = \overline{\tilde Q^{\natural}} = \overline{Q_0} - \overline{\vec Q} , \qquad \overline{\tilde Q} = \tilde Q^{\natural*} = \overline{Q_0} + \overline{\vec Q} , $$

where the bar on a coefficient is the complex conjugate. The central square is

$$ C(\tilde Q) = \sum_{\mu=0}^{3} Q_\mu^2 , \qquad C(\tilde Q)e_0 = \tilde Q\tilde Q^{\natural} = \tilde Q^{\natural}\tilde Q , $$

and its vanishing is the zero-divisor criterion of the general plain algebra (Zero Divisors of the General Plain Algebra). The product is $\tilde P\star\tilde Q = \tilde P^{\natural}\tilde Q^{*}$, with the natural conjugation ${}^{\natural}$ in the first slot and the star ${}^{*}$ in the second; the juxtaposition $\tilde P\tilde Q$ is the general plain bilinear product, the multiplication of the algebra.

The results. A nonzero element $\tilde Q$ is a zero divisor of the general quaternionic sesquilinear product $\tilde P\star\tilde Q=\tilde P^{\natural}\tilde Q^{*}$ if and only if its central square vanishes, $C(\tilde Q)=\sum_\mu Q_\mu^{2}=0$; the left and the right reading give the same condition, and the four general products share this one set. The set is the complex cone $\{C(\tilde Q)=0\}$ of complex dimension three and real dimension six, the same subset the plain product cuts out. It splits by the scalar part into the pure family $Q_0=0$ with $\sum_k Q_k^{2}=0$ and the non-pure family $Q_0\neq0$ with $\sum_k Q_k^{2}=-Q_0^{2}$, and the square-zero elements, of real dimension four, lie in the non-pure family, so the split by the square and the split by the scalar part cut across each other. Each section below states its result first and then proves it.

The Definition

Definition (zero divisor of a product). Let $\star$ be a product on $\mathbb{B}$. A nonzero element $\tilde Q$ is a left zero divisor of $\star$ when there is a nonzero $\tilde R$ with $\tilde Q\star\tilde R = 0$; a right zero divisor of $\star$ when there is a nonzero $\tilde R$ with $\tilde R\star\tilde Q = 0$; and a zero divisor of $\star$ when it is a left zero divisor or a right zero divisor. The equation and the pair both name the product, so the notion is relative to it; the general definition, with the two-sided reading, is Definitions for the Study of the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space.

Throughout this article the product is the general quaternionic sesquilinear product $\tilde P\star\tilde Q = \tilde P^{\natural}\tilde Q^{*}$, and, unqualified, zero divisor means zero divisor of $\star$. The zero-divisor sets of the other three general products, and the theorem that the four coincide, are Zero Divisors in the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space.

The Criterion

A nonzero element $\tilde Q$ is a zero divisor of $\star$ exactly when its central square vanishes, $C(\tilde Q)=\sum_\mu Q_\mu^{2}=0$, the same equation as for the plain product.

Theorem (the criterion). A nonzero element $\tilde Q$ is a zero divisor of $\star$ if and only if

$$ C(\tilde Q) = \sum_{\mu=0}^{3} Q_\mu^2 = 0 . $$

Proof. The product is $\tilde Q\star\tilde R = \tilde Q^{\natural}\tilde R^{*}$. An element $\tilde R$ runs through $\mathbb{B}$ exactly when $\tilde R^{*}$ does, because ${}^{*}$ is a bijection, so the existence of a nonzero $\tilde R$ with $\tilde Q\star\tilde R = 0$ is the existence of a nonzero $\tilde Y$ with $\tilde Q^{\natural}\tilde Y = 0$, that is, $\tilde Q^{\natural}$ is a left zero divisor of the plain product, with $\tilde Y = \tilde R^{*}$. The zero-divisor criterion of the plain algebra says that this happens exactly when $C(\tilde Q^{\natural}) = 0$, and the natural conjugation keeps the three vector squares and the scalar square, so $C(\tilde Q^{\natural}) = Q_0^2 + \sum_k Q_k^2 = C(\tilde Q)$. The left statement follows; the right statement is the same computation with $\tilde R^{\natural}\tilde Q^{*} = 0$, which for varying $\tilde R$ says that $\tilde Q^{*}$ is a right zero divisor of the plain product; the plain criterion applied to $\tilde Q^{*}$ gives back one condition because $C(\tilde Q^{*}) = \overline{C(\tilde Q)}$ vanishes exactly when $C(\tilde Q)$ does. The two conditions are one, which is the statement.

The criterion is the same equation as for the plain product, and the coincidence is the subject of the comparison below. What the GQS criterion does not inherit is the reading of the criterion: an element of $C = 0$ whose scalar part vanishes is a nilpotent of the plain algebra, while here the square of a zero divisor need not vanish at all.

Example. The element $\tilde Q = e_1 + ie_2$ has $\vec Q = e_1 + ie_2$, so $C(\tilde Q) = 1 + i^2 = 0$ and it is a zero divisor. Its left partner is the element $\tilde R = ie_0 - e_3$, for which

$$ \tilde Q\star\tilde R = (e_1+ie_2)^{\natural}(ie_0-e_3)^{*} = (-e_1-ie_2)(-ie_0+e_3) = 0 , $$

and its square is

$$ \tilde Q\star\tilde Q = (\overline{\tilde Q}\tilde Q)^{\natural} = (-2e_0+2ie_3)^{\natural} = -2e_0-2ie_3 \neq 0 . $$

So $\tilde Q$ is a zero divisor whose left multiplication is not square-zero, in contrast to a nonzero nilpotent, which annihilates itself (Nilpotents of the General Quaternionic Sesqualgebra).

The Two Families

The zero-divisor set splits by the scalar part into the disjoint pure family $Q_0=0$ with $\sum_k Q_k^{2}=0$ and non-pure family $Q_0\neq0$ with $\sum_k Q_k^{2}=-Q_0^{2}\neq0$, whose union with the origin is the whole set, and the square-zero elements lie in the non-pure family, so the split by the square cuts across the split by the scalar part.

The zero-divisor set splits by the scalar part, as it does for the plain product, and the split is named.

Definition (the two families). A zero divisor $\tilde Q$ of $\star$ is pure when $Q_0 = 0$ and non-pure when $Q_0 \neq 0$. The set is the disjoint union of the two families and the origin.

Proposition (the pure family). A pure zero divisor $\tilde Q = \vec Q$ satisfies

$$ \sum_{k=1}^{3} Q_k^2 = 0 , $$

and conversely every nonzero pure element with $\sum_k Q_k^2 = 0$ is a zero divisor.

Proof. Purity is $Q_0 = 0$, and the criterion $C(\tilde Q) = Q_0^2 + \sum_k Q_k^2 = 0$ is then the displayed relation. The converse is the criterion read backwards.

Proposition (the non-pure family). A non-pure zero divisor satisfies

$$ \sum_{k=1}^{3} Q_k^2 = -Q_0^2 \neq 0 , $$

and the family is non-empty; every element of the square-zero family of Nilpotents of the General Quaternionic Sesqualgebra, in particular $e_0 + ie_1$, is non-pure.

Proof. The relation is the criterion with $Q_0 \neq 0$. The element $e_0 + ie_1$ has $Q_0 = 1$, $Q_1 = i$, $Q_2 = Q_3 = 0$, so $C = 1 - 1 = 0$, and it is a zero divisor with nonzero scalar part. Its square vanishes, as the cited article records, so the non-pure family is not the complement of the square-zero family.

This is the point at which the GQS set departs from the plain one. In the plain algebra the pure family is exactly the nonzero nilpotents and the non-pure family is the part of the cone with non-vanishing scalar part; here the square-zero elements are non-pure, so the two splittings, the one by the scalar part and the one by the vanishing of the square, cut across each other. The family split is a split of the cone, and the square-zero split is a different split of the same cone.

The Set

The zero-divisor set is the punctured complex cone $\{\tilde Q\neq0:C(\tilde Q)=0\}$, of complex dimension three and real dimension six, the zero set of the non-degenerate complex quadratic form $C$ on $\mathbb{C}^4$, containing the pure vector-subspace part of complex dimension two and the non-pure part of complex dimension three.

Theorem (the set). The zero-divisor set of $\star$ is

$$ \{\tilde Q \neq 0 : C(\tilde Q) = 0\} = \{\tilde Q \neq 0 : Q_0^2 + Q_1^2 + Q_2^2 + Q_3^2 = 0\} , $$

a complex cone of complex dimension three and real dimension six, the zero set of the non-degenerate complex quadratic form $C$ on $\mathbb{C}^4$. Its vertex is the origin, which is not a zero divisor, and it contains the whole vector subspace part $\{Q_0 = 0, \sum_k Q_k^2 = 0\}$ of complex dimension two together with the non-pure part $Q_0 \neq 0$ of complex dimension three.

Proof. The description is the criterion. The form $C$ is non-degenerate and its zero set is the affine cone over the non-singular quadric $C = 0$ in $\mathbb{P}^3$, of complex dimension two, so the affine cone has complex dimension three and real dimension six. The vector-subspace part is the cone over a conic in $\mathbb{P}^2$, of complex dimension two, and for $Q_0 \neq 0$ one solves $\sum_k Q_k^2 = -Q_0^2$, a quadric in three variables, so the non-pure part has complex dimension three.

The dimension is the same as for the plain product, because the criterion is the same form; the two cones are the same subset of $\mathbb{B}$.

The Coincidence with the Other Three Products

The four general products have the same zero-divisor set $\{\tilde Q\neq0:C(\tilde Q)=0\}$, a quoted theorem that holds for the left and the right reading separately; the predicate coincides but the square does not, the square-zero set of $\star$ being of real dimension four while that of the plain product is the pure isotropic cone.

Theorem (the four products have one zero-divisor set, quoted). The four general products of The Four General Products of the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space have the same zero-divisor set, namely $\{\tilde Q \neq 0 : C(\tilde Q) = 0\}$. The theorem is Zero Divisors in the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space, and it holds for the left and the right reading separately.

Proof. The four products are $\theta(\tilde Q)\phi(\tilde R)$ with $\theta \in \{\mathrm{id}, {}^{\natural}\}$ and $\phi \in \{\mathrm{id}, {}^{*}\}$, and both $\theta$ and $\phi$ are bijections preserving the vanishing of the central square, so the plain criterion transfers to each of the four. The general-argument form is in the cited article.

The coincidence is the reason the criterion of §The Criterion needed no new computation and the reason the set of §The Set is the set of the block: the four products separate the elements only through the product, and the zero-divisor predicate is blind to the two slot involutions because it is the vanishing of the central square, which the involutions preserve. It is not a licence to transfer the classification silently, and the difference appears as soon as the square is taken: the square-zero set of the plain product is the pure isotropic cone, of real dimension four, while the square-zero set of $\star$ is a different real four-dimensional family, that of Nilpotents of the General Quaternionic Sesqualgebra. The predicate coincides, the square does not.

The Zero Divisors and the Square-Zero Elements

Every nonzero element with $\tilde Q\star\tilde Q=0$ is a zero divisor of $\star$, and the square-zero elements form a proper subfamily of the zero-divisor set, of real dimension four, lying in the non-pure family; in the plain algebra the square-zero elements are instead the pure zero divisors.

The relation between the two families of this article and the nilpotents of the block is the one place where the GQS zero-divisor theory says something the plain theory does not.

Theorem (the square-zero elements are zero divisors). Every nonzero element with $\tilde Q\star\tilde Q = 0$ is a zero divisor of $\star$, and the square-zero elements form a proper subfamily of the zero-divisor set, of real dimension four, lying in the non-pure family.

Proof. If $\tilde Q\star\tilde Q = 0$ then $\tilde Q$ is a zero divisor with $\tilde R = \tilde Q$. The identification with the family $c(\hat u + i)$, its dimension and its non-purity are Square Roots of Zero and the Nilpotents of the General Quaternionic Sesqualgebra.

Corollary (the contrast with the plain product). In the plain algebra the square-zero elements are the pure zero divisors and the non-pure zero divisors have non-vanishing square; in the GQS the square-zero elements are non-pure and a pure zero divisor, such as $e_1 + ie_2$, has nonzero square.

Proof. The plain statement is Nilpotents of the General Plain Algebra; the GQS statement is the example of §The Criterion and the previous theorem.

So the two products agree on which elements are zero divisors and disagree on how a zero divisor squares. That is the sharpest form of the zero-divisor clarification for the block: the definition belongs to the product, the criterion happens to coincide, and the element theory built on the square does not.

Summary

The zero divisors of the general quaternionic sesquilinear product $\tilde P\star\tilde Q = \tilde P^{\natural}\tilde Q^{*}$ are defined relative to the product, and they are the nonzero elements with $C(\tilde Q) = \sum_\mu Q_\mu^2 = 0$, equivalently $\tilde Q\tilde Q^{\natural} = 0$; the left and the right reading give the same condition. The set is the complex cone $C = 0$ of complex dimension three and real dimension six, the same subset of the space that the plain product, the natural product and the general plain sesquilinear product cut out, a coincidence that is a theorem of Zero Divisors in the Algebraic Structures over the Biquaternion $\mathbb{C}$ Space because the criterion is the vanishing of the central square and the four products differ only by the two slot involutions.

The set splits by the scalar part into the pure family $Q_0 = 0$, with $\sum_k Q_k^2 = 0$, and the non-pure family $Q_0 \neq 0$, with $\sum_k Q_k^2 = -Q_0^2$; the square-zero elements, which are a real four-dimensional proper subfamily, lie in the non-pure family, so the split by the square and the split by the scalar part cut across each other and the analogy with the plain product stops at the criterion. The element $e_1 + ie_2$ is a pure zero divisor whose square is $-2e_0 - 2ie_3$, and the element $e_0 + ie_1$ is a non-pure zero divisor whose square vanishes.
