## Kravchenko chiral paper re-supplied as the arXiv preprint — the kernel sign was wrong

**The new PDF was a duplicate.** `Vladislav V. Kravchenko - On a quaternionic Maxwell equation for the
time-dependent electromagnetic field in a chiral medium.pdf` (md5 `841a9d84a84bf5b340dd3dc373867f37`) is
arXiv:math-ph/0309062v1, the *preprint* of Grudsky–Khmelnytskaya–Kravchenko, the paper already recorded in
the section *A Chiral Medium: One Quaternionic Equation* of `electromagnetism-in-media-the-local-complex-
structure-at-work.md`. The journal version is `material/traités/Vladislav V. Kravchenko - On a quaternionic
form of the Maxwell equations for the time-dependent electromagnetic field in c.pdf`
(md5 `f01c76f1659c4fbc6358a4416e66f7fc`), *J. Phys. A* **37** (2004) 4641–4647. Three files, two versions,
one work. Both were re-extracted and read side by side.

**A real error found and fixed: the sign of `Theta_alpha`.** The article had
`Theta_alpha = +e^{i alpha |x|}/(4 pi |x|)`; the source prints the **minus** in both versions, in the sentence
immediately before "`D_alpha K_alpha = delta`". The earlier pass had *noticed* the minus and set it aside as a
layout artefact, having checked identity (11) — which is empty reasoning, because (11) factors `Theta_alpha`
out of `K_alpha` and so reads identically with either sign. The test that discriminates is the kernel's
defining property, and it needs a flux integral, not a pointwise check:

    for a ball around the origin,  int_{|x|=r} n K_alpha dS -> +1.0000  for the source's minus
                                                            -> -1.0000  for the plus

So `D_alpha K_alpha = +delta` only with the minus. It is confirmed independently at `alpha = 0`: the minus
gives `K_0 = -x/(4 pi r^3)`, the Cauchy kernel of `D`, which is what the article's own inhomogeneous-media
section and the dedicated chiral article both give.

**Two content items added.** (i) The source's defining equation `D_alpha K_alpha = delta`, absent until now.
(ii) The residue series **resum to Bessel functions**: `I_1 = i H(t) e^{i a_y t} J_0(2 sqrt(ct))` and
`I_2 = -H(t) sqrt(t/c) e^{i a_y t} J_1(2 sqrt(ct))`, with `c(x) = |x|/(beta^2 sqrt(eps mu))`; verified to
6.1e-13 and 5.1e-13 over 200 random values of `ct`. The previous pass had read the outcome as "not a finite
combination of the customary kernels" and implied the expansion was *asymptotic* — the source does not say
that, it justifies the interchange by uniform convergence, so the series is exact. A false caveat in the
`.context` claiming "the body says so" was withdrawn in place (the word "asymptotic" never appeared in the
article).

**Not recorded, deliberately.** The source's `A(x), B(x), c(x), E(x)` of the "more convenient form" of the
frequency-domain kernel. `B(x)` as printed carries three terms of different length dimension, and the
"convenient form" attaches the essential singularity to one term only, whereas an independent derivation puts
it on the whole bracket (with `E(x)` multiplying both terms). The article records the structure and the Bessel
result, not those normalisations.

**A biquaternion-arithmetic trap worth remembering.** Representing a biquaternion as `(scalar, (x,y,z))` of
Python complex numbers and using `1j` for **both** the scalar imaginary **and** the quaternion units `e_k`
silently conflates them: `(1j)(1j) = -1` folds the scalar imaginary into the units, so a left multiplication
by `e_k` returns `i * grad` instead of `grad`. It is invisible for probe functions that are real with real
coefficients, which is why the `alpha = 0` checks, `D^2 = -Delta` and `D[x/(4 pi r^3)] = 0` all passed while
the sign was wrong. Track `e_k` by position and use `e1 d = (-v1, (s, -v3, v2))` plus cyclic permutations.
The flux test is immune because the normal `n` is real.
