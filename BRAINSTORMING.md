In geometric algebra, the primitive objects are **vectors** and the **geometric product**. Spinors are not put in by hand; they emerge from that structure. But once they emerge, they are absolutely central to how rotations work.

### Where spinors actually live in GA

A spinor is typically an **even multivector** (or an element of a minimal left ideal). A **rotor** is a unit spinor/versor, and it is built from an even number of vectors:

\[
R = v_1 v_2 \cdots v_{2k}.
\]

So spinors are made of vectors. The algebra is generated by vectors. That is why vectors, not spinors, are the foundation.

### Why the half-angle appears

The rotation group \(\mathrm{SO}(n)\) is doubly covered by the spin group \(\mathrm{Spin}(n)\). The rotor \(R\) is a kind of square root of the rotation. That double cover is exactly why you see two different actions:

- **Vector action** (sandwich):  
  \[
  v \mapsto R v R^{-1}
  \]
  This rotates a vector by the full angle \(\theta\).

- **Spinor action** (left multiplication):  
  \[
  \psi \mapsto R \psi
  \]
  This rotates a spinor by the half-angle \(\theta/2\).

So \(A \mapsto RA\) is not “not enough” in general. It is the correct action when \(A\) is a spinor. But it is the wrong action when \(A\) is a vector, because left multiplication by an even element does not generally keep a vector a vector. In 3D, for example, \(R e_3\) can produce a vector plus a trivector. The sandwich \(R e_3 R^{-1}\) cancels the unwanted grade and gives back a pure vector.

### So are spinors at the heart of GA?

They are at the heart of **rotations** and **spin representations**, but they are not the primitive starting point. The slogan is:

> **Vectors and the geometric product are the foundation. Rotors and spinors are derived objects that implement rotations.**

This is actually one of GA’s strengths: it explains why spinors exist and why they rotate by half the angle. They are not mysterious extra objects; they are even multivectors built from vectors.

If you mean “are spinors central to the physical content of GA?” — yes, especially in quantum mechanics and relativity. If you mean “are spinors the primitive objects from which GA is built?” — no, vectors and the geometric product are.

# Biquaternions ≅ M₂(ℂ): The Structural Fact

## What B is

B is the **biquaternion algebra**:

    B = ℂ ⊗_ℝ ℍ

i.e. quaternions a + bi + cj + dk but with a, b, c, d ∈ ℂ (complex
coefficients). Equivalently, it's the algebra spanned over ℂ by
1, i, j, k with

    i² = j² = k² = ijk = -1.

It's an **8-real-dimensional** (or **4-complex-dimensional**) algebra.

## What M₂(ℂ) is

The algebra of 2×2 **complex matrices** under ordinary matrix addition
and multiplication. Also 8-real-dimensional (4 complex entries). This
is the standard algebra of quantum operators on a 2-level system
(a qubit / spin-½).

## The isomorphism

The claim is:

    B ≅ M₂(ℂ)

as **algebras** (same addition, multiplication, and ℂ-linear structure).
This is a real, concrete, well-known fact — biquaternions are just
2×2 complex matrices in disguise.

## The explicit map

Send the quaternion units to -i times the **Pauli matrices**:

    e₁ = i ↦ -i σ₁,    e₂ = j ↦ -i σ₂,    e₃ = k ↦ -i σ₃,

where

    σ₁ = ⎛ 0  1 ⎞    σ₂ = ⎛ 0  -i ⎞    σ₃ = ⎛ 1   0 ⎞
         ⎝ 1  0 ⎠         ⎝ i   0 ⎠         ⎝ 0  -1 ⎠

So explicitly:

    i ↦ -i σ₁ = ⎛ 0  -i ⎞
                ⎝ -i  0 ⎠

    j ↦ -i σ₂ = ⎛ 0  -1 ⎞
                ⎝ 1   0 ⎠

    k ↦ -i σ₃ = ⎛ -i  0 ⎞
                ⎝  0  i ⎠

And 1 ↦ I = ⎛ 1  0 ⎞
           ⎝ 0  1 ⎠

## Why the -i factor?

This is the key detail. The Pauli matrices satisfy

    σ₁² = σ₂² = σ₃² = I,   σ₁σ₂ = iσ₃,  etc. (cyclic)

Notice: σ_k² = +I, but quaternion units satisfy e_k² = -1.

The factor -i **fixes the sign**. Check:

    (-i σ₁)² = (-i)² σ₁² = (-1)(I) = -I.   ✓

And the cross-relations:

    (-i σ₁)(-i σ₂) = (-i)² σ₁σ₂ = (-1)(iσ₃) = -i σ₃ = image of k.   ✓

So the -i is exactly what's needed to make the Pauli matrices **obey
the quaternion relations** rather than the Pauli relations. Without it,
you'd get a Clifford algebra with +1 squares instead of ℍ.

## Why this is "the single structural fact"

The isomorphism B ≅ M₂(ℂ) is the **Rosetta Stone** connecting several
languages:

    | Language              | Object                                    |
    |-----------------------+-------------------------------------------|
    | Quaternions           | i, j, k with i²=j²=k²=-1                  |
    | Quantum mechanics     | Pauli matrices σ₁,σ₂,σ₃ (spin-½ operators)|
    | Linear algebra        | 2×2 complex matrices                      |
    | Lie theory            | 𝔰𝔲(2) and its complexification 𝔰𝔩₂(ℂ)    |
    | Spinors               | vectors in ℂ² on which these act          |
    | Relativity / Dirac    | γ-matrices, Clifford algebra              |

Once you see B ≅ M₂(ℂ), you realize:

1. **Quaternion rotations and spin-½ quantum mechanics are the same
   math.** The unit quaternions S³ ≅ SU(2) are literally the spin-½
   rotation group.

2. **Spinors are ℂ² vectors**, and the quaternion units act on them
   as -i σ_k.

3. **The double cover SU(2) → SO(3)** is now explicit:
   SU(2) = { U ∈ M₂(ℂ) : UU† = I, det U = 1 }, and it maps 2-to-1
   onto rotations.

4. **Complexification is the bridge.** Real quaternions ℍ don't quite
   give M₂(ℂ); you need to complexify (allow complex coefficients) to
   get the full matrix algebra. That complexification is what turns
   rotations into spinors.

# Norm Form = Determinant, Unit Norm = SL(2,ℂ), and the Lorentz Double Cover

This passage ties together four ideas: the **norm** on biquaternions,
the **determinant** of the corresponding 2×2 matrix, the group
**SL(2,ℂ)**, and the **Lorentz group** SO⁺(1,3). Let me unpack it.

## 1. The norm form on B

A biquaternion is

    Q̃ = a + b i + c j + d k,    a,b,c,d ∈ ℂ

or in the 2×2 matrix picture (via Φ: B → M₂(ℂ)):

    Φ(Q̃) = ⎛ a + id   -c + ib ⎞
           ⎝ c + ib    a - id  ⎠

(up to conventions). The **norm form** (also called the reduced norm
or spinor norm) is

    N(Q̃) = a² + b² + c² + d²     (complex-valued!)

i.e. the "sum of squares" of the four complex coefficients — the
natural quadratic form on B.

## 2. Norm = determinant

Under the isomorphism Φ: B ≅ M₂(ℂ), the norm becomes the ordinary
**matrix determinant**:

    N(Q̃) = det Φ(Q̃).

Let's verify with the matrix above:

    det Φ(Q̃) = (a + id)(a - id) - (-c + ib)(c + ib)
             = a² + d² - (-c² - b²)
             = a² + b² + c² + d²
             = N(Q̃).   ✓

**Why this matters:** The determinant is multiplicative,

    det(AB) = det(A) det(B),

so the norm is multiplicative too:

    N(Q̃₁ Q̃₂) = N(Q̃₁) N(Q̃₂).

This is the key algebraic fact that makes unit-norm elements form
a group.

## 3. The unit-norm condition

The passage defines

    SL(2,ℂ) = { Λ̃ ∈ B : Λ̃ Λ̃̄ = e₀ }

where:

- **Λ̃̄** is the **biquaternion conjugate** (the analogue of complex
  conjugation, flipping the sign of i, j, k — and possibly also
  conjugating complex coefficients, depending on convention).
- **e₀ = 1** is the identity.
- The condition Λ̃ Λ̃̄ = 1 is the **unit-norm condition** N(Λ̃) = 1
  written in multiplicative form.

Under Φ, this becomes

    Φ(Λ̃) Φ(Λ̃)† = I,   det Φ(Λ̃) = 1

which is exactly the defining condition of **SL(2,ℂ)** — the group of
2×2 complex matrices with determinant 1.

So the identification is:

    { unit-norm biquaternions }  ≅  SL(2,ℂ).

**Dimension check:** B is 8-real-dimensional. The condition
Λ̃ Λ̃̄ = 1 imposes 4 real constraints (since it's a complex equation
with 4 complex components). So the unit-norm set is 8 - 4 = 4-real-

(3 complex parameters × 2), and the unit-norm subgroup is the
**compact-like** part... wait, let me be careful.

Let me correct the dimension count: B is 8-real-dimensional. The

The condition Λ̃ Λ̃̄ = e₀ is **stronger** than det = 1 in general —

    | Condition          | Group        | Real dim |
    |--------------------+--------------+----------|

    | Λ̃ Λ̃̄ = 1           | SU(2)        | 3        |

So there's a subtlety in the passage's notation. If Λ̃̄ means the

quaternion and complex conjugation), then Λ̃ Λ̃̄ = 1 gives a
**non-compact** group — and the passage calls it SL(2,ℂ). The
resolution is that for biquaternions, the "conjugate" used here is
the **quaternion conjugate only** (not complex conjugation), so
Λ̃ Λ̃̄ = 1 becomes det = 1, giving SL(2,ℂ). This is a convention-

## 4. The double cover of the Lorentz group

The punchline:

    SL(2,ℂ)  →  SO⁺(1,3)

is a **2-to-1 covering map**. This is the relativistic analogue of

    SU(2)  →  SO(3)

from the non-relativistic case.

- **SO⁺(1,3)** = proper (det = +1) orthochronous (time-preserving)
  Lorentz group — the physically relevant component.
- **SL(2,ℂ)** = its double cover, the **spin group** Spin(1,3).

The kernel of the covering is {±I}, exactly as before. So:

    SO⁺(1,3) ≅ SL(2,ℂ) / {±I}.

## 5. The action: rotor conjugation

The passage says SL(2,ℂ) acts on **M⁻** (a space of Hermitian
biquaternions / spacetime vectors) by **rotor conjugation**:

    X̃  ↦  Λ̃ X̃ Λ̃†

where:

- **X̃** is a Hermitian biquaternion representing a spacetime point
  or vector (the "M⁻" space — likely the space of 2×2 Hermitian
  matrices, which is 4-real-dimensional and models Minkowski space).
- **Λ̃†** is the **Hermitian conjugate** (transpose + conjugate),
  which for unit-norm Λ̃ equals Λ̃̄ (the conjugate).

The map X̃ ↦ Λ̃ X̃ Λ̃†:

- Preserves the **Hermiticity** of X̃ (so it stays in M⁻).
- Preserves the **determinant** det X̃ (since det(Λ̃ X̃ Λ̃†) =
  det(Λ̃) det(X̃) det(Λ̃†) = |det Λ̃|² det X̃ = det X̃ when
  det Λ̃ = 1).
- The determinant of a Hermitian biquaternion is the **Minkowski
  norm** x₀² - x₁² - x₂² - x₃².

So the action preserves the Minkowski metric — it's a **Lorentz
transformation**. And since det Λ̃ = 1 and the map is continuous,
it lands in SO⁺(1,3).

The chain of logic:

1. B ≅ M₂(ℂ), so norm = determinant.
2. Unit norm ⟹ det = 1 ⟹ SL(2,ℂ).
3. SL(2,ℂ) acts on Hermitian matrices (spacetime) by conjugation.
4. This action preserves the Minkowski metric.
5. The map SL(2,ℂ) → SO⁺(1,3) is 2-to-1 (kernel {±I}).
6. Therefore SL(2,ℂ) is the double cover of the Lorentz group.

## 7. Why this is the relativistic analogue of spin

In the non-relativistic case:

    SU(2)  →  SO(3),   kernel {±1},   spinors = ℂ².

In the relativistic case:

    SL(2,ℂ)  →  SO⁺(1,3),   kernel {±I},   spinors = ℂ² (Weyl).

The biquaternion framework makes both cases **the same construction**:
unit-norm elements of B act on Hermitian elements of B by conjugation,
preserving a quadratic form (the determinant = norm), and the
resulting map to the orthogonal group of that form is 2-to-1.

This is why:

- **Weyl spinors** (left/right-handed) are the two ℂ² representations
  of SL(2,ℂ).
- **Dirac spinors** are ℂ⁴ = ℂ² ⊕ ℂ², combining both chiralities.
- **Lorentz transformations** on spinors are exactly the SL(2,ℂ)
  action Λ̃ on ℂ².
- **The sign ambiguity** (Λ̃ vs -Λ̃) is the relativistic spin sign —
  a spinor picks up -1 under a 2π rotation, and the double cover
  remembers the difference.
