Why These Matrices

Six reasons.

  1. The matrices are the operators. Each @@M658@@ is @@M659@@. A table entry written as @@M660@@ says which left and right multiplications produce it. A table entry written as @@M661@@ says only which entry of a matrix is nonzero.
  2. The product is closed up to sign. The thirty-two matrices @@M662@@ form a group. Products, inverses and powers stay in a finite set. In the matrix-unit basis the product of two basis elements is either zero or a third, but the coefficients of a general product spread over sixteen terms.
  3. The coefficients are traces. Orthogonality gives @@M663@@ with norm @@M664@@. There is no linear system to solve.
  4. The coefficient rank is the length. The least number of products is the rank of a @@M665@@ matrix. This is the natural measure of complexity of an operator, and it is basis-independent.
  5. The physics matrices are sparse in this basis. The chirality projectors, the mass bilinear and the Lorentz generators are linear combinations of a few basis operators with coefficients in @@M666@@.
  6. The matrix units are the wrong default. Every @@M667@@ has length four. A table in matrix units overstates the number of products by a factor of four in every entry.

The caveat. The matrix units remain the canonical basis of the abstract algebra: they are the definition of the product, and the theorems of the category are stated in them. The eigenbasis remains canonical for spectral theory. The Peirce basis remains canonical for the chiral blocks. The sixteen matrices are the default for the operator question, not a replacement for the others.

A test. Take a matrix from a physics table. If it is a projector, a field strength or a mass term, it has a short expression in the method's basis. Compute the sixteen traces and the rank. If the rank is small, the matrix is a short product, and the table is hiding a product structure. If the rank is four, the matrix is a genuinely complicated operator.

Summary

The algebra @@M687@@ is built by the two-sided operators method, which produces the sixteen matrices @@M688@@, all signed permutations of determinant one. They are orthogonal for the Hilbert–Schmidt form with @@M689@@, so the coefficients of a matrix are sixteen traces. As words @@M690@@ they are the identity, the six @@M691@@ and @@M692@@ and the nine @@M693@@; only @@M694@@ carries the trace, so the deletion of that one word gives the fifteen, in the ratio @@M695@@. They are closed under multiplication up to sign, and the thirty-two matrices @@M696@@ form a group of order @@M697@@, isomorphic to @@M698@@. The product rule is read from two @@M699@@ tables, the left quaternion table and the right opposite table. Every matrix unit is a sum of four basis operators with coefficients @@M700@@, so every matrix unit has length four; the matrix-unit basis is the worst basis for the product. In the Peirce basis the sixteen products @@M701@@ are exactly the sixteen matrix units with coefficient @@M702@@. The coefficient matrix of a product is an outer product, and the rank of the coefficient matrix is the length, the least number of two-sided operators. The Clifford frame is five of the sixteen matrices up to the scalar @@M703@@. The sixteen matrices are the default for matrices that come from operators; they do not replace the matrix units for the abstract algebra, the eigenbasis for spectral theory or the Peirce basis for the chiral blocks.
